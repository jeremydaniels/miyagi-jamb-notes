## General characteristics of the plant kingdom

Members of the kingdom Plantae:

1. Are **eukaryotic** and **multicellular** (though the simplest, like *Spirogyra*, are just chains of similar cells).
2. Have cell walls made of **cellulose**.
3. Contain **chlorophyll** in **chloroplasts** and make their own food by **photosynthesis** (autotrophic).
4. Store food mainly as **starch**.
5. Are usually fixed in one place, and grow throughout life at their tips.
6. Show **alternation of generations** in their life cycles (explained below).

## Alternation of generations: the key idea

Every plant life cycle has two generations, or stages, that follow each other:

| Generation | Chromosomes | What it produces | How it produces it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Gametophyte** ("gamete plant") | One set of chromosomes (**haploid**, n) | Gametes (sperm and eggs) | By mitosis, in sex organs: **antheridia** (male) and **archegonia** (female) |
| **Sporophyte** ("spore plant") | Two sets of chromosomes (**diploid**, 2n) | Spores | By meiosis, in **sporangia** |

The cycle goes:

1. The **sporophyte** (2n) produces spores by meiosis.
2. Each **spore** (n) grows into a **gametophyte** (n).
3. The gametophyte produces **gametes** (n).
4. A male gamete fertilises a female gamete, forming a **zygote** (2n).
5. The zygote grows into a new **sporophyte** (2n), and the cycle repeats.

The main story of plant evolution is the change in which generation is the large, long-lived, independent plant:

- In **mosses and liverworts**, the **gametophyte** is the main plant; the sporophyte is small and grows on it.
- In **ferns**, the **sporophyte** is the main plant; the gametophyte is a tiny, separate, short-lived prothallus.
- In **seed plants**, the **sporophyte** is the whole plant you see; the gametophytes are reduced to a few cells hidden inside the pollen grain and the ovule.

**Textbook variation:** some simpler textbooks describe *Spirogyra*'s life cycle without using the terms gametophyte and sporophyte, because the only diploid stage in *Spirogyra* is the zygospore. The full alternation of generations is usually taught from the bryophytes onwards.

## Thallophyta: *Spirogyra*

**Thallophyta** are the simplest plants. Their body is a **thallus**: it is not divided into true roots, stems and leaves, and it has no vascular (conducting) tissue. Most live in water.

**Textbook variation:** in older classifications, Thallophyta included algae, fungi and bacteria. In the five-kingdom system used by JAMB, fungi and bacteria are in separate kingdoms, so "Thallophyta" here means the plant-like **algae**, such as *Spirogyra*.

### External features and structure of *Spirogyra*

*Spirogyra* is a green alga that grows as fine, slimy, bright-green threads in clean, still or slow-moving fresh water, such as ponds, streams and rice fields. It is sometimes called "pond silk" or "water silk" because of its silky feel.

| Feature | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filament | Long, unbranched thread of identical cylindrical cells joined end to end | Each cell can carry out all life processes |
| Mucilage sheath | Slimy layer around the filament | Makes it slippery; protects it from being eaten and from drying out |
| Cell wall | Made of cellulose (inner) with pectin (outer) | Support and protection |
| Chloroplasts | One or more **spiral, ribbon-shaped** chloroplasts in each cell, running along the inside of the wall | Photosynthesis. The spiral shape gives *Spirogyra* its name |
| Pyrenoids | Small round bodies along the chloroplast | Store starch |
| Vacuole | Large central vacuole | Keeps the cell firm |
| Nucleus | Suspended in the middle of the vacuole by fine strands of cytoplasm | Controls the cell |

Flat textbook style, soft greens, white background, no text.]

### Life history of *Spirogyra*

**1. Asexual reproduction by fragmentation:** the filament breaks into short pieces, for example when the water is disturbed. Each piece grows by cell division into a new long filament.

**2. Sexual reproduction by conjugation** (usually towards the end of the growing season, or when conditions become unfavourable). The most common type is **scalariform** (ladder-like) conjugation:

1. Two filaments come to lie side by side.
2. Opposite cells of the two filaments grow small bumps towards each other. The bumps meet, and the walls between them dissolve, forming **conjugation tubes**. The joined filaments look like a ladder.
3. The contents of each cell shrink and round up to form a **gamete**. The gametes look the same, but they behave differently.
4. The gametes of one filament (the "male" or donor filament) creep through the conjugation tubes into the cells of the other filament (the "female" or receiving filament).
5. Each pair of gametes fuses to form a **zygote** (fertilisation).
6. Each zygote forms a thick, dark wall and becomes a **zygospore**. The male filament is left as a row of empty cells.
7. The zygospores sink to the bottom of the pond when the filament decays, and survive the dry season or cold.
8. When conditions improve, the zygospore nucleus divides by **meiosis** into four nuclei. Three break down, and the remaining one is the nucleus of a new cell.
9. The zygospore wall bursts, and the cell grows out and divides to form a new filament.

Less often, two neighbouring cells in the **same** filament join through a tube beside their shared wall (**lateral conjugation**).

[IMAGE: Two Spirogyra filaments lying side by side joined by short conjugation tubes like the rungs of a ladder (scalariform conjugation). In the upper filament the cell contents are rounded and moving through the tubes; in the lower filament several cells each contain a dark, oval thick-walled zygospore. Flat textbook style, soft greens with dark brown zygospores, white background, no text.]

### Why *Spirogyra* is a simple plant

- No roots, stems or leaves; no vascular tissue.
- All cells are the same; there is no division of labour.
- Fully dependent on water: it lives in water, takes in water and minerals over its whole surface, and its gametes meet in water-filled tubes.

## Bryophyta: mosses and liverworts

**Bryophytes** are small, green, non-flowering plants that grow in damp, shady places, such as on wet walls, rocks, tree trunks and the ground near streams, especially in the rainy season. They are the simplest true **land plants**, but they still need water for fertilisation. For this reason they are often called the **"amphibians of the plant kingdom"**.

**General characteristics of bryophytes:**

1. The main plant is the **gametophyte** (haploid).
2. They have no true roots; they are anchored by thread-like **rhizoids**, which also absorb some water.
3. They have **no vascular tissue** (no xylem or phloem). Water moves slowly from cell to cell, so they stay small.
4. Their leaves (in mosses) are very thin, often one cell thick, with little or no waxy cuticle, so they dry out easily.
5. Male sex organs are **antheridia**; they produce **sperm** (antherozoids) with two flagella, which must **swim** through a film of water to reach the egg.
6. Female sex organs are flask-shaped **archegonia**, each containing one **egg** (ovum).
7. The **sporophyte** grows out of the gametophyte and depends on it for water and some food. It produces **spores** in a **capsule**.

### Moss (e.g. *Brachymenium*)

*Brachymenium* is a genus of small mosses that grows in tropical regions, including West Africa, on soil, rocks, walls and tree bark. Many textbooks describe the moss life cycle using the closely related common cord moss, *Funaria*; the structure and life cycle are the same in outline.

#### External features of a moss plant

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rhizoids | Brown, multicellular, branched threads at the base | Anchor the plant; absorb some water and minerals |
| "Stem" (axis) | Short, upright, slender | Holds up the leaves |
| "Leaves" | Small, thin, green, spirally arranged around the stem; often with a midrib | Photosynthesis; absorb water directly over their surface |
| Antheridia | Club-shaped male organs, in a cluster at the tip of a male shoot | Produce sperm |
| Archegonia | Flask-shaped female organs at the tip of a female shoot | Each holds one egg in its swollen base (venter) |
| Sporophyte | Grows up from the tip of the female shoot after fertilisation | Produces spores |

The **sporophyte** has three parts:

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foot | Buried in the tip of the gametophyte | Absorbs water and food from the gametophyte |
| Seta (stalk) | Long, thin stalk | Raises the capsule high, so spores are carried further by wind |
| Capsule | Swollen spore case at the top, covered at first by a loose cap (**calyptra**) and closed by a lid (**operculum**) | Spores form inside by meiosis. When the lid falls off, a ring of **peristome teeth** opens in dry weather and closes in wet weather, releasing spores only when it is dry and windy |

[IMAGE: A moss plant: a small leafy green shoot (gametophyte) with brown rhizoids at the base, and a long thin brown stalk (seta) growing from its tip, ending in a capsule with a pointed cap (calyptra); next to it, an enlarged capsule with the lid removed showing a ring of peristome teeth. Flat textbook style, soft greens and browns, white background, no text.]

#### Life history of a moss

1. **Spore germination:** a spore lands on damp soil and germinates into a green, branched thread, called the **protonema**, which looks like a filamentous alga.
2. **Leafy shoots:** buds form on the protonema and grow into upright, leafy shoots with rhizoids. These are the **gametophytes**.
3. **Sex organs:** at the tips of the shoots, **antheridia** and **archegonia** develop (in some mosses on the same plant, in others on separate male and female plants).
4. **Fertilisation:** after rain, or when there is dew, the antheridia burst and release **sperm**. The sperm swim through the film of water on the plant, attracted by chemicals from the archegonium, down its neck to the egg. One sperm fuses with the egg, forming a **zygote**.
5. **Sporophyte growth:** the zygote divides and grows, still inside the archegonium, into a **sporophyte** with foot, seta and capsule. It stays attached to the gametophyte all its life.
6. **Spore formation:** inside the capsule, spore mother cells divide by **meiosis** to form many haploid **spores**.
7. **Spore dispersal:** in dry weather the operculum falls off, the peristome teeth open, and the spores are shaken out and blown by the wind.
8. Each spore can grow into a new protonema, and the cycle repeats.

[IMAGE: Circular life cycle diagram of a moss with arrows: spores → green branched protonema thread → leafy gametophyte shoots with rhizoids → male shoot with antheridia releasing swimming sperm and female shoot with flask-shaped archegonia → zygote → sporophyte (seta and capsule) growing on the female shoot → capsule releasing spores. Flat textbook style, soft greens and browns, white background, no text.]

### Liverwort: *Marchantia*

*Marchantia* is a liverwort that grows as flat, green, ribbon-like plants spread over damp soil, rocks and the banks of streams and wells, often in large patches.

#### External features of *Marchantia*

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thallus | Flat, green, leaf-like body, lying on the ground. It forks again and again into two equal branches (**dichotomous branching**). It has a midrib and a notch at the tip of each branch | Photosynthesis; the growing point is in the notch |
| Air pores | Tiny pores on the upper surface, visible as dots | Gas exchange |
| Rhizoids | Single-celled, hair-like threads on the lower surface | Anchor the thallus; absorb water |
| Scales | Small purple or brown flaps on the lower surface | Hold water by capillarity; protect the growing point |
| Gemma cups | Small cup-shaped structures on the upper surface | Contain **gemmae**, small green discs used for asexual reproduction |
| Antheridiophores (male plants) | Stalks with a flat, lobed disc on top | Antheridia sink in the upper surface of the disc |
| Archegoniophores (female plants) | Stalks with an umbrella-like top of finger-like rays | Archegonia hang upside down under the rays |

*Marchantia* plants are either male or female (it is **dioecious**).

[IMAGE: Marchantia: a flat, green, ribbon-like thallus that branches into two again and again (dichotomous branching), lying on soil, with rhizoids underneath, small round gemma cups containing tiny green discs on the upper surface; beside it a male plant with a stalked flat lobed disc (antheridiophore) and a female plant with a stalked umbrella of finger-like rays (archegoniophore). Flat textbook style, soft greens, white background, no text.]

#### Life history of *Marchantia*

**Asexual reproduction:**

1. **By gemmae:** gemmae form in the gemma cups. Raindrops splash them out of the cups. Each gemma that lands on damp soil grows into a new thallus.
2. **By fragmentation:** as the older parts of the thallus die back, the branches separate and grow into separate plants.

**Sexual reproduction:**

1. Male plants grow antheridiophores, and female plants grow archegoniophores.
2. Antheridia release swimming **sperm**. Raindrops splash the sperm from the male discs onto the female plants nearby.
3. The sperm swim through water to the archegonia under the umbrella rays, and one fertilises the egg in each, forming a **zygote**.
4. The zygote grows into a small **sporophyte** (foot, short seta and capsule) that hangs under the umbrella and depends on the female plant.
5. In the capsule, spores form by meiosis, together with long, coiled cells called **elaters**. When the capsule opens, the elaters twist as they dry, flicking the spores out.
6. Spores are dispersed by wind and grow into new male or female thalli.

### Moss and liverwort compared

| Feature | Moss | Liverwort (*Marchantia*) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body form | Upright, leafy shoot with a "stem" and "leaves" | Flat, ribbon-like thallus lying on the ground |
| Branching | Irregular | Dichotomous (forks into two equal branches) |
| Rhizoids | Multicellular, branched | Unicellular, unbranched |
| Asexual reproduction | Fragmentation; buds on the protonema | Gemmae in gemma cups; fragmentation |
| Sex organs | At the tips of leafy shoots | On special stalks: antheridiophores and archegoniophores |
| Sporophyte | Long seta; capsule with peristome teeth | Short seta; capsule with elaters |
| Protonema stage | Well-developed | Very short or absent |

## Pteridophyta: ferns (e.g. *Dryopteris*)

**Pteridophytes** (ferns) are the first plants with **vascular tissue** (xylem and phloem) and with **true roots, stems and leaves**. This allows them to grow much larger than mosses. They still reproduce by spores and need water for fertilisation.

*Dryopteris* (e.g. the male fern, *Dryopteris filix-mas*) is a common fern used as the textbook example. Many ferns grow in Nigeria in shady, damp places: in forests, on stream banks, on old walls and on the trunks of oil palms.

**General characteristics of pteridophytes:**

1. The main plant is the **sporophyte** (diploid), with true roots, stem and leaves.
2. They have **vascular tissue**: xylem carries water and minerals, phloem carries food.
3. Their leaves have a waxy **cuticle** and **stomata**, which reduce water loss and allow gas exchange.
4. They produce **spores** in **sporangia**, grouped in clusters called **sori** on the underside of the leaves.
5. The **gametophyte** is a tiny, flat, independent plant called the **prothallus**.
6. **Sperm** swim through water to reach the egg.
7. They produce no flowers or seeds.

### External features of *Dryopteris*

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rhizome | Short, thick underground stem, covered in brown scales | Stores food; survives the dry season; produces new leaves |
| Adventitious roots | Many thin, dark roots growing from the rhizome | Absorb water and minerals; anchor the plant |
| Fronds (leaves) | Large, **compound pinnate** leaves; each is divided into leaflets (**pinnae**), which are further divided into **pinnules** | Photosynthesis |
| Rachis | The main stalk of the frond | Supports the leaflets |
| Ramenta | Brown, papery scales on the young fronds and rhizome | Protect the young parts |
| Young fronds | Tightly coiled at the tip, like a watch spring or the head of a violin ("fiddleheads"); this is called **circinate vernation** | Protects the delicate growing tip as the frond unrolls |
| Sori (singular: sorus) | Brown dots in rows on the underside of fertile pinnules | Contain sporangia |
| Indusium | Kidney-shaped flap of tissue covering each sorus | Protects the developing sporangia |
| Sporangium | Small stalked capsule in the sorus, with a ring of thick-walled cells called the **annulus** | Produces spores; the annulus dries and springs back, catapulting the spores out |

[IMAGE: A fern plant (Dryopteris type) showing an underground rhizome with brown scales and many thin adventitious roots, several large feathery compound fronds, and one young coiled frond (fiddlehead) unrolling; beside it, an enlarged view of the underside of a pinnule with rows of round brown sori, each partly covered by a kidney-shaped indusium; and one stalked sporangium with a ring of thick-walled annulus cells. Flat textbook style, soft greens and browns, white background, no text.]

### Life history of a fern

1. **Spore formation:** in each sporangium, spore mother cells divide by **meiosis** to produce haploid **spores**.
2. **Spore release:** in dry weather the indusium shrivels. The cells of the **annulus** lose water and pull the sporangium open; the annulus then springs back suddenly, throwing out the spores. The wind carries them away.
3. **Prothallus:** a spore landing on damp, shady soil germinates and grows into a small (about 0.5 to 1 cm), thin, green, **heart-shaped prothallus**, with rhizoids underneath. This is the **gametophyte**. It makes its own food and lives independently.
4. **Sex organs:** on the underside of the prothallus, **antheridia** form among the rhizoids, and **archegonia** form near the notch.
5. **Fertilisation:** when the underside is wet (from rain or dew), the antheridia release **sperm** with many flagella. The sperm swim to the archegonia, and one fertilises the egg in each, forming a diploid **zygote**.
6. **Young sporophyte:** the zygote grows into an embryo, which for a short time gets food from the prothallus. It soon grows its own first leaf and root.
7. **Independence:** the young fern becomes independent; the prothallus withers and dies.
8. The young sporophyte grows into a large, leafy fern, which in time produces sori again.

[IMAGE: Circular life cycle diagram of a fern with arrows: mature fern (sporophyte) → underside of frond with sori → sporangium releasing spores → spore germinating → small heart-shaped green prothallus with rhizoids → underside of prothallus with antheridia (releasing swimming sperm) and archegonia near the notch → young fern sporophyte growing out of the prothallus → back to mature fern. Flat textbook style, soft greens and browns, white background, no text.]

## Spermatophyta: the seed plants

**Spermatophytes** are plants that produce **seeds**. They are the most advanced and most successful land plants. They are divided into **gymnosperms** and **angiosperms**.

**General characteristics of spermatophytes:**

1. The main plant is a large, complex **sporophyte**, with well-developed roots, stems and leaves.
2. They have efficient **vascular tissue**.
3. They produce **pollen grains** (containing the male gamete) and **ovules** (containing the egg).
4. Male gametes are carried to the egg by a **pollen tube**, so **no external water is needed** for fertilisation.
5. After fertilisation, the ovule becomes a **seed**, which contains an embryo and a food store, protected by a seed coat. Seeds can survive harsh conditions and be dispersed far away.
6. The gametophytes are tiny and hidden inside the pollen grain and the ovule; they never live independently.

### Gymnosperms ("naked seeds")

In gymnosperms, the seeds are **not enclosed in an ovary**. They lie "naked" on the surface of scales, usually arranged in **cones**. Gymnosperms have no true flowers and no fruits.

**General characteristics:**

1. Mostly trees or shrubs; many are evergreen.
2. Reproductive structures are **cones** (strobili). Male cones produce pollen; female cones carry ovules on their scales.
3. Pollination is by **wind**.
4. Ovules (and later seeds) are exposed on the scales, not enclosed in an ovary.
5. The xylem is made mainly of **tracheids** (there are usually no vessels).
6. Many have needle-like or scale-like leaves with thick cuticles and sunken stomata, which reduce water loss.

#### Cycads

Cycads look like small palm trees or tree ferns. The sago palm (*Cycas revoluta*) and other cycads are common **ornamental plants** in Nigerian compounds, gardens and roundabouts, although they are not true palms.

- A stout, usually unbranched trunk, covered with the bases of old leaves.
- A crown of large, stiff, **compound pinnate leaves** at the top; young leaves are coiled like fern fronds (circinate vernation).
- Plants are either male or female (dioecious). Male plants bear a large **male cone**. Female plants bear a loose cluster of leaf-like **megasporophylls** (in *Cycas*) or a female cone (in other cycads), with large ovules on their edges.
- Some have coralloid (coral-shaped) roots containing nitrogen-fixing blue-green algae.
- **Evolutionary link:** cycads produce **swimming sperm** with many flagella. They are released from the pollen tube into a small chamber of liquid inside the ovule, and swim the last short distance to the egg. This shows that seed plants evolved from ancestors whose sperm swam, like ferns.

#### Conifers

Conifers ("cone bearers") include pines, cypresses and *Araucaria*. In Nigeria, *Araucaria* (often called the "Christmas tree") is a popular ornamental tree, and pines such as *Pinus caribaea* are grown in some forest plantations for timber and pulp.

- Tall trees with a single straight trunk and a cone-shaped crown.
- **Needle-like** leaves (pine) or **scale-like** leaves (cypress), with thick cuticles and sunken stomata.
- Woody stems that produce **resin**.
- Separate male and female cones on the same tree (in pine). Male cones are small and soft, and release huge amounts of pollen; each pollen grain has two air sacs ("wings") that help it float on the wind. Female cones are larger and woody, with two ovules on the upper surface of each scale.

**Life history of a pine (in outline):**

1. The pine tree is the **sporophyte**.
2. Male cones release **pollen grains**, which are carried by the wind.
3. Some pollen lands on the ovules of young female cones (**pollination**). The female cone scales close up.
4. A **pollen tube** grows slowly into the ovule and carries a male gamete to the egg. In pine, fertilisation happens **about a year** after pollination.
5. The fertilised ovule develops into a **winged seed** on the cone scale. The whole process from pollination to ripe seed takes about two years in many pines.
6. The mature cone dries and opens its scales, and the winged seeds are blown away.
7. A seed germinates into a seedling, which grows into a new tree.

### Angiosperms (flowering plants)

Angiosperms are the most advanced and most widespread plants on Earth. Almost all our food crops (maize, rice, yam, cassava, beans, groundnut, oil palm, cocoa) are angiosperms.

**General characteristics:**

1. They produce **flowers**, which are their organs of sexual reproduction.
2. Their ovules are **enclosed in an ovary**. After fertilisation the ovules become **seeds**, and the ovary becomes a **fruit**, which protects the seeds and helps disperse them.
3. Pollination is by insects, birds, bats, wind or water.
4. They have **double fertilisation**: one male gamete fuses with the egg to form the zygote, and the other fuses with the polar nuclei to form the **endosperm** (food store).
5. Their xylem contains **vessels**, which carry water very efficiently.
6. They range from tiny herbs to giant trees, and live in almost every habitat.

Angiosperms are divided into **monocotyledons** (monocots) and **dicotyledons** (dicots), according to the number of seed leaves (**cotyledons**) in the embryo.

#### Monocot example: maize (*Zea mays*)

Maize is one of Nigeria's most important cereal crops. It is eaten roasted or boiled on the cob, and processed into ogi (pap), tuwo and animal feed.

- **Roots:** a **fibrous root system** (many thin roots of similar size), with **prop roots** growing from the lower nodes to support the tall stem.
- **Stem:** upright, unbranched, with clear **nodes** and **internodes**.
- **Leaves:** long and narrow, with **parallel veins** and a **leaf sheath** wrapping around the stem.
- **Flowers:** maize has separate male and female flowers on the same plant (it is **monoecious**). The male flowers form the **tassel** at the top of the plant; the female flowers form the **cob** (ear) lower down, with long **silks** (styles) hanging out.
- **Pollination:** by **wind**. Pollen from the tassel falls or is blown onto the silks.
- **Fruit:** each "grain" of maize is actually a one-seeded **fruit** (a **caryopsis**), in which the fruit wall is joined to the seed coat.
- **Seed:** has **one cotyledon** (the scutellum) and a large endosperm.
- **Germination:** **hypogeal** (the cotyledon stays below the ground).

#### Dicot example: waterleaf (*Talinum triangulare*)

Waterleaf is a soft, succulent leafy vegetable grown all over southern Nigeria and widely used in soups such as edikang ikong and afang. It grows easily in moist soil, and often grows as a weed in gardens.

**Textbook variation:** waterleaf's scientific name is given as *Talinum triangulare* in most Nigerian textbooks; some newer sources use *Talinum fruticosum* for the same plant.

- **Roots:** a **tap root system** (one main root with side branches).
- **Stem:** soft, fleshy (succulent) and green, branching.
- **Leaves:** simple, fleshy, broad, oval-shaped, with **net (reticulate) venation**.
- **Flowers:** small, pinkish-purple, with **five petals**, which open for only a few hours each day.
- **Fruit:** a small, round capsule containing many tiny black seeds.
- **Seed:** the embryo has **two cotyledons**.
- **Propagation:** easily from stem cuttings, as well as from seeds.

#### Monocots and dicots compared

| Feature | Monocotyledons (e.g. maize) | Dicotyledons (e.g. waterleaf) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of cotyledons | One | Two |
| Root system | Fibrous | Tap root |
| Leaf venation | Parallel | Net (reticulate) |
| Leaf shape | Usually long and narrow, with a sheathing base | Usually broad, often with a stalk (petiole) |
| Flower parts | In threes or multiples of three | In fours or fives, or multiples of these |
| Vascular bundles in stem | Scattered | Arranged in a ring |
| Cambium and secondary growth | Usually absent, so most do not form wood | Present in many, so many form wood and become trees |
| Examples | Maize, rice, sorghum, millet, guinea grass, oil palm, coconut, onion, yam, banana | Waterleaf, groundnut, cowpea, cocoa, mango, orange, hibiscus, cassava, okra |

**Note:** yam is a monocot but has net-veined leaves; trees such as the oil palm and coconut are monocots that grow tall without true wood. These exceptions show that you should use several features, not just one, to decide.

#### Life history of a flowering plant

1. **Seed:** a seed contains an embryo (with radicle, plumule and one or two cotyledons), a food store and a seed coat.
2. **Germination:** with water, oxygen and a suitable temperature, the seed absorbs water, the radicle grows down to form the root, and the plumule grows up to form the shoot.
3. **Seedling and vegetative growth:** the young plant grows roots, stems and leaves, and makes food by photosynthesis.
4. **Flowering:** the mature plant produces flowers. The **stamens** (anthers) produce pollen grains; the **carpels** contain ovules inside the ovary.
5. **Pollination:** pollen is carried from an anther to a stigma by insects, birds or wind.
6. **Fertilisation:** the pollen grain grows a **pollen tube** down the style into the ovule and delivers two male gametes. One fuses with the egg (forming the zygote); the other fuses with the polar nuclei (forming the endosperm). This is **double fertilisation**.
7. **Seed and fruit formation:** the zygote grows into the embryo, the ovule becomes the seed, and the ovary becomes the fruit.
8. **Dispersal:** fruits and seeds are scattered by wind, water, animals or explosive mechanisms, and the cycle begins again.

### Gymnosperms and angiosperms compared

| Feature | Gymnosperms | Angiosperms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seeds | Naked, on cone scales | Enclosed in an ovary (inside a fruit) |
| Reproductive structures | Cones | Flowers |
| Fruits | None | Present |
| Pollination | Wind only | Insects, birds, bats, wind, water |
| Double fertilisation | Absent | Present |

| Leaves | Usually needle-like or scale-like (cycads: large and pinnate) | Usually broad and flat |

## Plants classified on the span of their life cycles

**Objective: classify plants into annuals, biennials and perennials** (printed in the syllabus as "animals, biennials and perennials").

Flowering plants can be grouped by **how long they take to complete their life cycle**, from seed to producing new seeds.

| Group | Life span | What happens | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ephemerals** | A few weeks | Complete several life cycles in one year, growing quickly after rain and setting seed before the soil dries | Many short-lived desert and Sahel herbs that spring up after rain |
| **Annuals** | One growing season (within one year) | Germinate, grow, flower, produce seeds and die in one season. They survive the dry season or winter only as seeds | Maize, rice, sorghum, millet, cowpea, groundnut, okra, tomato |
| **Biennials** | Two growing seasons (two years) | In the first year they grow leaves and store food (often in a swollen root or stem). In the second year they use the stored food to flower, produce seeds and then die | Carrot, cabbage, onion, beetroot |
| **Perennials** | More than two years, often many years | Live for many years. Many flower and produce seeds year after year. They survive unfavourable seasons as woody stems or underground storage organs | Mango, orange, oil palm, cocoa, kola, cashew, iroko; also herbaceous perennials with storage organs, such as ginger |

**Types of perennials:**

- **Woody perennials** have hard, woody stems that stay above ground, e.g. trees such as mango and iroko, and shrubs such as hibiscus.
- **Herbaceous perennials** have soft stems that may die back in the dry season, but survive underground as bulbs, corms, rhizomes or tubers, then grow again in the rains, e.g. ginger (rhizome).

**Textbook variations and notes:**

- Some textbooks include **ephemerals** as a fourth group; others list only annuals, biennials and perennials.
- Carrot, cabbage and onion are true biennials in cool climates. In Nigeria they are usually **harvested in their first season** (for their roots, leaves or bulbs), so farmers grow them like annuals; they flower only if left in the ground, often in cooler places such as the Jos Plateau.
- Several crops that farmers grow as annuals are actually **perennials** by nature, because they can keep growing for years if not harvested, e.g. cassava, yam (which survives by its tuber), pepper and pawpaw. Exam questions use the typical examples above: maize (annual), carrot or cabbage (biennial), mango or oil palm (perennial).

Panel 2 (biennial): a carrot growing leaves and a swollen orange root in year 1, then a tall flowering stalk in year 2, then dying. ## Analysing features and increasing complexity

**Objectives i and ii: analyse the external features and characteristics of the listed plants, and apply this knowledge to demonstrate increase in structural complexity.**

| Feature | *Spirogyra* (Thallophyta) | Moss and *Marchantia* (Bryophyta) | *Dryopteris* (Pteridophyta) | Cycads and conifers (Gymnosperms) | Maize and waterleaf (Angiosperms) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Body | Unbranched filament of identical cells | Simple leafy shoot or flat thallus | True roots, stem (rhizome) and compound leaves | Large woody plants with roots, stems and leaves | Herbs to large trees, with roots, stems, leaves and flowers |
| Anchorage and absorption | None needed; absorbs over whole surface | Rhizoids | True roots | True roots | True roots |
| Vascular tissue | None | None | Xylem (tracheids) and phloem | Xylem (tracheids) and phloem | Xylem (vessels and tracheids) and phloem |
| Cuticle and stomata | None | Thin or absent cuticle; few or no true stomata on leaves | Cuticle and stomata present | Thick cuticle; sunken stomata | Cuticle and stomata present |
| Dominant generation | Haploid filament (only the zygospore is diploid) | Gametophyte | Sporophyte | Sporophyte | Sporophyte |
| Need water for fertilisation? | Yes (lives in water) | Yes (swimming sperm) | Yes (swimming sperm) | No (pollen tube); cycads still have swimming sperm inside the ovule | No (pollen tube) |
| Spores or seeds | Zygospore | Spores | Spores | Naked seeds | Seeds enclosed in fruits |
| Size | Microscopic threads | A few centimetres | Up to about 1–2 m (tree ferns taller) | Shrubs to very tall trees | Tiny herbs to very tall trees |

**The trends to remember:**

1. **Body organisation** becomes more complex: thallus → simple shoot with rhizoids → true roots, stems and leaves → large woody plants with flowers.
2. **Vascular tissue** appears (in ferns), allowing plants to grow tall and live in drier places, then becomes more efficient (vessels in angiosperms).
3. **Protection against drying out** improves: cuticle, stomata, bark, sunken stomata.
4. The **sporophyte** becomes larger and more independent, while the **gametophyte** becomes smaller and more dependent.
5. **Reproduction** moves from swimming sperm to pollen tubes, and from spores to seeds and then to seeds protected in fruits.

## From life in water to life on land

**Objective iv: apply the knowledge of the life histories to demonstrate gradual transition from life in water to life on land.**

Life began in water. Water supports the body, stops it drying out, supplies dissolved minerals, and carries gametes. To live on land, plants had to solve several problems:

| Problem on land | How plants solved it |
| --- | --- |
| Getting water and minerals from the soil | Rhizoids (bryophytes), then true roots (ferns and seed plants) |
| Carrying water up the plant | Vascular tissue: xylem (ferns, gymnosperms), with efficient vessels in angiosperms |
| Drying out | A waxy cuticle, stomata that can close, bark, and needle-like leaves with sunken stomata in conifers |
| Support without water to hold the body up | Lignified xylem and woody stems |
| Fertilisation without water | Pollen grains and pollen tubes, which carry male gametes straight to the egg |
| Surviving dry seasons and spreading to new places | Resistant spores (bryophytes, ferns), then seeds with a food store and a protective coat, then fruits that help dispersal |

**How the life histories show the transition step by step:**

1. ***Spirogyra* (fully aquatic):** lives in water all its life. Gametes move through water-filled conjugation tubes. Only the thick-walled zygospore can survive drying.
2. **Mosses and liverworts (amphibious):** live on land and use wind to spread spores, but their **sperm must swim** in a film of water to reach the egg. They have no vascular tissue and dry out easily, so they are restricted to damp, shady places. That is why they are called the "amphibians" of the plant kingdom.
3. **Ferns (land plants still tied to water for reproduction):** the large sporophyte is well adapted to land, with roots, xylem and phloem, a cuticle and stomata. But the small **prothallus** needs damp ground, and the **sperm still swim** to the egg. So ferns are common in damp, shady places, but can't spread into dry places easily.
4. **Gymnosperms (true land plants):** **pollen**, carried by wind, and the **pollen tube** take the male gamete to the egg, so fertilisation no longer needs outside water. The new plant is protected inside a **seed** that can survive drought. Cycads show the link: their sperm still swim, but only inside the ovule.
5. **Angiosperms (best adapted to land):** flowers attract animals that carry pollen precisely; double fertilisation provides food for the seed; **fruits** protect seeds and help disperse them; vessels carry water efficiently. Angiosperms live everywhere, from swamps to deserts.

## The evolution of the listed plants

**Objective v: trace the evolution of the listed plants.**

Plants are believed to have evolved from simple green algae that lived in water. Over millions of years, the main groups appeared in this order:

**Green algae (*Spirogyra*) → Bryophytes (mosses: *Brachymenium*; liverworts: *Marchantia*) → Pteridophytes (ferns: *Dryopteris*) → Gymnosperms (cycads, conifers) → Angiosperms (monocots: maize; dicots: waterleaf)**

| Stage | Group | Main evolutionary advance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Thallophyta (*Spirogyra*) | Photosynthesis in multicellular filaments; sexual reproduction (conjugation); resistant zygospore |
| 2 | Bryophyta (moss, *Marchantia*) | First land plants; rhizoids; multicellular sex organs (antheridia, archegonia) that protect gametes; the embryo is protected inside the archegonium; spores dispersed by wind |
| 3 | Pteridophyta (*Dryopteris*) | First vascular tissue and true roots, stems and leaves; sporophyte dominant and independent |
| 4 | Gymnospermae (cycads, conifers) | Seeds; pollen and pollen tubes, so fertilisation without external water; wood |
| 5 | Angiospermae (maize, waterleaf) | Flowers; seeds enclosed in an ovary; fruits; double fertilisation; vessels in xylem |

**Textbook note:** the order above shows increasing complexity and the order in which the groups appear in the fossil record. It does not mean that mosses turned directly into ferns, or ferns into pines. Each group branched off from shared ancestors long ago, and all of them are still alive today.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Thallus | A simple plant body not divided into roots, stems and leaves |
| Alternation of generations | A life cycle in which a gamete-producing generation (gametophyte) alternates with a spore-producing generation (sporophyte) |
| Gametophyte | The haploid generation that produces gametes |
| Sporophyte | The diploid generation that produces spores |
| Antheridium | Male sex organ in bryophytes and ferns, producing sperm |
| Archegonium | Flask-shaped female sex organ in bryophytes and ferns, containing one egg |
| Rhizoid | A thread-like structure that anchors simple plants and absorbs water |
| Protonema | The green, thread-like first stage that grows from a moss spore |
| Gemma | A small bud-like body used for asexual reproduction in *Marchantia* |
| Prothallus | The small, heart-shaped, independent gametophyte of a fern |
| Sorus | A cluster of sporangia on the underside of a fern leaf |

| Gymnosperm | A seed plant whose seeds are not enclosed in an ovary (naked seeds) |

| Cotyledon | A seed leaf in the embryo of a seed plant |
| Annual | A plant that completes its life cycle in one growing season |
| Biennial | A plant that completes its life cycle in two growing seasons |
| Perennial | A plant that lives for more than two years |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Dominant generation:** gametophyte in mosses and liverworts; sporophyte in ferns and seed plants. This is a favourite JAMB question.
- **First vascular plants:** ferns (pteridophytes), not mosses.
- **Water for fertilisation:** bryophytes and ferns need it (swimming sperm); seed plants do not (pollen tube).
- **The fern prothallus** is the gametophyte; the big leafy fern is the sporophyte.
- **Peristome teeth** (moss) and **annulus** (fern) both help release spores in dry weather. **Elaters** do the same job in *Marchantia*.
- **"Naked seeds"** = gymnosperms. Angiosperm seeds are inside a fruit.
- **Maize grain is a fruit** (caryopsis), not just a seed.
- **Monocot vs dicot:** parallel veins and fibrous roots (maize) vs net veins and tap root (waterleaf).
- **Biennials:** carrot, cabbage, onion. A common wrong option is to call cassava or yam biennials.
- **Spirogyra's spiral chloroplast** is the feature that names it; its zygospore is the resistant stage.

## Quick summary

1. Plants show alternation of generations: a haploid gametophyte (makes gametes) alternates with a diploid sporophyte (makes spores).
2. *Spirogyra* is an aquatic, filamentous green alga with spiral chloroplasts; it reproduces by fragmentation and by conjugation to form zygospores.
3. Bryophytes (moss, *Marchantia*) have a dominant gametophyte, rhizoids and no vascular tissue, and need water for their swimming sperm.
4. Ferns (*Dryopteris*) are the first vascular plants with true roots, stems and leaves; the sporophyte is dominant and the prothallus is a small independent gametophyte.
5. Seed plants use pollen and pollen tubes, so they need no water for fertilisation; gymnosperms have naked seeds in cones, angiosperms have seeds enclosed in fruits.
6. Monocots (maize) and dicots (waterleaf) differ in cotyledons, venation, root system, flower parts and vascular bundle arrangement.
7. Plants evolved from water to land: algae → bryophytes → pteridophytes → gymnosperms → angiosperms, with increasing complexity and independence from water.
8. Plants are classified by life span into annuals (maize), biennials (carrot) and perennials (mango).

## Practice questions

**1. In which of the following plants is the gametophyte the dominant generation?**

- A. Fern
- B. Moss
- C. Maize
- D. Pine

**Answer: B.** In mosses, the leafy green plant is the gametophyte; the sporophyte grows on it. In ferns and seed plants the sporophyte is dominant.

**2. The spiral chloroplast is a characteristic feature of**

- A. *Chlamydomonas*
- B. *Spirogyra*
- C. *Marchantia*
- D. *Dryopteris*

**Answer: B.** *Spirogyra* has spiral, ribbon-shaped chloroplasts, which give it its name.

**3. The heart-shaped prothallus of a fern is the**

- A. sporophyte
- B. sporangium
- C. gametophyte
- D. sorus

**Answer: C.** The prothallus is the small, independent gametophyte that produces antheridia and archegonia.

**4. Gemma cups are used for asexual reproduction in**

- A. *Marchantia*
- B. moss
- C. fern
- D. *Spirogyra*

**Answer: A.** *Marchantia* produces gemmae in gemma cups; rain splashes them out and they grow into new plants.

**5. Bryophytes are called "amphibians of the plant kingdom" because they**

- A. live only in water
- B. have true roots
- C. live on land but need water for fertilisation
- D. produce seeds

**Answer: C.** Their sperm must swim through water to reach the egg, even though they grow on land.

**6. Which of the following is the first group of plants to have vascular tissue?**

- A. Thallophyta
- B. Bryophyta
- C. Pteridophyta
- D. Angiospermae

**Answer: C.** Ferns (pteridophytes) were the first plants with xylem and phloem.

**7. Gymnosperms differ from angiosperms in that gymnosperms**

- A. produce seeds
- B. have naked seeds not enclosed in an ovary
- C. have vascular tissue
- D. are pollinated by wind

**Answer: B.** Gymnosperm seeds lie exposed on cone scales. Both groups produce seeds and have vascular tissue, and some angiosperms (like maize) are also wind-pollinated.

**8. Which of the following is a feature of a monocotyledonous plant like maize?**

- A. Net venation
- B. Tap root system
- C. Flower parts in fives
- D. Parallel venation

**Answer: D.** Monocots have parallel venation, fibrous roots and flower parts in threes.

**9. Which feature allowed seed plants to reproduce sexually without external water?**

- A. Rhizoids
- B. Pollen tube
- C. Spores
- D. Swimming sperm

**Answer: B.** The pollen tube carries the male gamete directly to the egg, so no film of water is needed.

**10. A plant that completes its life cycle in two growing seasons is called**

- A. an ephemeral
- B. an annual
- C. a biennial
- D. a perennial

**Answer: C.** Biennials, such as carrot, grow and store food in the first season, then flower, set seed and die in the second.
