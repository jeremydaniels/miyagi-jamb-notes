## General characteristics of the animal kingdom

Members of the kingdom Animalia:

1. Are **eukaryotic** and **multicellular**.
2. Have **no cell walls** and **no chlorophyll**.
3. Are **heterotrophic**: they feed by taking in (ingesting) ready-made food, usually into a gut.
4. Store food as **glycogen** and fat.
5. Can usually **move** from place to place, using muscles.
6. Have **nervous tissue** (except sponges) and respond quickly to stimuli.
7. Usually reproduce sexually, with a large, non-moving egg and small, swimming sperm.

Animals are divided into two big groups:

| Feature | Invertebrates | Vertebrates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backbone (vertebral column) | Absent | Present |
| Skeleton | None, a hydrostatic skeleton, an exoskeleton or a shell | Internal skeleton (endoskeleton) of bone or cartilage |
| Nerve cord | Usually on the lower (ventral) side, solid | On the upper (dorsal) side, hollow, protected by the backbone |
| Heart (if present) | Usually on the upper (dorsal) side | On the lower (ventral) side |
| Size | Usually small | Usually larger |
| Examples | *Hydra*, tapeworm, earthworm, insects, snail | Fish, frog, lizard, bird, human |

## How we measure "advancement" in animals

To trace the advancement of animals, biologists compare a few key features. As animals become more advanced, they change in the following ways:

| Feature | Simple condition | More advanced condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Symmetry** | **Radial**: the body can be cut into similar halves along many planes through the centre, like a cake (e.g. *Hydra*) | **Bilateral**: the body has a definite left and right side, a front (head) end and a back end; it can be cut into two mirror-image halves along one plane only (e.g. earthworm, human) |
| **Germ layers** | **Diploblastic**: two cell layers, ectoderm and endoderm (e.g. *Hydra*) | **Triploblastic**: three layers, with a middle **mesoderm** that forms muscles and organs (all other phyla here) |
| **Body cavity** | **Acoelomate**: no body cavity (e.g. flatworms) | **Pseudocoelomate** (a false cavity, e.g. roundworms), then **coelomate**: a true fluid-filled cavity in the mesoderm (e.g. earthworm and higher animals) |
| **Gut** | One opening, used as both mouth and anus (e.g. *Hydra*) | A complete gut, with a separate mouth and anus (from roundworms upwards) |
| **Segmentation** | Unsegmented body | Body divided into repeated segments (e.g. earthworm, insects) |
| **Head (cephalisation)** | No head | Sense organs and nerve cells collected at a head end, with a brain |
| **Skeleton** | None, or a fluid (hydrostatic) skeleton | Exoskeleton (arthropods), then a jointed endoskeleton of bone (vertebrates) |
| **Organ systems** | Few or none | Well-developed circulatory, excretory, nervous and reproductive systems |

## Invertebrates

### Coelenterata: *Hydra*

The coelenterates (also called **Cnidaria**) include *Hydra*, jellyfish, sea anemones and corals. Almost all of them live in the sea; *Hydra* is one of the few that live in fresh water.

**Characteristics of coelenterates:**

1. **Radially symmetrical.**
2. **Diploblastic**: the body wall is made of two cell layers, the outer **ectoderm** and the inner **endoderm**, with a jelly-like **mesoglea** between them.
3. A single body cavity, the **enteron** (coelenteron), with **one opening** (the mouth), which also serves as the anus.
4. **Tentacles** around the mouth, armed with stinging cells (**cnidoblasts**) containing **nematocysts**.
5. A simple **nerve net**, with no brain.
6. No organs for breathing, circulation or excretion; these happen by diffusion.
7. Reproduction both asexual (**budding**) and sexual.

#### External features of *Hydra*

*Hydra* was introduced in Topic A1 as the example of the tissue level of organisation. Its main external features are:

- A small, soft, tube-shaped body, about 1 cm long when stretched out, green or brown in colour.
- At the top, a cone-shaped **hypostome** with the **mouth** at its tip.
- A ring of 6 to 10 long, hollow, thread-like **tentacles** around the hypostome.
- At the bottom, a sticky **basal disc**, used to attach to water plants and stones.
- Buds, testes or ovaries may be seen as swellings on the side of the body.

[IMAGE: Diagram of Hydra in longitudinal section, showing tentacles, mouth on the hypostome, enteron (body cavity), ectoderm, endoderm, mesoglea, a bud growing from the side, and the basal disc attached to a water plant. Flat textbook style, soft greens and blues, white background.]

#### How *Hydra* lives

- **Feeding:** *Hydra* is carnivorous. When small animals such as water fleas (*Daphnia*) touch a tentacle, the nematocysts shoot out threads that sting, paralyse and hold the prey. The tentacles push it through the mouth into the enteron. Gland cells release enzymes and digestion begins in the enteron; endoderm cells then take in the small particles and finish digestion inside the cells. Undigested remains are pushed out through the mouth.
- **Movement:** it can glide slowly on its basal disc, or move by **somersaulting** (bending over, attaching by the tentacles, then flipping the base over) or "looping" like a caterpillar.
- **Response:** if touched, the whole body and tentacles contract quickly into a small ball, as impulses spread through the nerve net.
- **Regeneration:** if *Hydra* is cut into pieces, each piece can grow into a complete new animal.

#### Life history of *Hydra*

**1. Asexual reproduction by budding (when food is plentiful):**

1. A small bulge (bud) forms on the side of the body, about halfway down.
2. The bud grows longer; its enteron is connected to the parent's.
3. A mouth and tentacles develop at the free end of the bud.
4. The base of the bud narrows and pinches off.
5. The young *Hydra* separates and lives independently.

**2. Sexual reproduction (often in cool, unfavourable conditions):**

1. **Testes** (cone-shaped swellings) develop on the upper part of the body, near the tentacles. **Ovaries** (rounded swellings) develop lower down, near the base. In some species both are on the same animal (hermaphrodite); in others the sexes are separate.
2. Sperm are released into the water and swim to an ovary.
3. One sperm fertilises the single large egg in the ovary.
4. The zygote divides to form an embryo, which surrounds itself with a hard, spiky protective shell (a cyst).
5. The embryo in its shell drops off the parent and can survive cold or drought.
6. When conditions improve, the shell breaks and a tiny young *Hydra* hatches out. There is no larval stage.

### Platyhelminthes (flatworms): *Taenia*

Platyhelminthes are **flatworms**. They include free-living planarians, and the parasitic **flukes** (e.g. the blood fluke *Schistosoma*, which causes bilharzia) and **tapeworms** (e.g. *Taenia*).

**Characteristics of flatworms:**

1. The body is **flattened** from top to bottom (dorsoventrally), soft and unsegmented (though tapeworms are divided into proglottids).
2. **Bilaterally symmetrical** (the first group to show this).
3. **Triploblastic**: they have a third, middle cell layer (mesoderm).
4. **Acoelomate**: there is no body cavity; the space between gut and body wall is filled with tissue.
5. The gut, if present, has **only one opening**. Tapeworms have no gut at all.
6. They have simple excretory organs (**flame cells**) and a simple nervous system with a "brain" at the front.
7. Most are **hermaphrodite**.

#### *Taenia* (tapeworm)

Two tapeworms infect humans:

- ***Taenia solium***, the **pork tapeworm**, about 2 to 7 m long.
- ***Taenia saginata***, the **beef tapeworm**, usually 4 to 10 m long.

**Textbook variation:** the lengths given for tapeworms vary between textbooks; some quote up to 8 m for *T. solium* and over 12 m for *T. saginata*.

Adult tapeworms live attached to the inside wall of the **small intestine** of humans. Humans are the **primary (definitive) host**, where the adult worm lives and reproduces. The pig (for *T. solium*) or cow (for *T. saginata*) is the **secondary (intermediate) host**, where the young stage lives.

#### External features of *Taenia*

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scolex ("head") | Small, about the size of a pin head | Attachment to the host's intestine wall |
| Suckers | Four round, muscular suckers on the scolex | Grip the intestine wall |
| Rostellum with hooks | A round knob on top of the scolex with a double ring of curved hooks (in *T. solium*; *T. saginata* has no hooks) | Hook firmly into the intestine wall |
| Neck | Short, narrow region behind the scolex | Grows new proglottids continuously (budding zone) |
| Proglottids (segments) | A long chain (strobila) of flat segments, up to about 1,000 | Each has its own male and female reproductive organs |
| Immature proglottids | Young, small ones near the neck | Sex organs not yet developed |
| Mature proglottids | In the middle | Contain fully developed male and female organs; fertilisation happens here |
| Gravid proglottids | Oldest, largest ones at the end | Filled with a branched uterus packed with thousands of fertilised eggs; break off and pass out in faeces |

#### Adaptations of *Taenia* to its parasitic life

- **Hooks and suckers** stop it being swept out of the intestine.
- **No gut:** it lives in digested food and absorbs simple nutrients directly over its whole body surface. Its flat, thin, long shape gives a very large surface area for absorption.
- A thick outer covering (**tegument**) and the production of **anti-enzymes** protect it from being digested by the host's enzymes.
- It can respire **anaerobically**, because there is little oxygen in the intestine.
- It is **hermaphrodite**, so a single worm can fertilise itself; it does not need to find a mate.
- It produces **huge numbers of eggs** (each gravid proglottid contains tens of thousands), increasing the chance that some will reach a new host.
- The eggs have a **tough shell**, so they survive outside the host.
- It uses an **intermediate host** (pig or cow) to carry it to the next human.
- Its nervous and sense organs are greatly reduced, because it does not need to move or find food.

#### Life history of the pork tapeworm (*Taenia solium*)

1. **Eggs leave the human:** gravid proglottids, full of fertilised eggs, break off the end of the worm and pass out with the faeces of the infected person.
2. **Pig is infected:** where people defecate in the open, a pig feeding on contaminated soil or food swallows the eggs or proglottids.
3. **Hatching:** in the pig's intestine, the eggshell is digested, releasing a tiny embryo with six hooks, called an **onchosphere** (hexacanth embryo).
4. **Migration:** the onchosphere uses its hooks to bore through the intestine wall into a blood vessel, and is carried by the blood to the **muscles** (especially the tongue, heart and limbs).
5. **Bladder worm:** in the muscle, it grows into a small, fluid-filled bladder, about 1 cm across, with an inverted (inside-out) scolex. This is the **cysticercus** or **bladder worm**. Infected pork with many cysticerci is called **"measly pork"**.
6. **Human is infected:** a person eats raw or undercooked pork containing live cysticerci.
7. **Development in human:** in the human small intestine, the bladder is digested away; the scolex turns outwards (evaginates) and attaches to the intestine wall with its hooks and suckers.
8. **Growth:** the neck begins to bud off proglottids, and in about 2 to 3 months the worm is an adult, releasing gravid proglottids again.

**Danger:** if a human swallows ***T. solium* eggs** (from dirty hands, water or vegetables), cysticerci can develop in the person's own muscles, eyes and brain. This disease, **cysticercosis**, can cause fits (epilepsy).

The life history of the beef tapeworm is the same, except that the intermediate host is a cow, which picks up the eggs while grazing on contaminated pasture.

[IMAGE: Circular life cycle diagram of the pork tapeworm: an adult tapeworm in a human intestine → gravid proglottids passing out in faeces onto the ground → a pig eating contaminated food → six-hooked embryo boring through the pig's gut wall into blood → bladder worms (cysticerci) as small round cysts in pig muscle → raw or undercooked pork being eaten by a human → back to adult tapeworm in the human intestine. Flat textbook style, soft pinks, creams and browns, white background, no text.]

**Prevention and control of tapeworm infection:**

- Cook pork and beef thoroughly.
- Inspect meat in abattoirs and destroy measly meat.
- Use toilets and dispose of human faeces properly, so pigs and cattle cannot eat the eggs.
- Keep pigs in pens, away from human waste.
- Treat infected persons with deworming drugs.
- Wash hands before eating and after using the toilet.

### Nematoda (roundworms)

Nematodes are **roundworms**. Many live freely in the soil and water; others are important parasites of humans, animals and plants.

**Examples of parasitic roundworms in Nigeria:**

| Roundworm | Disease or effect |
| --- | --- |
| *Ascaris lumbricoides* (the large human roundworm) | Ascariasis: abdominal pain, malnutrition, blocked intestines, especially in children |
| Hookworms (*Ancylostoma* and *Necator*) | Hookworm disease: larvae enter through bare feet; adults suck blood in the intestine, causing **anaemia** |
| *Wuchereria bancrofti* (filarial worm) | **Elephantiasis**: swelling of the legs and other parts; spread by mosquitoes |
| *Onchocerca volvulus* | **River blindness** (onchocerciasis); spread by blackflies (*Simulium*) near fast-flowing rivers |
| *Dracunculus medinensis* (Guinea worm) | Guinea worm disease, from drinking water containing infected water fleas. Nigeria was certified free of Guinea worm transmission in 2013 |
| Plant nematodes, e.g. root-knot nematodes | Swellings on roots of crops such as tomato and yam, reducing yields |

**Characteristics of nematodes:**

1. Long, thin, **cylindrical** body, pointed at both ends, and **not segmented**.
2. Covered by a tough, non-living **cuticle**, which protects parasitic forms from the host's enzymes. They **moult** the cuticle as they grow.
3. **Bilaterally symmetrical** and **triploblastic**.
4. A body cavity called a **pseudocoelom** ("false coelom").
5. A **complete gut**, with a separate **mouth** at the front and **anus** near the back. This is the first group (in this list) with a one-way gut.
6. Only **longitudinal muscles**, so they move by whipping or thrashing from side to side.
7. **Sexes are separate**; the male is usually smaller than the female.

#### *Ascaris lumbricoides*: a typical roundworm

**External features:**

- Creamy-white or pinkish, smooth and shiny.
- The female is up to about 35 cm long, with a straight tail; the male is smaller (about 15–30 cm) with a **curved (hooked) tail** carrying two small spines (spicules) used in mating.
- The mouth is at the front, surrounded by **three lips**.
- Four faint lines run along the body.

[IMAGE: An adult female and an adult male Ascaris roundworm side by side: long smooth creamy-pink cylindrical worms tapering at both ends; the female longer and straight, the male shorter with a curled tail. An enlarged front view of the mouth with three lips. Flat textbook style, soft pinks and creams, white background, no text.]

**Life history of *Ascaris*:**

1. Adult male and female worms live in the human **small intestine** and mate. A female can lay up to about **200,000 eggs** per day.
2. The fertilised eggs, with thick, tough shells, pass out with the **faeces**.
3. In warm, moist soil, a larva develops inside each egg over about 2 to 3 weeks. The egg is now **infective**.
4. A person swallows the infective eggs with contaminated food, water, or dirty hands (e.g. eating unwashed vegetables or fruits grown in soil with human faeces).
5. The eggs hatch in the small intestine, releasing larvae.
6. The larvae bore through the intestine wall into the blood, and are carried through the liver and heart to the **lungs**.
7. In the lungs, they grow and moult, then climb up the windpipe to the throat, where they are **coughed up and swallowed** again.
8. Back in the small intestine, they grow into adults in about 2 to 3 months.

There is **no intermediate host**: the whole life cycle uses only one host (humans).

**Prevention:** use toilets; wash hands before eating; wash fruits and vegetables well; drink clean water; deworm children regularly.

### Annelida: earthworm

Annelids are **segmented worms**. They include earthworms, leeches and marine bristle worms (e.g. *Nereis*).

**Characteristics of annelids:**

1. A long, soft, cylindrical body divided into many similar **segments** (rings). This is called **metameric segmentation**: each segment has its own set of muscles, nerves, blood vessels and excretory organs.
2. **Bilaterally symmetrical** and **triploblastic**.
3. A **true coelom** (body cavity), divided by walls (septa) between the segments.
4. A **complete gut** with mouth and anus.
5. A **closed blood system**, with blood (containing haemoglobin) flowing in vessels pumped by several "hearts".
6. A nerve cord on the **lower (ventral) side**, with a simple brain.
7. Many have bristles called **chaetae** (setae).

#### External features of the earthworm

Earthworms are common in moist, rich soil in Nigeria. In the rainy season, their worm casts (small heaps of soil they pass out) appear on lawns and farmland. A well-known West African species is *Eudrilus eugeniae* (the African night crawler); the textbook example worldwide is *Lumbricus terrestris*.

| Feature | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segments | Body divided into many (often over 100) ring-like segments | Allow parts of the body to move separately |
| Prostomium | A fleshy lobe overhanging the mouth on the first segment | Feeling and pushing into soil |
| Mouth | On the first segment | Takes in soil and decaying leaves |
| Anus | On the last segment | Passes out undigested soil (worm casts) |
| Clitellum (saddle) | A swollen, smooth, lighter-coloured band of segments about one third of the way along the body | Secretes mucus during mating and makes the cocoon |
| Chaetae (setae) | Tiny bristles, four pairs on each segment except the first and last | Grip the soil during movement |
| Skin | Thin, moist and covered with mucus, with many blood vessels underneath | Gas exchange (oxygen in, carbon dioxide out) |
| Dorsal and ventral surfaces | Upper surface darker; lower surface flatter and paler | The ventral surface is pressed against the soil |
| Genital openings | Small pores on certain segments in front of the clitellum | Pass out sperm and eggs |

#### How the earthworm moves

The earthworm has a **hydrostatic skeleton**: the fluid in its coelom is held under pressure by the body wall.

1. The **circular muscles** at the front end contract, making the front segments long and thin. They push forward into the soil.
2. The chaetae at the front grip the soil.
3. The **longitudinal muscles** at the front then contract, making the segments short and fat. This pulls the back part of the body forward.
4. The chaetae at the back are pulled in and the front ones grip, so the worm moves forward in waves.

#### Life history of the earthworm

Earthworms are **hermaphrodite** (each worm has both testes and ovaries), but they **cannot fertilise themselves**. Two worms must mate to exchange sperm (cross-fertilisation).

1. **Mating:** on a warm, damp night, two worms lie side by side with their heads pointing in opposite directions and their lower surfaces together. The clitellum of each worm lies against the sperm receptacles of the other. They are held together by a thick layer of mucus from the clitellum.
2. **Exchange of sperm:** sperm from each worm pass into the **sperm receptacles** (spermathecae) of the other, where they are stored. The worms then separate.
3. **Cocoon formation:** a few days later, each worm's **clitellum** secretes a tough, slimy band (the cocoon) around its body.
4. **Eggs and sperm added:** the worm wriggles backwards out of the band. As the band slides forward over the body, it collects **eggs** from the female opening and then the stored **sperm** from the sperm receptacles.
5. **Fertilisation:** the eggs are fertilised inside the band.
6. **Cocoon sealed:** the band slips off over the head; its two ends close up, forming a small, lemon-shaped **cocoon** in the soil.
7. **Hatching:** after a few weeks, one or more tiny worms hatch out. They look like small adults; there is **no larval stage** (development is direct).

#### Importance of earthworms

- Their burrows **aerate** the soil and improve **drainage**.
- They pull dead leaves into the soil, mixing organic matter and increasing **humus**.
- They bring deeper soil to the surface as worm casts, **mixing soil layers**.
- Their casts are rich in nutrients, making the soil more **fertile**. Earthworms are used to make compost (vermicomposting).
- They are food for birds, frogs and moles, and are used as **fish bait** and in poultry and fish feed.

### Arthropoda

Arthropods ("jointed feet") are the largest phylum in the animal kingdom, with more known species than all other animal groups combined.

**Characteristics of arthropods:**

1. **Jointed limbs** (legs, antennae and mouthparts).
2. A hard **exoskeleton** (outer skeleton) made of **chitin**, which protects the body, supports it, stops water loss and provides attachment for muscles.
3. They must shed their exoskeleton to grow. This is called **moulting** (ecdysis).
4. A **segmented** body, with segments grouped into regions (e.g. head, thorax and abdomen).
5. **Bilaterally symmetrical** and **triploblastic**.
6. An **open blood system**: blood is pumped by a tube-like heart on the upper side into a body cavity called the **haemocoel**, where it bathes the organs.
7. A ventral nerve cord, a brain, and well-developed sense organs, including **compound eyes**.

**Main classes of arthropods:**

| Class | Body regions | Legs | Antennae | Wings | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Insecta (insects) | 3: head, thorax, abdomen | 3 pairs | 1 pair | Usually 2 pairs (1 pair in flies; none in some) | Mosquito, cockroach, housefly, bee, butterfly, termite |
| Crustacea | 2: cephalothorax, abdomen | Many (often 5 pairs of walking legs) | 2 pairs | None | Crab, prawn, crayfish, woodlouse |
| Arachnida | 2: cephalothorax, abdomen | 4 pairs | None | None | Spider, scorpion, tick, mite |
| Myriapoda (Chilopoda and Diplopoda) | Head plus many similar segments | Many: 1 pair per segment (centipede) or 2 pairs per segment (millipede) | 1 pair | None | Centipede, millipede |

The insects studied in the syllabus are the mosquito, cockroach, housefly, bee and butterfly.

#### General external features of an insect

| Body region | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Head | A pair of **antennae** (feelers) for touch and smell; a pair of **compound eyes** made of many units; often simple eyes (ocelli); **mouthparts** adapted to the insect's diet |
| Thorax | Three segments, each with one pair of **jointed legs**; usually two pairs of **wings** on the second and third segments |
| Abdomen | Several segments with no legs; **spiracles** (breathing holes) along the sides, leading into air tubes (**tracheae**); the reproductive openings at the end |

#### Metamorphosis: how insects grow up

Insects change their form as they grow. This is called **metamorphosis**.

| Feature | Complete metamorphosis | Incomplete metamorphosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stages | Four: **egg → larva → pupa → adult (imago)** | Three: **egg → nymph → adult** |
| Young stage | **Larva**, which looks completely different from the adult (e.g. a caterpillar, maggot or wriggler) | **Nymph**, which looks like a small adult without fully grown wings |
| Pupa (resting stage) | Present: the larva's body is broken down and rebuilt into the adult | Absent |
| Food of young and adult | Often different (e.g. caterpillar eats leaves, butterfly drinks nectar), so they don't compete | Often the same |
| Wings | Develop inside the body during the pupa stage | Develop gradually outside the body, as small wing pads that grow at each moult |
| Examples | Mosquito, housefly, bee, butterfly, beetles | Cockroach, grasshopper, locust, termite |

#### The mosquito

There are three common types of mosquito in Nigeria:

- ***Anopheles***: carries the malaria parasite (*Plasmodium*); can also carry the filarial worm.
- ***Culex***: carries the filarial worm that causes elephantiasis; it is the common night-biting "house" mosquito.
- ***Aedes***: carries the viruses of yellow fever and dengue fever; often bites during the day.

**External features of an adult mosquito:**

- Slender body with head, thorax and abdomen.
- Head with a long, piercing **proboscis**, a pair of **palps**, a pair of **antennae** (feathery and bushy in males, with fewer, shorter hairs in females) and large compound eyes.
- **One pair of wings**, with scales along the veins; the hind wings are reduced to small knobs called **halteres**, used for balance.
- Three pairs of long, thin legs.
- Only the **female** sucks blood: she needs the protein in blood to make her eggs. The male feeds on plant juices and nectar.

**Life history of the mosquito (complete metamorphosis):**

1. **Eggs:** after a blood meal, the female lays her eggs on the surface of still water, such as ponds, puddles, blocked drains, old tyres, tins and pots holding rainwater.
2. **Larva ("wriggler"):** in 2 to 3 days the eggs hatch into larvae. The larva lives in water, hanging just below the surface, and breathes air through a tube (**siphon**) or spiracles at the end of its abdomen. It feeds on tiny organisms and organic matter, using brushes around its mouth. It moults four times as it grows.
3. **Pupa ("tumbler"):** after about a week, the larva becomes a **comma-shaped** pupa. The pupa does not feed, but it is active: it tumbles down when disturbed. It breathes through two **respiratory trumpets** on its thorax.
4. **Adult:** after 2 to 3 days, the pupa's skin splits along the back, and the adult mosquito climbs out, rests on the old skin on the water surface until its wings dry, and then flies away.

In warm tropical conditions, the whole cycle takes about **10 to 14 days**.

**Differences between *Anopheles* and *Culex*:**

| Stage | *Anopheles* | *Culex* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eggs | Laid singly, each with air-filled **floats** at the sides | Laid together in a raft of 100 or more eggs |
| Larva | No siphon; lies **parallel** to the water surface | Has a long siphon; hangs **at an angle** to the water surface |

**Control of mosquitoes:**

1. **Destroy breeding sites:** drain stagnant water, clear blocked gutters, cover water tanks, remove old tins and tyres.
2. **Kill larvae and pupae:** spray oil on water surfaces (it blocks their breathing tubes); use larvicides; stock ponds with fish (e.g. *Tilapia* and *Gambusia*) that eat larvae.
3. **Kill adults:** spray insecticides indoors; use insecticide-treated nets.
4. **Prevent bites:** sleep under insecticide-treated mosquito nets; use window and door screens and insect repellents.

#### The cockroach

The common cockroach in Nigerian homes is the **American cockroach** (*Periplaneta americana*). It is active at night (**nocturnal**), hides in cracks, cupboards, kitchens and drains in the day, and eats almost anything (it is **omnivorous**).

**External features:**

- A flattened, oval, shiny reddish-brown body, about 3 to 4 cm long.
- **Head** bent downwards, with very long, thread-like **antennae**, a pair of kidney-shaped **compound eyes** and **biting and chewing mouthparts**.
- A broad shield (the **pronotum**) covering the first segment of the thorax.
- Two pairs of wings: the **forewings** are hard and leathery (**tegmina**) and protect the thin, membranous **hindwings**, which are used for flying and fold like a fan under the forewings.
- Three pairs of long, spiny, jointed legs, adapted for running.
- At the tip of the abdomen, a pair of jointed **cerci** (feelers that detect air movements); males also have a pair of thin **anal styles**.

[IMAGE: Dorsal (top) view of an adult cockroach with long thread-like antennae, compound eyes, a broad shield-like pronotum behind the head, two leathery forewings covering most of the abdomen, three pairs of spiny jointed legs, and a pair of short cerci at the tail end. Flat textbook style, warm reddish-browns, white background, no text.]

**Life history of the cockroach (incomplete metamorphosis):**

1. **Egg case:** after mating, the female produces a hard, brown, purse-shaped **egg case** (ootheca) containing about 14 to 16 eggs in two rows. She carries it at the tip of her abdomen for a day or two, then glues it in a dark, warm, safe place such as a crack.
2. **Nymphs:** after about 1 to 2 months, small, white, wingless **nymphs** hatch. They look like small adults without wings. They soon darken.
3. **Moulting:** the nymphs feed and grow, moulting many times (often 6 to 13 times, depending on the species and conditions). Small wing pads appear and get bigger at each moult.
4. **Adult:** after the final moult, the adult with fully developed wings emerges. In the American cockroach, the nymph stage can last from several months to over a year.

**Importance:** cockroaches spoil food with their droppings and smell, damage books and cloth, and carry the germs of diseases such as typhoid, dysentery and diarrhoea on their bodies. Keep kitchens clean, cover food, seal cracks and use insecticides or baits to control them.

#### The housefly

The housefly (*Musca domestica*) is found wherever people live, especially near rubbish heaps, open drains, toilets, markets and animal pens.

**External features:**

- A grey body, about 6 to 7 mm long, with dark stripes on the thorax and a hairy body.
- Large, reddish-brown **compound eyes** (close together in males, further apart in females).
- Short, three-segmented **antennae** with a bristle (arista).
- **Sponging (lapping) mouthparts**: a fleshy **proboscis** ending in two pad-like **labella**, with fine grooves that suck up liquid food. The fly cannot bite.
- **One pair of wings**; the hind wings are reduced to **halteres** for balance.
- Three pairs of hairy legs, each foot with two claws and two sticky, hairy pads (**pulvilli**), which let it walk on walls and ceilings and pick up germs.

**How the housefly feeds and spreads disease:**

1. The fly lands on food and pours **saliva** (and often some of its previous meal) onto it.
2. The saliva dissolves the food.
3. The fly sucks up the liquid with its labella.
4. It often leaves behind **vomit spots** and **faeces** on the food.
5. Because it visits faeces and rubbish, then human food, its hairy legs, body, saliva and droppings carry the germs of **typhoid**, **cholera**, **dysentery** and **diarrhoea**, and the eggs of parasitic worms.

**Life history of the housefly (complete metamorphosis):**

1. **Eggs:** the female lays batches of about 100 to 150 white, banana-shaped eggs in moist, rotting material such as rubbish heaps, animal dung and human faeces. She can lay several hundred eggs in her life.
2. **Larva (maggot):** within about a day, the eggs hatch into white, legless, worm-like **maggots**, pointed at the head end. They feed on the rotting material and moult twice, growing quickly for about 4 to 7 days.
3. **Pupa:** the maggot moves to a drier place and its last larval skin hardens into a dark brown, barrel-shaped case called a **puparium**, in which the pupa develops.
4. **Adult:** after about 3 to 6 days, the adult fly pushes open the end of the puparium and crawls out.

In warm weather, the whole cycle can take as little as **7 to 10 days**, which is why flies multiply so fast.

[IMAGE: Circular life cycle of the housefly with arrows: a cluster of white banana-shaped eggs on rotting material → white, legless, tapering maggot → dark brown barrel-shaped pupa case (puparium) → adult housefly with one pair of wings and hairy legs. Flat textbook style, soft greys, creams and browns, white background, no text.]

**Control of houseflies:**

- Cover food and drinking water.
- Dispose of refuse properly: cover dustbins, burn or bury rubbish.
- Use proper toilets and keep them clean; keep animal pens clean.
- Spray insecticides on breeding sites; use fly traps and fly-proof screens.

#### The honey bee

The honey bee (*Apis mellifera*) is a **social insect** that lives in large colonies in hives, hollow trees or rock crevices. The African honey bee is common across Nigeria and is kept by bee farmers for honey. (The castes of social insects are covered in detail in Topic A3.)

**External features of a worker bee:**

- A hairy body, about 1 to 1.5 cm long, with dark brown and golden-yellow bands on the abdomen.
- **Head:** two large compound eyes, three simple eyes (ocelli) on top, a pair of elbowed antennae, and **chewing and lapping mouthparts**: strong mandibles for handling wax, and a long, hairy tongue (proboscis) for sucking nectar.
- **Thorax:** two pairs of thin, clear wings, hooked together in flight. Three pairs of legs: the hind legs have a **pollen basket** (a hollow fringed with stiff hairs) for carrying pollen; the front legs have an **antenna cleaner**.
- **Abdomen:** wax glands underneath, and a barbed **sting** at the tip, connected to a poison sac. The sting is a modified egg-laying tube, so only females (workers and queen) can sting.

[IMAGE: An adult honey bee worker in side view, showing its hairy body with dark and golden bands on the abdomen, large compound eyes, elbowed antennae, a long tongue, two pairs of clear wings, three pairs of legs with a yellow pollen load in the pollen basket on the hind leg, and the sting at the end of the abdomen. Flat textbook style, warm golds, browns and soft blues, white background, no text.]

**Life history of the honey bee (complete metamorphosis):**

1. **Eggs:** the queen lays one egg in each six-sided wax cell of the honeycomb. **Fertilised** eggs become females (workers or queens); **unfertilised** eggs become males (**drones**).
2. **Larva:** after 3 days, a white, legless, curled larva hatches. All larvae are fed **royal jelly** (a protein-rich food made by young workers) for about the first 3 days. After that, worker and drone larvae are fed **"bee bread"** (pollen and honey). Larvae chosen to become **queens** are kept on royal jelly throughout, in special large cells.
3. **Pupa:** after about 5 to 6 days, the workers cap the cell with wax, and the larva spins a cocoon and becomes a pupa.
4. **Adult:** the adult bee bites its way out of the cell.

Development times differ for each caste: about **16 days** for a queen, **21 days** for a worker and **24 days** for a drone.

#### The butterfly

Butterflies are common in gardens, farms and forests, especially in the rainy season. A well-known example in Nigeria is the citrus swallowtail (*Papilio demodocus*), whose caterpillars feed on the leaves of orange and lemon trees.

**External features of an adult butterfly:**

- A slender body with head, thorax and abdomen.
- **Clubbed antennae** (thin, with a knob at the end).
- Large compound eyes.
- **Siphoning mouthparts**: a long, coiled, tube-like **proboscis**, which uncoils to suck nectar from flowers, and is rolled up like a watch spring when not in use.
- **Two pairs of large, brightly coloured wings** covered with tiny, overlapping **scales** (which rub off like dust). At rest, the wings are usually held upright over the back.
- Three pairs of legs.

**Life history of the butterfly (complete metamorphosis):**

1. **Eggs:** the female lays tiny eggs, singly or in small groups, on the leaves of the plant the caterpillars will eat (the food plant).
2. **Larva (caterpillar):** after a few days, a caterpillar hatches. It has **biting and chewing mouthparts** and eats leaves all the time, growing very fast. It has three pairs of true, jointed legs on the thorax, and several pairs of fleshy, unjointed **prolegs** (false legs) on the abdomen. It moults about four or five times.
3. **Pupa (chrysalis):** the fully grown caterpillar attaches itself to a twig or leaf with a pad of silk and changes into a **chrysalis**. It does not feed or move much; inside, the body is completely reorganised into the adult.
4. **Adult (imago):** after one to a few weeks, the chrysalis splits and the adult butterfly crawls out. It pumps blood into its crumpled wings to expand them, waits for them to dry and harden, then flies off to feed on nectar and mate.

Flat textbook style, soft greens, oranges and yellows, white background, no text.]

### Mollusca: snails

Molluscs are soft-bodied animals, most of which are protected by a hard **shell**. They include snails, slugs, oysters, mussels, periwinkles, octopuses and squids.

**Characteristics of molluscs:**

1. A **soft**, **unsegmented** body.
2. The body has three main parts: a **head**, a large muscular **foot** for movement, and a **visceral mass** containing the organs.
3. A fold of skin called the **mantle** covers the visceral mass and secretes the **shell** (made of calcium carbonate).
4. **Bilaterally symmetrical** (though in snails the body is twisted and coiled), **triploblastic** and with a reduced coelom.
5. Most have a rasping, tongue-like ribbon covered with tiny teeth, called the **radula**, for feeding.

#### The giant African land snail

Giant African land snails, such as *Archachatina marginata* and *Achatina achatina*, are common in the forests and farmlands of southern Nigeria. They are collected and farmed (**snail farming**, or heliciculture) as a popular, protein-rich food.

**External features:**

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shell | Large, hard, spirally coiled shell, often brown with darker stripes, with an opening (aperture) | Protects the soft body from enemies and drying out; the snail can withdraw completely into it |
| Head | At the front, with the mouth underneath | Feeding and sensing |
| Tentacles | Two pairs on the head: a longer upper pair with **eyes** at their tips, and a shorter lower pair for touch and smell | Sensing light, touch and chemicals; can be pulled in when touched |
| Foot | Broad, flat, muscular sole on the underside | Movement by waves of muscle contraction, gliding over a layer of slime |
| Mucus (slime) glands | In the foot and skin | Produce slime that helps the snail glide, protects the foot and stops it drying out |
| Breathing pore (pneumostome) | An opening on the right side, under the edge of the shell | Lets air into the **mantle cavity**, which acts as a **lung** |
| Radula | Inside the mouth | Rasps (scrapes) leaves, fruits and other plant matter |

Flat textbook style, soft browns and creams, white background, no text.]

**Life history of the land snail:**

1. Land snails are **hermaphrodite** (each has both male and female organs) but usually **cross-fertilise**: two snails mate and exchange sperm.
2. Each snail then lays its fertilised **eggs** (giant African land snails lay batches of up to a few hundred small, hard-shelled, yellowish eggs) in a hole it digs in moist soil.
3. After a few weeks, tiny **young snails** with soft, thin shells hatch out. They look like small adults; there is **no larval stage**.
4. They feed, grow and add to their shells over time, and become mature in about one to two years.

**Aestivation:** in the dry season, land snails bury themselves in soil or leaf litter, withdraw into their shells and seal the opening with a dried layer of mucus (an **epiphragm**). They stay inactive until the rains return. (Aestivation is covered further in Topic A3.)

**Importance of snails:**

- **Food:** snail meat is rich in protein and iron, and low in fat. Snail farming provides income.
- **Shells:** used for ornaments, and crushed as a source of calcium in livestock feed.
- **Crop pests:** snails eat the leaves and fruits of crops and vegetables.
- **Disease:** some **freshwater** snails are **intermediate hosts** of parasites. *Bulinus* and *Biomphalaria* carry the blood fluke *Schistosoma*, which causes **bilharzia** (schistosomiasis); *Lymnaea* carries the liver fluke of sheep and cattle.

### Advancement of the invertebrates

**Objective (Animalia) i: trace the advancement of the invertebrate animals.**

| Feature | Coelenterata (*Hydra*) | Platyhelminthes (*Taenia*) | Nematoda (*Ascaris*) | Annelida (earthworm) | Arthropoda (insects) | Mollusca (snail) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Symmetry | Radial | Bilateral | Bilateral | Bilateral | Bilateral | Bilateral (body coiled) |
| Germ layers | Diploblastic | Triploblastic | Triploblastic | Triploblastic | Triploblastic | Triploblastic |
| Body cavity | Enteron only (no coelom) | None (acoelomate) | Pseudocoelom | True coelom | Haemocoel (reduced coelom) | Reduced coelom |

| Blood system | None | None | None | Closed | Open | Open |
| Limbs | Tentacles | None | None | Chaetae, no limbs | Jointed legs and wings | Muscular foot |

**The main lines of advancement:**

1. **Radial to bilateral symmetry**, which allows a definite front end that meets new surroundings first.
2. **Diploblastic to triploblastic**: the mesoderm allows muscles and complex organs.
3. **No body cavity → false cavity → true coelom**: a coelom lets the gut and body wall move separately and leaves room for organs.
4. **One-way gut** with mouth and anus, allowing food to be processed in stages.
5. **Segmentation** (annelids and arthropods), and grouping of segments into regions with special jobs (head, thorax, abdomen).
6. **Cephalisation**: a head with a brain and sense organs.
7. **Skeletons** for support and movement: hydrostatic skeleton → hard exoskeleton.
8. **Jointed limbs and wings** in arthropods, which, with the waterproof exoskeleton, made insects the most successful land invertebrates.

**Textbook variation:** the exact order in which the phyla are placed differs a little between textbooks, especially for the molluscs, which some place before the annelids and others after the arthropods. Molluscs and arthropods are both advanced groups, and they evolved along separate lines.

## Vertebrates

All vertebrates belong to the phylum **Chordata**. At some stage of their life, chordates have:

1. A **notochord**: a flexible rod along the back that supports the body. In vertebrates, it is replaced by the **vertebral column** (backbone) in the adult.
2. A **hollow nerve cord** on the upper (dorsal) side, which forms the spinal cord and brain.
3. **Gill slits** (pharyngeal slits) in the throat region, at least in the embryo.
4. A **tail** that extends beyond the anus (post-anal tail), at least in the embryo.

**General characteristics of vertebrates:**

1. A **vertebral column** (backbone) made of small bones (vertebrae), surrounding and protecting the spinal cord.
2. A **skull** (cranium) protecting a well-developed brain.
3. An internal skeleton (**endoskeleton**) of bone or cartilage, which grows with the animal, so there is no need to moult.
4. Usually two pairs of limbs (fins, legs or wings).
5. A **closed blood system**, with a muscular heart on the lower (ventral) side, and red blood cells containing haemoglobin.
6. A bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic body with a true coelom.

### Pisces (fish)

Fish are vertebrates that live in water, breathe with **gills**, and move with **fins**. They are **cold-blooded** (poikilothermic or ectothermic): their body temperature changes with the temperature of the water. The syllabus divides them into **cartilaginous** and **bony** fish.

#### External features of a bony fish (e.g. *Tilapia*)

*Tilapia* is one of the most common freshwater fish in Nigeria. It is widely farmed in fish ponds, along with the African catfish (*Clarias*).

#### Life history of a bony fish

1. Most bony fish reproduce in water by **external fertilisation**. The female releases large numbers of eggs (spawn), and the male sheds sperm (milt) over them.
2. The fertilised eggs develop in the water. Many eggs are eaten, so fish lay thousands to make sure some survive.
3. The eggs hatch into tiny **fry** (larvae), which at first feed on the yolk in a yolk sac attached to their belly.
4. The fry grow into **fingerlings** and then into adults.

**Example:** many *Tilapia* species show **parental care**. In some, the female keeps the fertilised eggs, and later the young fry, in her mouth to protect them (**mouth brooding**).

### Amphibia (toads and frogs)

Amphibians ("double life") live part of their life in water and part on land. They include toads, frogs, newts and salamanders. In Nigeria, the common toad (*Sclerophrys*, formerly *Bufo*, e.g. *Bufo regularis*) is seen around houses at night, especially in the rainy season, and many species of frog live in and around ponds, swamps and streams.

**Characteristics of amphibians:**

1. A moist, smooth or warty skin with **no scales**, kept moist by mucus glands. The skin is used for gas exchange.
2. Two pairs of **pentadactyl** (five-fingered) limbs: the forelimbs have four fingers, and the longer hind limbs have five toes, often **webbed**.
3. Adults breathe with **lungs**, through the **skin**, and through the lining of the mouth (buccal cavity). Tadpoles breathe with **gills**.
4. A **three-chambered heart** (two atria and one ventricle).
5. **Cold-blooded** (poikilothermic).
6. Eyes with eyelids, and a **tympanum** (eardrum) on the surface behind each eye.
7. Most return to water to breed. Fertilisation is usually **external**, and the eggs have no shell, only a coat of jelly.
8. The young (**tadpoles**) live in water and go through **metamorphosis** to become adults.

#### Toads and frogs compared

| Feature | Toad | Frog |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skin | Dry, rough and **warty** | Moist, smooth and slimy |
| Poison glands | Large **parotid glands** behind the eyes, which release a bitter, poisonous fluid | No large parotid glands |
| Hind legs | Short; they **walk or hop** | Long and powerful; they **leap** |
| Webbing of toes | Little webbing | Toes fully webbed (for swimming) |
| Habitat | Mainly on land, returning to water only to breed | In or near water |
| Eggs (spawn) | Laid in long **strings** of jelly, wound around water plants | Laid in **clumps** (masses) of jelly |
| Body shape | Broad and squat | Slender and streamlined |

#### Life history of a toad or frog

1. **Breeding:** in the rainy season, males gather at ponds and croak to attract females. The male climbs onto the female's back and holds her tightly with his forelimbs (**amplexus**).
2. **Spawning and fertilisation:** the female lays her eggs in the water, and the male immediately sheds sperm over them (**external fertilisation**). Each egg is surrounded by a coat of **jelly**, which swells in water, protects the egg, stops it being eaten, and keeps it warm.
3. **Embryo:** the black part of each egg develops into a comma-shaped embryo inside the jelly.
4. **Tadpole with external gills:** after a few days to a week, the tadpole hatches. At first it clings to water plants with a sucker and lives on its yolk. It breathes with feathery **external gills**.
5. **Internal gills:** a fold of skin (the **operculum**) grows over the gills, which are now **internal**. The tadpole swims with its long tail, and feeds on algae and water plants.
6. **Hind legs appear:** small hind legs grow at the base of the tail. Lungs begin to develop, and the tadpole begins to come to the surface to gulp air.
7. **Forelegs appear:** the forelegs break through the skin (the left one through the opening of the operculum). The mouth widens, and the tadpole stops feeding as its gut changes from a plant-eating to an insect-eating type.
8. **Tail absorbed:** the tail gets shorter as it is absorbed into the body for food. The gills disappear and the lungs take over.
9. **Young toad or frog:** the young adult (toadlet or froglet), with no tail, leaves the water for land, feeds on insects and grows into an adult.

The whole process from egg to young adult takes several weeks to a few months, depending on the species and the temperature.

### Reptilia (lizards, snakes and turtles)

Reptiles were the first vertebrates to become **fully adapted to life on land**. They include lizards, snakes, turtles, tortoises and crocodiles.

**Characteristics of reptiles:**

1. A **dry skin** covered with horny **scales** (made of keratin) or, in turtles, bony plates (scutes). The scales **prevent water loss**.
2. They breathe only with **lungs**, even those that live in water (turtles and crocodiles come up for air).
3. A heart with **three chambers** (two atria and a partly divided ventricle). Crocodiles have a four-chambered heart.
4. **Cold-blooded** (poikilothermic): they control their body temperature by their behaviour, such as **basking** in the sun and hiding in shade (see Topic A3).
5. **Internal fertilisation**: the male places sperm inside the female, so no outside water is needed.
6. They lay **eggs with a leathery or hard shell** on land. Inside, the egg has membranes (the **amnion** and others) and a fluid that protects the embryo from drying out. This **amniotic (land) egg** is a key step in the move to land.
7. The young hatch as small adults; there is **no larval stage**.
8. Limbs, where present, have **claws**.

#### Examples

**Lizards: the Agama lizard (*Agama agama*)**

The rainbow lizard or Agama is one of the most familiar animals in Nigeria, seen on walls, rocks and trees. It eats insects.

- A long, slender body covered with small scales, with a long tail.
- Two pairs of limbs with five clawed toes each, used for running and climbing.
- Eyelids that move, and a visible **ear opening** (tympanum) behind the eye.
- The breeding male has a bright **orange-red head and tail** and a dark blue body; the female and young males are a dull brown, with spots on the sides. (Their behaviour is covered in Topic A3.)
- **Life history:** after internal fertilisation, the female digs a hole in moist, sandy soil with her snout and lays about 5 to 7 oval, leathery-shelled eggs. She covers them and leaves them; the sun's warmth incubates them. The young hatch after several weeks and look like small, brown adults.

[IMAGE: A male Agama lizard on a rock with a bright orange-red head and the front of the tail, dark blue-black body, long tail, four limbs with clawed toes and visible ear opening; beside it a smaller brown female Agama with lighter spots on the sides. Flat textbook style, warm oranges, blues and browns, white background, no text.]

**Snakes**

- A long body with **no limbs**, covered with scales; broad scales on the belly help them grip the ground as they move.
- **No eyelids** (the eyes are covered by a clear scale) and **no external ear openings**.
- A forked **tongue** that is flicked in and out to "taste" the air (smell).
- Very flexible jaws that open wide to swallow prey whole.
- They shed (**slough**) their whole skin as they grow.
- Some are **venomous**, with hollow or grooved **fangs**, e.g. the carpet viper (*Echis ocellatus*), which causes most snakebites in northern Nigeria, the puff adder and the spitting cobra. Others, like the python, kill by squeezing (constriction).
- Most lay eggs; some (e.g. the puff adder) keep the eggs inside the body until they hatch and give birth to live young.

**Turtles and tortoises**

- The body is enclosed in a hard **shell** made of bony plates covered with horny scutes: an upper **carapace** and a lower **plastron**. Head, legs and tail can be drawn into the shell for protection (in many species).
- **No teeth**; they have a horny **beak**.
- **Turtles** live mainly in water and have flipper-like or webbed limbs. Sea turtles come ashore on beaches (such as those in the Niger Delta) to lay their eggs in the sand. **Tortoises** live on land and have stumpy, elephant-like legs.

**Textbook variation:** in British English, "turtle" means a water-living species and "tortoise" a land-living one; in American English, "turtle" is used for all of them.

### Aves (birds)

Birds are vertebrates adapted for **flight**. Examples in Nigeria include the domestic fowl (chicken), pigeon, weaver bird, kite, vulture, cattle egret, owl and hornbill.

**Characteristics of birds:**

1. The body is covered with **feathers** (made of keratin), which keep the body warm and are used for flight. The legs and feet are covered with **scales**.
2. The forelimbs are modified as **wings**.
3. A horny **beak** and **no teeth**.
4. **Warm-blooded** (homoiothermic or endothermic): they keep a constant, high body temperature.
5. A **four-chambered heart**, which keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood completely separate.
6. Light, **hollow (pneumatic) bones** containing air spaces, which reduce weight for flight.
7. Lungs connected to a system of **air sacs**, giving a very efficient one-way flow of air through the lungs.
8. A large, keel-shaped breastbone (**sternum**) to which the powerful flight muscles are attached.
9. **Internal fertilisation**; they lay **eggs with hard, chalky (calcareous) shells**, which they incubate (keep warm) with their body heat.
10. Well-developed **parental care**: building nests, incubating eggs and feeding and protecting the young.

**Life history of a bird (e.g. the domestic fowl):**

1. After mating and internal fertilisation, the hen lays a hard-shelled egg containing the embryo, a large **yolk** (food), **albumen** (egg white: water and protein), membranes and an air space.
2. The hen sits on the eggs to keep them warm (**incubation**) for about **21 days** in the domestic fowl. She turns them regularly.
3. The chick uses a small, hard point on its beak (the egg tooth) to break out of the shell.
4. Chicks of the domestic fowl can walk and feed themselves soon after hatching, but stay close to the hen for warmth and protection. In many other birds (e.g. pigeons and weaver birds), the young hatch naked and helpless, and are fed in the nest by their parents.

### Mammalia (mammals)

Mammals include humans, goats, cattle, dogs, rats, bats, monkeys, whales and many more.

**Characteristics of mammals:**

1. The body is covered with **hair** (fur).
2. Females have **mammary glands**, which produce **milk** to feed the young.
3. **External ears** (pinnae).
4. A muscular **diaphragm** separates the chest (thorax) from the abdomen, and helps in breathing.
5. **Warm-blooded** (homoiothermic): sweat glands, hair and fat help control body temperature.
6. A **four-chambered heart**; red blood cells have **no nucleus** (in most mammals).
7. **Different types of teeth** (incisors, canines, premolars and molars) for different jobs (**heterodont** dentition).
8. A large, well-developed **brain**.
9. Most are **viviparous**: the young develop inside the mother's uterus, nourished through a **placenta**, and are born alive.
10. A long period of **parental care**.

**Textbook variation and exceptions:** a few mammals do not give birth to live young after a long pregnancy. The **monotremes** (the platypus and the spiny anteaters of Australia and New Guinea) **lay eggs**, then feed their young on milk. The **marsupials** (e.g. kangaroos) give birth to very small, undeveloped young, which complete their development in a pouch, feeding on milk. These animals are still mammals because they have hair and mammary glands.

**Life history of a mammal (e.g. a goat or a human):**

1. After internal fertilisation, the zygote divides to form an embryo, which attaches to the wall of the **uterus**.
2. The **placenta** forms, through which the embryo (later called the fetus) gets food and oxygen from the mother and gets rid of its wastes.
3. The young develops inside the mother for a fixed time, called the **gestation period** (about 9 months in humans; about 5 months in goats).
4. The young is **born alive**.
5. The mother feeds the young on **milk** from her mammary glands and protects it until it can look after itself.

## Advancement of multicellular animals (vertebrates)

**Objective (Animalia) v: trace the advancement of multi-cellular animals.**

The vertebrate classes show a clear story of advancement, from life in water to complete independence on land:

**Pisces → Amphibia → Reptilia → Aves and Mammalia**

| Step | Advance | What it allowed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fish | Backbone, jaws, paired fins, gills | Efficient swimming and breathing in water |
| Fish → Amphibians | Lungs, pentadactyl legs, eyelids, eardrum, three-chambered heart | First vertebrates able to walk and breathe air on land |
| Amphibians → Reptiles | Dry scaly skin, internal fertilisation, the shelled **amniotic egg** | Fully independent of water for reproduction; able to live in dry places |
| Reptiles → Birds and Mammals | **Warm-bloodedness**, four-chambered heart, feathers or hair, more parental care, larger brains | Active life in all climates, including at night and in cold places; better survival of young |

**Main trends in vertebrate advancement:**

1. **Breathing:** gills → lungs and skin → lungs only → lungs with air sacs (birds) or a diaphragm (mammals).
2. **Heart and circulation:** 2 chambers → 3 chambers → 4 chambers, giving complete separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
3. **Body temperature:** cold-blooded → warm-blooded.
4. **Skin:** moist and permeable → dry and scaly → feathers or hair, reducing water loss and heat loss.
5. **Reproduction:** external fertilisation in water → internal fertilisation; eggs in water → shelled eggs on land → young developing inside the mother.
6. **Number of eggs:** very many (fish, amphibians) → few (birds) → very few young (mammals), with more **parental care** to make up for fewer offspring.
7. **Brain:** becomes larger and more complex, especially the cerebrum.

**Textbook note:** birds and mammals both evolved from reptile ancestors, along separate lines. Mammals did not evolve from birds. The order fish → amphibians → reptiles → birds/mammals reflects increasing complexity and the order in which the groups appear in the fossil record.

## From life in water to life on land: the animal story

**Objective iv (Topic 2): demonstrate gradual transition from life in water to life on land.**

The life histories above show how animals became less and less dependent on water:

1. ***Hydra* and fish** live and reproduce entirely in water. Their gametes and young need water.
2. **Amphibians** live on land as adults, but return to water to breed: their eggs have no shell, fertilisation is external, and the tadpoles have gills. Their moist skin also limits them to damp places.
3. **Insects** solved the problem of life on land with a waterproof exoskeleton, tracheae for breathing air, and internal fertilisation; but mosquitoes still lay their eggs in water, and their larvae and pupae live there.
4. **Reptiles** became fully land animals: dry scaly skin, internal fertilisation, and the **shelled amniotic egg** that can be laid on dry land.
5. **Birds and mammals** added warm-bloodedness, insulating feathers or hair, and parental care. In most mammals the embryo develops inside the mother, in a watery fluid of its own (the amniotic fluid), so it never needs an outside pond.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- ***Hydra* is radially symmetrical and diploblastic**; all the other animals here are bilateral and triploblastic.
- **Tapeworms have no gut**: they absorb digested food through their body surface. Roundworms are the first group here with a complete gut (mouth and anus).
- **Hosts of *Taenia*:** humans are the primary (definitive) host; the pig (*T. solium*) or cow (*T. saginata*) is the intermediate host.
- **Insects:** 3 body parts, 3 pairs of legs, 1 pair of antennae. Spiders have 4 pairs of legs and are **not** insects.
- **Complete vs incomplete metamorphosis:** cockroaches (and grasshoppers, termites) have nymphs; mosquitoes, houseflies, bees and butterflies have larvae and pupae.
- **Mouthparts:** mosquito (piercing and sucking), housefly (sponging), cockroach (biting and chewing), butterfly (siphoning).
- **Only female mosquitoes suck blood.** *Anopheles* larvae lie parallel to the water surface; *Culex* larvae hang at an angle.
- **Earthworms are hermaphrodite but cross-fertilise.** The clitellum makes the cocoon.
- **Heart chambers:** fish 2, amphibians 3, reptiles 3 (crocodiles 4), birds 4, mammals 4.
- **Reptiles were the first vertebrates fully independent of water**, because of their shelled eggs and internal fertilisation.
- **Birds and mammals are both warm-blooded**; fish, amphibians and reptiles are cold-blooded.

## Quick summary

1. Animal advancement is traced by symmetry, germ layers, body cavity, gut, segmentation, head development, skeleton and organ systems.
2. Invertebrates progress from *Hydra* (radial, diploblastic, one gut opening) through flatworms (*Taenia*, bilateral, acoelomate), roundworms (pseudocoelom, complete gut) and annelids (segmented, true coelom) to arthropods and molluscs.
3. *Taenia* is a gutless parasite with hooks, suckers and proglottids; its life cycle passes from human (primary host) to pig or cow (intermediate host) and back through undercooked meat.
4. Arthropods have jointed limbs and a chitin exoskeleton; insects have 3 body parts, 3 pairs of legs and 1 pair of antennae, and show complete (mosquito, housefly, bee, butterfly) or incomplete (cockroach) metamorphosis.
5. Insects are useful (pollination, honey, wax, silk, food, decomposition) and harmful (vectors of malaria, yellow fever, typhoid and cholera; crop and storage pests).
6. Vertebrates advance from fish (gills, 2-chambered heart) to amphibians (double life, tadpoles), reptiles (scales, shelled eggs on land), and birds and mammals (warm-blooded, 4-chambered heart, parental care).
7. The move from water to land in animals depended on lungs, waterproof skin, internal fertilisation and the shelled egg.
8. Vertebrates are economically important as food, sources of leather and other products, and pest controllers, but some are pests or spread diseases.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following animals is diploblastic?**

- A. Earthworm
- B. *Taenia*
- C. *Hydra*
- D. Snail

**Answer: C.** *Hydra*'s body wall has only two cell layers (ectoderm and endoderm), with non-cellular mesoglea between them. All the others are triploblastic.

**2. The intermediate host of the pork tapeworm is the**

- A. human
- B. pig
- C. cow
- D. dog

**Answer: B.** The bladder worm (cysticercus) of *Taenia solium* develops in pig muscle; humans are the primary host.

**3. Which feature is an adaptation of the tapeworm to its parasitic life?**

- A. A well-developed gut
- B. Hooks and suckers on the scolex
- C. Large compound eyes
- D. Jointed legs

**Answer: B.** Hooks and suckers keep the tapeworm attached to the intestine wall. Tapeworms have no gut and no eyes.

**4. The saddle-like clitellum of the earthworm is used for**

- A. movement
- B. gas exchange
- C. forming the cocoon
- D. feeding

**Answer: C.** The clitellum secretes mucus during mating and forms the cocoon in which the eggs are fertilised.

**5. Which of the following insects undergoes incomplete metamorphosis?**

- A. Housefly
- B. Cockroach
- C. Mosquito
- D. Butterfly

**Answer: B.** The cockroach has three stages: egg, nymph and adult. The others have larva and pupa stages.

**6. The larva of Anopheles mosquito can be distinguished from that of Culex because it**

- A. has a long breathing siphon
- B. lies parallel to the water surface
- C. hangs at an angle to the water surface
- D. lives on land

**Answer: B.** *Anopheles* larvae have no siphon and lie parallel to the surface; *Culex* larvae hang at an angle using a siphon.

**7. The mouthparts of the housefly are adapted for**

- A. piercing and sucking
- B. biting and chewing
- C. sponging (lapping)
- D. siphoning

**Answer: C.** The housefly pours saliva onto food and sponges up the liquid with its labella.

**8. Which of the following is a characteristic of all reptiles?**

- A. Feathers
- B. Moist skin without scales
- C. Dry skin with horny scales
- D. Mammary glands

**Answer: C.** Reptiles have dry, scaly skin that reduces water loss on land.

**9. The vertebrates that were the first to become fully independent of water for reproduction are the**

- A. fishes
- B. amphibians
- C. reptiles
- D. mammals

**Answer: C.** Reptiles have internal fertilisation and lay shelled (amniotic) eggs on land, so they need no water to breed.

**10. Which of the following pairs of animals are warm-blooded?**

- A. Toad and lizard
- B. Fish and frog
- C. Bird and goat
- D. Snake and tortoise

**Answer: C.** Birds and mammals are warm-blooded (homoiothermic). Fish, amphibians and reptiles are cold-blooded.
