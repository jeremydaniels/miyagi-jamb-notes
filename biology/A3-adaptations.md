## What is an adaptation?

**Objective i: describe how the various structures, functions and behaviour adapt these organisms to their environment, and way of life.**

An **adaptation** is any feature of an organism that helps it to survive and reproduce in its environment. Organisms that are well adapted are more likely to find food, escape enemies, find mates and survive harsh conditions, so they leave more offspring. Over many generations, natural selection makes these features more common.

Adaptations are of three kinds:

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Structural (morphological)** | A feature of the body's shape, form or parts | The hooked beak of an eagle; the webbed feet of a duck; the spines of a cactus; the thick fur of a goat |
| **Functional (physiological)** | A feature of how the body works (its chemistry or processes) | Sweating to cool down; a camel producing very concentrated urine to save water; a toad producing poison from its skin glands; the tapeworm's anti-enzymes |
| **Behavioural** | Something the organism does, either inborn (instinct) or learned | A lizard basking in the sun; bees dancing to show where food is; birds migrating; snails aestivating in the dry season |

Many adaptations combine more than one type. For example, the toad's warty, poisonous skin is structural (the glands) and functional (the poison), and its habit of puffing itself up when attacked is behavioural.

**Textbook variation:** some textbooks use "physiological adaptation" instead of "functional adaptation"; they mean the same thing.

## Adaptive colouration and its functions

**Adaptive colouration** is any colour or pattern of an organism that helps it to survive. Colours can hide an animal, warn enemies away, trick them, or help animals recognise and attract mates.

### Types and functions of adaptive colouration

| Type | How it works | Function | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Camouflage (cryptic colouration)** | The animal's colour and pattern match its background | Hides prey from predators, or hides predators from prey | Green grasshoppers on grass; brown toads on soil; the praying mantis on leaves; the leopard's spots in dappled forest light |
| **Countershading** | The upper (dorsal) surface is dark and the lower (ventral) surface is pale | Cancels out the shadow on the underside, so the body looks flat and blends into the background | Fish, toads, many snakes, antelopes |
| **Disruptive colouration** | Bold stripes, spots or patches break up the outline of the body | Makes the shape of the animal hard to recognise | Zebra stripes; the patterned skin of the Gaboon viper |
| **Colour change** | The animal changes its colour to match its surroundings or mood | Camouflage; signalling | Chameleons; the male *Agama* changing colour; some fish and frogs |
| **Warning (aposematic) colouration** | Bright, contrasting colours (red, orange, yellow, black) | Warns predators that the organism is poisonous, distasteful or dangerous, so they learn to avoid it | Brightly coloured poisonous mushrooms; bees and wasps; the variegated grasshopper (*Zonocerus variegatus*); the African monarch butterfly (*Danaus chrysippus*) |
| **Mimicry** | A harmless organism looks like a harmful or distasteful one (Batesian mimicry), or two harmful ones look alike (Müllerian mimicry) | The mimic is avoided by predators that have learned to avoid the model | The female diadem butterfly (*Hypolimnas misippus*) mimics the distasteful African monarch; hoverflies look like wasps |
| **Recognition and courtship colouration** | Bright colours shown by one sex, often only in the breeding season | Attract mates; warn off rivals; help members of a species recognise each other | The red head of the male *Agama*; the bright yellow of the male village weaver bird in the breeding season |

### Countershading in fish, toads and snakes

**Objective ii (part 1): categorise countershading in fish, toads and snakes.**

**What is countershading?** Light usually comes from above. It brightens the top of an animal and leaves its underside in shadow. This light-and-shadow pattern makes a solid, rounded shape stand out. In **countershading**, the animal's **back is dark** and its **belly is pale**. The dark back cancels out the bright light from above, and the pale belly cancels out the shadow underneath. The animal looks evenly coloured and **flat**, so it is harder to see. Countershading is a type of **camouflage** (cryptic colouration).

| Animal | Countershading pattern | How it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fish** (e.g. *Tilapia*, mackerel) | Dark (grey, green or blue) on the back, silvery-white on the belly | Seen **from above** (by a fish-eating bird), the dark back blends with the dark water or muddy bottom. Seen **from below** (by a predatory fish), the pale belly blends with the bright water surface and sky. This protects the fish from predators on both sides |
| **Toads** (e.g. the common toad) | Dull brown, olive or grey with dark blotches on the back; pale cream or white on the belly | The dark, mottled back blends with soil and dead leaves, hiding the toad from snakes and birds above; the flat look helps it stay hidden while it waits for insects |
| **Snakes** (e.g. many grass snakes and the green tree snakes) | Darker, often patterned back; paler, plain belly scales | The darker back blends with the ground, leaves or branches; the pale underside removes the shadow, so the snake is hard to see. This hides it both from its prey (it can ambush them) and from its enemies (such as hawks) |

### Warning colouration in mushrooms

**Objective ii (part 2): categorise warning colouration in mushrooms.**

Some poisonous mushrooms have very **bright, striking colours**, such as scarlet, orange or bright yellow, often with white spots. The best-known example is the **fly agaric** (*Amanita muscaria*), which has a bright red cap with white warty spots. Its poison can cause severe illness, hallucinations and, rarely, death.

**How warning colouration works:**

1. A bright colour is easy to see and remember.
2. An animal that tastes or eats a poisonous, brightly coloured mushroom becomes sick.
3. It learns to link the bright colour with a bad experience.
4. In future, it avoids mushrooms with that colour, so the fungus is not eaten and can produce its spores.

Warning colouration is the **opposite** of camouflage: instead of hiding, the organism advertises itself.

**Textbook variation and important safety note:** most textbooks give brightly coloured mushrooms as examples of warning colouration, as the syllabus does. However, scientists still debate how far mushroom colours really evolved as warnings, and **colour is not a reliable guide to safety**. Some of the deadliest mushrooms, such as the death cap (*Amanita phalloides*) and the destroying angel, are dull greenish or pure white, and some bright mushrooms are edible. Never eat a wild mushroom unless an expert has identified it.

**Other examples of warning colouration in Nigeria:**

- The **variegated grasshopper** (*Zonocerus variegatus*), a common pest of cassava and other crops, is brightly coloured with green, yellow, orange and black. It stores bitter, poisonous chemicals from the plants it eats, and birds learn to avoid it.
- **Bees and wasps** have yellow-and-black bands, which warn of their sting.
- The **African monarch butterfly** (*Danaus chrysippus*) has bright orange wings and is distasteful to birds.

[IMAGE: A group of brightly coloured poisonous mushrooms in a forest floor scene: a fly agaric with a bright red cap covered in white warty spots and a white stalk with a ring, and a smaller bright orange mushroom beside it; next to them for contrast, a dull brown mushroom. Flat textbook style, white background, no text.]

### Categorising the colouration examples

| Organism | Type of colouration | Category | Main function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fish | Countershading | Concealing (cryptic) colouration | Hides from predators above and below |
| Toad | Countershading (with mottled camouflage) | Concealing (cryptic) colouration | Hides from predators and from prey |
| Snake | Countershading | Concealing (cryptic) colouration | Hides from prey (ambush) and from predators |
| Poisonous mushroom (e.g. fly agaric) | Bright warning colours | Advertising (aposematic) colouration | Warns animals not to eat it |

## Behavioural adaptations in social animals

**Social animals** live together in organised groups in which individuals cooperate. The most highly organised are the **social insects**: termites, bees, ants and some wasps. Their colonies show:

- **Division of labour:** different individuals do different jobs.
- **Castes:** groups of individuals that differ in body structure and in their jobs.
- **Cooperation:** individuals work for the good of the whole colony, not for themselves alone.
- **Communication:** individuals pass information by chemicals (**pheromones**), touch, sound and, in bees, dances.
- **Care of the young** by many members of the colony.

A single termite or bee cannot survive alone for long. The colony works like one large "super-organism".

Other social animals include baboons and monkeys (living in troops), lions (in prides), wild dogs, elephants and weaver birds (which nest in large colonies).

### Termites

Termites are social insects that live in large colonies, often of hundreds of thousands to millions of individuals. In Nigeria, *Macrotermes* species build the tall, reddish-brown **termite mounds** (anthills) that are common in the savanna of the middle belt and the north. Other termites live underground or in dead wood. Termites undergo **incomplete metamorphosis** (egg → nymph → adult).

**Note:** termites are often called "white ants", but they are **not ants**. Ants belong to the same order as bees and wasps; termites are more closely related to cockroaches.

#### The castes of termites and their functions

**Objective iii: differentiate various castes in social insects like termites and their functions in their colony.**

| Caste | Appearance | Sex and fertility | Functions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Queen** (primary reproductive) | Very large. Her abdomen swells enormously with eggs (it can be several centimetres long), so she can hardly move. Her wings have fallen off, leaving stumps | Fertile female | Lays eggs: in large mound-building termites, she may lay thousands of eggs every day for many years. She produces pheromones that control the development of the other castes |
| **King** (primary reproductive) | Normal-sized, dark, wingless (wing stumps); lives with the queen | Fertile male | Mates with the queen from time to time throughout her life |
| **Workers** | Small, soft, pale or whitish, **wingless** and usually **blind** | Sterile males and females | The largest caste. They build and repair the nest, collect food (wood, grass, leaves), feed the queen, king, soldiers and young, care for the eggs and nymphs, and in *Macrotermes*, grow fungus gardens for food |
| **Soldiers** | Wingless and blind, with a large, hard, dark **head**; some have huge, sharp **mandibles** (jaws), others have a nozzle-like head that squirts sticky poison | Sterile males and females | Defend the colony against enemies, especially ants. They guard the entrances and come out when the nest is damaged. They cannot feed themselves and are fed by workers |
| **Winged reproductives** (alates, "flying termites") | Dark brown, with two pairs of long, equal-sized wings and working compound eyes | Fertile males and females | Leave the nest in huge swarms, usually after the first heavy rains of the rainy season, to start new colonies |
| **Supplementary (secondary) reproductives** | Like nymphs, with short wing pads or none | Fertile | Replace the king or queen if they die, and help produce eggs in large colonies |

Flat textbook style, soft creams and browns, no text.]

#### How a new termite colony is formed

1. At the start of the rainy season, thousands of **winged reproductives** fly out of the nest at dusk. Many are eaten by birds, lizards, frogs and people (roasted termites are a popular snack in many parts of Nigeria).
2. The survivors land and break off their wings.
3. A female and a male pair up and run along in tandem, looking for a good site.
4. The pair digs a small chamber in the soil and seals themselves in. They mate. They are now the new **queen** and **king**.
5. The queen lays the first eggs. The king and queen feed and care for the first nymphs, which become **workers**.
6. The workers take over all the work of the colony. Later, soldiers are produced, and, after several years, new winged reproductives.

#### The termite mound

A *Macrotermes* mound is a remarkable piece of building:

- It is built by workers from **soil, saliva and droppings**, which set as hard as cement.
- A system of **air channels** and chimneys circulates air through the mound, keeping the nest at a fairly **constant temperature and humidity** and supplying oxygen. The ventilation of termite mounds has inspired the design of some buildings.
- Deep inside is the **royal chamber**, where the queen and king live, guarded and fed by workers.
- Around it are **nursery chambers** for eggs and young, and **fungus gardens** (fungus combs), where workers grow a fungus (*Termitomyces*) on chewed plant material. The fungus breaks down the tough plant material, and the termites eat the fungus. (The large edible *Termitomyces* mushrooms that appear on termite mounds after rain are the fruiting bodies of this fungus; see Topic A2a.)
- Underground **tunnels** lead out to foraging areas.

[IMAGE: A cut-away view of a tall savanna termite mound on red soil: the outer hard clay wall with air channels and chimneys running up through it, the royal chamber near the bottom with a large queen, honeycomb-like fungus gardens in chambers around it, nursery chambers, and tunnels leading out underground. Flat textbook style, warm reddish-browns and creams, white background, no text.]

#### Behavioural adaptations of termites

- **Division of labour** between castes, so each job is done by specialists.
- **Trophallaxis:** members of the colony exchange food and saliva mouth to mouth. This spreads food and pheromones through the colony and helps them recognise nest mates.
- **Pheromones** control the colony: for example, the queen's chemicals stop workers from becoming reproductives; alarm pheromones call soldiers to a breach; trail pheromones lead workers to food.
- **Head-banging alarm:** soldiers bang their heads against the tunnel walls, and the vibrations warn the colony of danger.
- **Nest building and repair:** a damaged mound is quickly sealed by workers while soldiers guard the gap.
- **Swarming** at the start of the rains, when the soil is soft for digging new nests.

### Honey bees

Honey bees (introduced in Topic A2c) also live in organised colonies of tens of thousands of bees (a strong hive may hold about 50,000 or more), with three castes.

| Caste | Number in the colony | Sex and fertility | Origin | Functions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Queen** | One | Fertile female, larger, with a long abdomen | Fertilised egg; larva fed only on royal jelly | Lays all the eggs (up to about 1,500 to 2,000 a day at her peak); her pheromones hold the colony together and stop workers from laying eggs |
| **Drones** | A few hundred, mainly in the breeding season | Fertile males, larger and stouter than workers, with very large eyes; no sting | Unfertilised egg | Mate with a new queen on her mating flight; they do no other work and are driven out of the hive when food is short |
| **Workers** | Thousands (the great majority) | Sterile females, the smallest bees | Fertilised egg; larva fed royal jelly for about 3 days, then pollen and honey | Do all the work of the hive, changing jobs as they get older (see below) |

**Jobs of worker bees according to age (approximately):**

1. **Days 1–3:** clean the cells.
2. **Days 3–12:** feed the larvae (nurse bees), making royal jelly from glands in their heads.
3. **Days 12–18:** produce wax from glands under the abdomen and build the honeycomb; receive nectar from foragers and turn it into honey; fan their wings to cool the hive and evaporate water from the nectar.
4. **Days 18–21:** guard the entrance of the hive.
5. **From about day 21 until death (about 5 to 6 weeks of age in the busy season):** fly out to collect nectar, pollen, water and propolis (plant resin used to seal gaps).

**Textbook variation:** the exact ages for each job vary between textbooks and between colonies, as workers adjust their jobs to the colony's needs.

**Communication by dancing:** a worker that finds a good source of food returns to the hive and performs a dance on the comb. A **round dance** tells other workers that the food is close to the hive. A **waggle dance** (a figure-of-eight with a waggling run in the middle) shows the **direction** of the food in relation to the sun, and the length of the waggling run shows its **distance**. This was discovered by the Austrian scientist **Karl von Frisch**, who received a Nobel Prize in 1973.

### Termite and bee colonies compared

| Feature | Termites | Honey bees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type of metamorphosis | Incomplete (egg → nymph → adult) | Complete (egg → larva → pupa → adult) |
| Reproductives | King and queen live together; the king stays with the queen for life | Queen only; drones die after mating |
| Workers | Sterile males and females, blind and wingless | Sterile females only, with eyes and wings |
| Soldiers | Present as a separate caste | No separate soldier caste; workers guard and sting |
| Food | Wood, grass, leaves; fungus gardens | Nectar (made into honey) and pollen |
| Nest | Mound or underground nest of soil and saliva | Hive or cavity with wax honeycomb |
| Communication | Mainly pheromones, touch and vibrations | Pheromones and dances |

## Basking, territorial behaviour, hibernation and aestivation

**Objective iv: account for basking in lizards, territorial behaviour of other animals, and behaviour under unfavourable conditions (hibernation and aestivation).**

### Basking in lizards

Lizards are **cold-blooded** (poikilothermic, or ectothermic). They cannot produce enough heat inside their bodies to keep warm. Their body temperature depends on their surroundings. To be active, digest food and escape enemies, they need a warm body (for many lizards, about 30–38 °C).

**Basking** is lying in the sun to absorb heat. It is a **behavioural** way of controlling body temperature.

**How the Agama lizard regulates its temperature through the day:**

1. **Early morning:** after a cool night, the lizard is cold and slow. It comes out and **basks** on a sunny wall, rock or tree trunk, often flattening its body and turning its side towards the sun to absorb as much heat as possible. Dark skin colours also help absorb heat.
2. **Mid-morning:** once warm, it becomes active: it hunts insects, chases rivals and displays to females.
3. **Midday (hottest time):** to avoid overheating, it moves into the **shade**, under rocks or into crevices, or raises its body off the hot ground on stretched legs and points its head towards the sun to expose less of its body.
4. **Late afternoon:** it basks again to stay warm and active.
5. **Night:** it shelters in cracks and holes, where it is safer and warmer.

**Why basking is an advantage:** it allows a cold-blooded animal to become active quickly and stay at the best temperature for its muscles and enzymes, without using food energy to make heat. This is why lizards can survive on much less food than a warm-blooded animal of the same size.

### Territorial behaviour

A **territory** is an area that an animal (or a group) defends against other members of the same species, especially other males. **Territorial behaviour** is the set of actions animals use to claim, mark and defend a territory.

**Examples:**

| Animal | Territorial behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| **Male *Agama* lizard** | The dominant, brightly coloured male holds a territory, such as a compound wall, rock or tree, with several females. He perches high to be seen, **bobs his head** and does **"press-ups"** to display his red head and blue body. If a rival male comes close, he raises his body, puffs out his throat, and chases and fights the intruder, often lashing his tail. Young or defeated males stay dull-coloured |
| **Birds** | Males **sing** from perches to announce their territory and warn rivals; they also display and chase intruders |
| **Dogs and other mammals** | Mark the boundaries of their territory with **urine**, droppings or scent from glands; bark or growl at intruders |
| **Fish** (e.g. *Tilapia*) | Males guard a patch of the pond bottom where they dig a nest, and chase other males away |

**Advantages of territorial behaviour:**

1. Ensures that the territory holder has enough **food** for itself and its young.
2. Provides a safe place to **mate**, build nests and raise young.
3. **Spaces out** individuals, so there is less overcrowding, less competition, and less spread of disease.
4. Females are attracted to males with good territories, so the strongest males breed (helping natural selection).
5. Most disputes are settled by **display** rather than serious fighting, so fewer animals are injured.

### Behaviour under unfavourable conditions: hibernation and aestivation

When conditions become too harsh (too cold, too hot, or too dry) or food is scarce, some animals become **dormant**. They become inactive, and their **metabolism** (body processes such as heartbeat, breathing and respiration) slows down greatly, so they use very little energy and can survive on stored food.

#### Hibernation

**Hibernation** is a state of dormancy that animals enter during the **cold season (winter)**.

- It is found mainly in **temperate** and cold countries, where winters are very cold and food is scarce.
- During hibernation, the animal's body temperature falls close to that of its surroundings, and its heartbeat and breathing rate drop to a very low level.
- The animal lives on **fat** stored in its body during the warmer months.
- **Examples:** hedgehogs, dormice, ground squirrels and many bats (in temperate regions), as well as cold-blooded animals such as frogs, toads, lizards, snakes and many insects in cold climates.

**Textbook variation:** some textbooks give the bear as an example of hibernation. Bears do sleep through winter, but their body temperature falls only a few degrees, so many scientists call their winter rest **torpor** rather than true hibernation.

#### Aestivation

**Aestivation** (also spelled estivation) is a state of dormancy that animals enter during the **hot, dry season**.

- It is very common in **tropical** countries such as Nigeria, where the long **dry season** (with the harmattan in the north) brings heat, drought and scarce food.
- The animal reduces water loss and saves energy by staying inactive, often buried in mud or soil, until the rains return.

**Examples of aestivation:**

| Animal | How it aestivates |
| --- | --- |
| **African lungfish** (*Protopterus*) | Found in rivers and swamps in Africa, including the Lake Chad basin and other Nigerian waters. When its pond dries up, it burrows into the mud, coils up and secretes a layer of **mucus** that hardens into a **cocoon**, leaving only a small opening near the mouth. It breathes air using its lungs and lives on its own body tissues, for months or even years, until the rains refill the pond |
| **Land snails** | Bury themselves in soil or leaf litter, withdraw into their shells, and seal the opening with a dried sheet of mucus (an **epiphragm**), which reduces water loss |
| **Earthworms** | Burrow deep into moist soil, coil into a knot inside a small mucus-lined chamber, and stay inactive |
| **Some frogs and toads** | Bury themselves in moist soil, some forming a protective cocoon from shed skin |
| **Some crocodiles and tortoises** | Shelter in burrows or under mud during droughts |

#### Hibernation and aestivation compared

| Feature | Hibernation | Aestivation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Season | Cold season (winter) | Hot, dry season (or drought) |
| Main danger avoided | Cold and lack of food | Heat, drying out and lack of food |
| Where common | Temperate and cold regions | Tropical and desert regions, including Nigeria |
| Body processes | Greatly slowed; body temperature falls | Greatly slowed |
| Examples | Hedgehog, dormouse, temperate bats, frogs in cold climates | Lungfish, land snails, earthworms, some frogs |

Both are **behavioural and physiological adaptations** that allow animals to survive periods when they could not otherwise find enough food or water.

## Structural adaptations in organisms

**Objective v: account for adaptation in organisms with respect to obtaining food, protection and defence, securing mates, regulating body temperature and conserving water.**

### 1. Obtaining food

#### Beaks of birds

Birds have no teeth. The shape of the beak is closely adapted to the kind of food the bird eats.

| Type of beak | Shape | Food and how it is used | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Seed-cracking** | Short, thick, strong and cone-shaped | Cracks hard seeds and grains | Weaver bird, finch, sparrow, bishop bird |
| **Flesh-tearing** | Strong, sharp and **hooked** at the tip | Tears the flesh of prey into pieces | Eagle, hawk, kite, owl, vulture |
| **Nectar-sucking** | Long, thin, slender and often curved | Probes deep into flowers to suck nectar | Sunbird |
| **Mud-sieving (filter-feeding)** | Broad, flat, with comb-like edges (lamellae) | Scoops up mud and water and strains out small plants and animals | Duck |
| **Fish-catching (spearing)** | Long, straight and pointed like a dagger | Catches or spears fish and frogs | Kingfisher, heron, egret |
| **Wood-chiselling** | Strong, straight and chisel-like | Drills holes in tree bark to find insects | Woodpecker |
| **Pecking (general-purpose)** | Short, pointed and slightly curved | Picks up grains and small insects from the ground | Domestic fowl, pigeon |
| **Fruit-cracking** | Large, strong and curved, with a sharp hook | Cracks nuts and hard fruits | Parrot, hornbill |
| **Insect-catching** | Short and flat with a very wide gape, often with bristles at the sides | Catches insects in flight | Swallow, swift, nightjar |

#### Mouthparts of insects

Insects have mouthparts adapted to their food. The basic parts are an upper lip (**labrum**), a pair of jaws (**mandibles**), a pair of **maxillae**, a lower lip (**labium**) and a tongue-like **hypopharynx**. In different insects, these are changed in shape for biting, piercing, sucking or sponging.

**The mosquito: piercing and sucking mouthparts**

The mosquito's mouthparts form a long **proboscis**.

1. The outer covering is the **labium**, a flexible, grooved sheath. It does **not** enter the skin.
2. Inside the labium lie **six sharp, needle-like stylets** (together called the fascicle): the **labrum**, two **mandibles**, two **maxillae** and the **hypopharynx**.
3. When the female feeds, the labium bends back like an elbow, and the stylets **pierce the skin**. The toothed tips of the maxillae saw through the skin, and the stylets probe to find a small blood vessel.
4. **Saliva** is pumped down a narrow channel in the **hypopharynx**. It contains an **anticoagulant**, which stops the blood clotting. In an infected *Anopheles*, the malaria parasite enters the person's blood with this saliva.
5. Blood is **sucked up** through a channel formed by the **labrum**.

In the male, the mandibles and maxillae are small or poorly developed, so he cannot pierce skin. He feeds on nectar and plant juices.

**The butterfly and the moth: siphoning mouthparts**

1. Adult butterflies and most moths feed on liquids, mainly **nectar** from flowers, and also juice from rotting fruits.
2. The two **maxillae** are greatly lengthened and locked together to form a long, hollow tube called the **proboscis**. The mandibles are absent or tiny.
3. When not in use, the proboscis is **coiled** up like a watch spring under the head.
4. When feeding, blood pressure and muscles **uncoil** it, and the tip is pushed deep into the flower.
5. Nectar is **sucked up** by a muscular pump in the head.
6. The length of the proboscis matches the depth of the flowers the insect feeds on. Some hawkmoths have a proboscis longer than their whole body, which lets them reach nectar in long, tube-shaped flowers. While feeding, they carry pollen from flower to flower (**pollination**).

**Note:** the **caterpillars** (larvae) of butterflies and moths have **biting and chewing** mouthparts, which they use to eat leaves. The **adults of some moths**, such as the silk moth, do not feed at all; they live only a few days on food stored by the caterpillar.

### 2. Protection and defence

Animals protect themselves from predators by hiding (camouflage), by looking like something else (mimicry), by being poisonous or distasteful, by having hard coverings or weapons, or by their behaviour.

#### Stick insects

Stick insects are long, thin, slow-moving, plant-eating insects that live on shrubs and trees.

- **Structural adaptation:** the body and legs are **long, thin and twig-like**, often brown or green with bumps and lines like bark. Some have leaf-like flaps. This is a kind of camouflage called **mimicry of objects** (looking like a twig).
- **Behavioural adaptation:** by day, they stay **perfectly still** for hours, often with the front legs stretched forward, so they look like part of the plant. When the wind blows, some **sway gently** like a twig moving in the breeze. They feed mainly at night.
- **Other defences:** if disturbed, some drop to the ground and **pretend to be dead**; some can shed a leg caught by a predator (and may regrow it at the next moult); some have spines or spray bad-smelling chemicals.

#### The praying mantis

The praying mantis is a predatory insect. It gets its name from the way it holds its front legs folded up, as if praying.

- **Camouflage:** its body is **green or brown** and often shaped like a leaf, stem or flower, so it is hidden among plants. Some tropical mantises look exactly like flowers. This hides it **both from its prey** (so it can catch them) **and from its predators**, such as birds and lizards. When camouflage helps a predator get close to its prey, it is sometimes called **aggressive camouflage**.
- **Raptorial forelegs:** its front legs are long and armed with sharp **spines**. They shoot out very quickly to grab and hold insect prey.
- **Head and eyes:** a triangular head that can turn to look over its shoulder, with large compound eyes that judge distance well.
- **Behaviour:** it waits motionless, sometimes swaying gently like a leaf, until prey comes within reach (**ambush hunting**).
- **Defence display:** if threatened, some mantises suddenly spread their wings and raise their forelegs to look bigger, showing bright colours or eyespots to startle the enemy (a **startle** or **deimatic** display).

#### The toad

The toad has several adaptations for protection:

1. **Camouflage and countershading:** its dull brown or grey, warty, mottled back blends with the soil and dead leaves.
2. **Poison glands:** large **parotid glands** behind the eyes, and many smaller glands in its warty skin, produce a bitter, milky poison (**bufotoxin**). A dog or snake that bites a toad often drops it immediately and may become ill.
3. **Puffing up:** when attacked, the toad fills its lungs with air and **inflates** its body, raising itself on its legs, so it looks bigger and is harder to swallow.
4. **Nocturnal habits:** it hides under stones, logs or in holes by day and feeds at night, avoiding many predators and the drying heat of the sun.
5. **Staying still:** it often keeps very still when danger is near, relying on its camouflage.

### 3. Securing mates

To reproduce, an animal must attract a suitable mate of the same species, often in competition with rivals. Many animals have **structural** features (colours, ornaments) and **behaviours** (displays, songs, dances) for this.

#### Redhead male and female *Agama* lizards

The *Agama* lizard (*Agama agama*) shows clear differences between males and females (**sexual dimorphism**), especially in the breeding season.

| Feature | Dominant (breeding) male | Female (and young or subordinate males) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Head colour | Bright **orange-red** ("redhead") | Brown or olive-grey |
| Body colour | Dark **blue-black** | Brown, with pale spots or streaks; breeding females show orange or yellow patches on their sides |
| Tail | Orange-red at the base or middle, with a dark tip; long and banded | Brownish, banded |
| Size | Larger, with a bigger head | Smaller |
| Behaviour | Holds a territory; perches high; bobs his head and does "press-ups"; chases and fights rival males | Usually less conspicuous; when not ready to mate, she may avoid the male; she digs a hole and lays eggs |

**How these features help the male secure mates:**

1. The bright colours make the dominant male **conspicuous** to females, showing that he is healthy and strong.
2. **Head-bobbing** and **press-ups** are signals to females and warnings to rivals.
3. By **defending a territory** that includes several females, the dominant male can mate with all of them.
4. The colours of the male **brighten** when he is warm, active and dominant, and fade when he is cold, frightened or beaten in a fight.

The female's dull colours give her **camouflage**, which protects her (and the eggs she carries) from predators.

Flat textbook style, warm oranges, blues and browns, white background, no text.]

#### Display of feathers by birds

In many bird species, the males have bright colours or special feathers, which they show off (**display**) to attract females. This is called **courtship display**.

| Bird | Courtship display |
| --- | --- |
| **Peacock** | Raises and spreads its huge, shimmering tail feathers covered with eyespots into a fan, then shakes them and turns to face the peahen. The peahen is dull brown |
| **Village weaver bird** | In the breeding season, the male turns **bright yellow with a black face**. He builds a woven nest and hangs upside down from it, flapping his wings to show off his colours and the nest to females. A female inspects the nest and, if she accepts it, mates with him and lines the nest |
| **Domestic cock** | Has a large red **comb** and wattles, and long, shiny tail feathers. He struts around the hen, drops one wing and circles her |
| **Turkey** | Fans out its tail, puffs up its feathers and drops its wings, while the bare skin on its head turns bright red and blue |
| **Pigeon and dove** | The male puffs out its neck feathers, bows and coos to the female |

**Why males are usually brighter:** females usually choose the mate. Males compete for females, so males with the brightest colours or the best displays are chosen more often, and their features are passed on (**sexual selection**). Females, who often sit on the eggs, benefit from dull colours that hide them from predators.

### 4. Regulating body temperature

Birds and mammals are **warm-blooded** (homoiothermic): they keep their body temperature almost constant (about **37 °C** in humans, about **40–42 °C** in many birds), whatever the temperature outside. This keeps their enzymes working at the best rate. They use **skin**, **feathers** and **hair** to control heat loss and heat gain.

#### The skin

The mammalian skin is the main organ of temperature control.

| Structure in the skin | When the body is too hot | When the body is too cold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sweat glands** | Produce more **sweat**. Water in sweat **evaporates** from the skin surface, taking heat away with it and cooling the body | Produce little or no sweat |
| **Blood vessels (capillaries) near the surface** | **Widen (vasodilation)**: more warm blood flows near the surface and loses heat by radiation. This is why your skin looks flushed when you are hot | **Narrow (vasoconstriction)**: less blood flows near the surface, so less heat is lost. This is why your skin looks pale when you are cold |
| **Hair erector muscles** | **Relax**: hairs lie flat, so little air is trapped and heat escapes easily | **Contract**: hairs **stand up**, trapping a thicker layer of still air, which is a good **insulator** (in humans, this only produces "goose pimples") |
| **Fat layer (subcutaneous fat)** | — | Acts as an **insulator**, reducing heat loss. Whales and seals have a very thick layer of fat (blubber) |

Other ways mammals control temperature:

- **Shivering:** rapid contraction of muscles produces heat when the body is cold.
- **Panting:** dogs have few sweat glands, so they pant with their tongues out, losing heat by evaporation from the tongue and mouth.
- **Large ears:** the very large, thin ears of elephants (and desert foxes) have many blood vessels and lose heat; elephants flap them to cool down.
- **Behaviour:** resting in the shade, wallowing in mud or water (pigs and buffalo), huddling together in the cold.

Flat textbook style, soft pinks, creams and yellows, white background, no text.]

#### Feathers

- Birds have a thick covering of feathers. The soft, fluffy **down feathers** close to the skin trap a layer of still **air**, which is a very good **insulator**.
- When it is **cold**, a bird **fluffs up** its feathers using tiny muscles, trapping more air and reducing heat loss. This is why birds look round and puffed up on cold mornings.
- When it is **hot**, the bird **flattens** its feathers against its body to let heat escape, and may hold its wings away from its body, pant, or flutter its throat (gular fluttering) to lose heat by evaporation, because birds have no sweat glands.
- Waterbirds such as ducks spread oil from a preen gland over their feathers to keep them **waterproof**, so the insulating air layer stays dry.

#### Hair

- **Hair** (fur) traps a layer of still air next to the skin, which **insulates** the body.
- Animals in cold places have **thick fur**; many grow thicker coats in winter and shed them in summer (moulting).
- In hot places, animals often have **short, thin** coats; some light-coloured coats reflect sunlight.
- Hairs can be raised or lowered by **erector muscles** to change the thickness of the insulating air layer.

### 5. Conserving water

Organisms in dry places (deserts, the Sahel and the savanna of northern Nigeria, especially in the dry season and harmattan) must reduce water loss.

#### Spines in plants

Desert plants such as **cacti** (e.g. the prickly pear, *Opuntia*) and some *Euphorbia* species (e.g. the cactus-like candelabra trees and hedge plants seen in northern Nigeria) are called **xerophytes**: plants adapted to dry conditions.

- Their **leaves are reduced to spines** (in cacti) or are very small or absent. This greatly **reduces the leaf surface area**, and so **reduces water loss by transpiration**.
- The **stem** becomes thick, green and fleshy (**succulent**). It **stores water** in large cells, and takes over **photosynthesis** from the leaves.
- The spines also **protect** the plant from being eaten by thirsty animals.
- Spines can **shade** the stem surface and trap a layer of still, moist air near it, further reducing water loss.
- Other features: a thick, waxy **cuticle**; few, **sunken stomata** that open mainly at night; and long or widespread roots to collect water quickly when it rains.

**Note:** in many *Euphorbia* species, the spines are not modified leaves but outgrowths of the stem or modified stipules. They still help defend the plant, and the plant still reduces water loss by having very small leaves or shedding them in the dry season.

[IMAGE: Two water-conserving organisms side by side: left, a cactus (prickly pear type) with thick green fleshy pads and clusters of sharp spines instead of leaves, growing in dry sandy soil; right, a pangolin covered in large, overlapping brown keratin scales, walking on dry ground. Flat textbook style, soft greens, browns and sand colours, white background, no text.]

#### Scales in mammals

Most mammals are covered with hair, but a few have **scales**:

- The **pangolin** (scaly anteater) is covered in large, hard, overlapping **scales** made of **keratin** (the same protein as hair, nails and horns). Several species of pangolin live in Nigerian forests and savannas. Rats and mice also have small scales on their tails.
- A covering of scales reduces the area of exposed skin, which **reduces water loss** by evaporation from the body surface.
- The pangolin's scales are also an important **protection**: when threatened, it rolls up into a tight ball covered with sharp-edged scales that most predators cannot get through.

**Textbook variation:** the syllabus lists "scales in mammals" under conserving water. Most biologists see the pangolin's scales mainly as **armour** against predators, with any help in reducing water loss being a smaller benefit. Scales clearly reduce water loss in **reptiles**, whose dry, horny scales are one of their main adaptations to life on land. In the exam, give the syllabus answer (scales reduce water loss), but you can also mention protection.

#### Other water-conserving adaptations in animals

- **Camels** produce very concentrated urine and dry faeces, can let their body temperature rise during the day instead of sweating, and can drink large amounts of water quickly.
- **Desert rodents** (e.g. the gerbils of the Sahel) live in cool burrows by day, produce very concentrated urine, and get most of their water from their food.
- **Insects** have a waxy exoskeleton and spiracles that can close.
- **Reptiles** have dry, scaly skin and excrete uric acid as a paste, losing very little water.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Countershading = dark back, pale belly.** It is a type of camouflage (concealing colouration), not warning colouration. JAMB examples: fish, toads and snakes.
- **Warning colouration = bright colours advertising danger,** e.g. poisonous mushrooms, bees, the variegated grasshopper. It is the opposite of camouflage.
- **Termite castes:** queen (lays eggs), king (mates), workers (all labour, sterile, blind, wingless), soldiers (defence, large heads and jaws), winged reproductives (start new colonies). Worker and soldier termites can be male or female; worker bees are all female.
- **Hibernation is for cold; aestivation is for heat and drought.** In Nigeria, aestivation is the more common one (lungfish, snails).
- **Basking** is a behavioural adaptation of cold-blooded animals to raise body temperature.
- **Mosquito mouthparts:** the labium is the sheath and does not pierce the skin; the stylets pierce. Saliva with an anticoagulant goes in through the hypopharynx.
- **Butterfly vs moth:** clubbed antennae (butterfly) vs feathery antennae (moth).
- **Bird beaks:** hooked (eagle, flesh), short and conical (weaver bird, seeds), long and thin (sunbird, nectar), broad and flat (duck, mud-sieving).
- **Cactus spines are modified leaves** that reduce transpiration; the green stem does the photosynthesis.
- **Temperature control:** vasodilation and sweating cool the body; vasoconstriction, raised hairs or fluffed feathers, and shivering keep it warm.

## Quick summary

1. Adaptations are structural, functional (physiological) or behavioural features that help organisms survive and reproduce.
2. Adaptive colouration includes camouflage, countershading (fish, toads, snakes), disruptive colouration, mimicry and warning colouration (poisonous mushrooms).
3. Social insects such as termites live in colonies with castes: queen, king, workers, soldiers and winged reproductives, each with special jobs; bees have a queen, drones and workers.
4. Lizards bask to warm up and seek shade to cool down; many animals defend territories; animals survive cold by hibernation and heat and drought by aestivation.
5. Bird beaks and feet, and insect mouthparts (piercing-sucking in mosquitoes, siphoning in butterflies and moths), are adapted to their food.
6. Stick insects, praying mantises and toads protect themselves by camouflage, mimicry, poison and behaviour; male *Agama* lizards and many birds use bright colours and displays to win mates.
7. Skin, feathers and hair help warm-blooded animals regulate body temperature; spines in plants and scales in animals help reduce water loss.

## Practice questions

**1. The dark back and pale belly of a fish is an example of**

- A. warning colouration
- B. countershading
- C. mimicry
- D. disruptive colouration

**Answer: B.** Countershading cancels out the shadow on the belly, so the fish looks flat and blends into the water from above and below.

**2. Bright colours in some poisonous mushrooms serve as**

- A. camouflage
- B. countershading
- C. warning colouration
- D. courtship colouration

**Answer: C.** Bright colours warn animals that the mushroom is poisonous, so they learn to avoid it.

**3. In a termite colony, the caste responsible for defence is the**

- A. queen
- B. worker
- C. soldier
- D. king

**Answer: C.** Soldiers have large heads and powerful jaws (or chemical sprays) to defend the colony.

**4. Which of the following correctly describes worker termites?**

- A. Fertile, winged females
- B. Sterile, blind and wingless males and females
- C. Fertile males that mate with the queen
- D. Winged males and females that start new colonies

**Answer: B.** Worker termites are sterile, usually blind and wingless, and can be of either sex. They do all the labour of the colony.

**5. Lizards bask in the sun in the morning in order to**

- A. attract mates
- B. catch insects
- C. raise their body temperature
- D. lose water

**Answer: C.** Lizards are cold-blooded; basking warms them up so they can become active.

**6. The state of inactivity shown by the African lungfish during the dry season is called**

- A. hibernation
- B. aestivation
- C. migration
- D. basking

**Answer: B.** Aestivation is dormancy during the hot, dry season; the lungfish survives in a mucus cocoon in dried mud.

**7. The mouthparts of a butterfly are adapted for**

- A. piercing and sucking
- B. biting and chewing
- C. sponging
- D. siphoning

**Answer: D.** The adult butterfly's coiled proboscis uncoils to suck nectar from flowers.

**8. Which of the following birds has a beak adapted for tearing flesh?**

- A. Weaver bird
- B. Sunbird
- C. Hawk
- D. Duck

**Answer: C.** The hawk has a strong, sharp, hooked beak for tearing flesh. The weaver bird cracks seeds, the sunbird sucks nectar and the duck sieves mud.

**9. The bright orange-red head of the male Agama lizard is mainly an adaptation for**

- A. regulating body temperature
- B. conserving water
- C. securing mates
- D. obtaining food

**Answer: C.** The bright colours and head-bobbing displays of the dominant male attract females and warn rival males.

**10. In cacti, leaves are reduced to spines in order to**

- A. increase photosynthesis
- B. reduce water loss by transpiration
- C. absorb water from the air
- D. attract insects for pollination

**Answer: B.** Spines have a much smaller surface area than leaves, so less water is lost by transpiration. The green stem carries out photosynthesis.
