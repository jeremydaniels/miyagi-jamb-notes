## What is nutrition?

**Nutrition** is the process by which living things obtain or make **food** and use it. Every organism needs food:

1. **For energy:** food is broken down in respiration to release the energy needed for movement, growth, active transport, keeping warm and all other life processes.
2. **For growth and repair:** food supplies the raw materials (such as amino acids, sugars and fats) to build new cells and tissues and to repair damaged ones.
3. **For health:** small amounts of vitamins and mineral salts are needed for chemical reactions to work properly and to prevent deficiency diseases.

Nutrition is one of the characteristics of living things (the **N** in MR NIGER D; see Topic A1).

Organisms obtain food in two basic ways, called the two **modes of nutrition**:

- they **make** their own food from simple substances: **autotrophic** nutrition;
- they **take in ready-made** food made by other organisms: **heterotrophic** nutrition.

## Modes of nutrition

### Autotrophic nutrition

**Autotrophic nutrition** (from the Greek *auto*, "self", and *trophe*, "food") is the mode of nutrition in which an organism **makes its own complex organic food** (such as glucose, starch, proteins and fats) from **simple inorganic substances** (such as carbon (IV) oxide (CO₂), water and mineral salts), using an external source of **energy**. Such organisms are called **autotrophs**.

There are two kinds of autotrophs, depending on where the energy comes from:

1. **Photoautotrophs** (photosynthetic organisms) use **light energy** from the sun. They contain **chlorophyll** or other light-trapping pigments. The process is **photosynthesis**:

   carbon (IV) oxide + water → (light, chlorophyll) → glucose + oxygen

   6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

   Examples: all green plants (maize, cassava, mango, oil palm, grasses, ferns, mosses), algae (*Spirogyra*, *Chlamydomonas*), cyanobacteria (blue-green bacteria such as *Nostoc*), and *Euglena* in the light.

2. **Chemoautotrophs** (chemosynthetic organisms) use **chemical energy** released by oxidising simple **inorganic** substances such as ammonia, nitrites, hydrogen sulphide or iron compounds. The process is **chemosynthesis**. Examples: **nitrifying bacteria** in the soil, such as *Nitrosomonas* (which oxidises ammonium compounds to nitrites) and *Nitrobacter* (which oxidises nitrites to nitrates); sulphur bacteria; iron bacteria.

Both processes are explained in detail, and compared, in file B2b.

**Why autotrophs matter:** autotrophs are the **producers** in every food chain. All the food eaten by animals, fungi and most bacteria was first made by autotrophs, mainly by green plants. Green plants also release the **oxygen** that most living things need for respiration.

### Heterotrophic nutrition

**Heterotrophic nutrition** (from the Greek *hetero*, "other") is the mode of nutrition in which an organism **cannot make its own food** and must obtain **ready-made organic food** from other organisms, living or dead. Such organisms are called **heterotrophs**.

- All **animals** (from *Amoeba* to humans), all **fungi** (e.g. *Rhizopus*, mushrooms, yeast), most **bacteria**, and a few **flowering plants** that have no chlorophyll (e.g. dodder, *Cuscuta*) are heterotrophs.
- Heterotrophs depend, directly or indirectly, on autotrophs for food. A goat eats grass (directly); a lion eats the goat (indirectly).
- Heterotrophs are the **consumers** (animals) and **decomposers** (fungi and bacteria) of food chains.

Heterotrophs feed in different ways. These are the **types of heterotrophic nutrition**: holozoic, parasitic, saprophytic and symbiotic (explained below).

### Autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition compared

**Objective i: compare autotrophic and heterotrophic modes of nutrition.**

| Feature | Autotrophic nutrition | Heterotrophic nutrition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source of food | Makes its **own** food | Takes in **ready-made** food from other organisms |
| Raw materials | **Simple inorganic** substances: CO₂, water, mineral salts | **Complex organic** substances: carbohydrates, proteins, fats |
| Source of energy for making food | **Light** (photosynthesis) or **chemical energy** from inorganic reactions (chemosynthesis) | Energy comes from the **food** eaten |
| Chlorophyll | Present in photosynthetic autotrophs | **Absent** (except in partial parasites and carnivorous plants, which also photosynthesise) |
| Digestion | Not needed: food is made in usable form inside the cells | Usually needed: complex food must be **digested** into small, soluble molecules before it can be absorbed |
| Position in food chain | **Producers** (first trophic level) | **Consumers** and **decomposers** |
| Effect on environment's gases | Photosynthetic autotrophs take in CO₂ and give out O₂ in light | Take in O₂ and give out CO₂ (in respiration) |
| Dependence | Independent of other organisms for food | Depends on autotrophs, directly or indirectly |
| Movement | Most are fixed (plants); they do not need to look for food | Many move about to find food (animals); fungi grow towards it |
| Examples | Green plants, algae, *Euglena* (in light), cyanobacteria, nitrifying bacteria | Animals, fungi, most bacteria, dodder (*Cuscuta*) |

**Similarities:** both provide the organism with energy and raw materials; both end with food being used in the cells for respiration and growth (assimilation); both kinds of organisms respire.

**Note on mixed (mixotrophic) organisms:** some organisms use both modes. *Euglena* photosynthesises in light but can absorb dissolved organic food in the dark. **Carnivorous plants** (sundew, bladderwort) and **partial parasites** (*Loranthus*) photosynthesise but also take some nutrients from other organisms. These are explained below.

### Heterotrophic examples

| Group | Examples | Type of heterotrophic feeding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Flowering plants: total parasite** | Dodder (*Cuscuta*) | Parasitic: has no chlorophyll; takes all its food and water from its host |
| **Flowering plants: partial parasites** | *Loranthus* (African mistletoes), witchweed (*Striga*) | Parasitic, but with green leaves that photosynthesise; they take water and mineral salts (and some food) from the host |
| **Flowering plants: carnivorous (insectivorous)** | Sundew (*Drosera*), bladderwort (*Utricularia*), pitcher plants, Venus flytrap | Photosynthesise, but trap and digest small animals to obtain extra nitrogen and other minerals |
| **Flowering plants without chlorophyll that feed through fungi** | Indian pipe (*Monotropa*) and some forest orchids | Often called "saprophytic flowering plants"; they actually get their food from fungi in their roots |
| **Non-flowering: fungi** (placed with plants in older textbooks) | *Rhizopus* (bread mould), mushrooms, yeast, *Penicillium* | Saprophytic (and some are parasites, e.g. rusts and smuts of crops) |
| **Non-flowering: bacteria** (placed with plants in very old books) | Bacteria of decay; *Rhizobium* in root nodules | Saprophytic; symbiotic |

**Textbook variation:** older textbooks treated fungi (and even bacteria) as "non-flowering plants", so *Rhizopus* and mushrooms were given as examples of heterotrophic non-flowering plants. Modern biology places fungi in their own kingdom, **Fungi**, because they have no chlorophyll, have cell walls of **chitin** (not cellulose) and store food as **glycogen** (not starch) (see Topic A2a). In the exam, if fungi are offered as heterotrophic "non-flowering plants", accept it as the expected answer, but know that they are not true plants. Among true non-flowering plants (algae, mosses, ferns, conifers), almost all are **autotrophic**.

**Textbook variation (saprophytic flowering plants):** older textbooks call plants like the Indian pipe "saprophytic flowering plants". Botanists now know that these plants cannot digest dead matter themselves; they take food from fungi living in their roots, which in turn get food from dead matter or from other plants. Modern books call them **mycoheterotrophs**.

## Types of nutrition

The **types of nutrition** are the different ways in which organisms obtain their food. Autotrophic nutrition has two types (photosynthetic and chemosynthetic). Heterotrophic nutrition has several types:

| Type | How the organism feeds | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Photosynthetic** (autotrophic) | Makes food using light energy and chlorophyll | Green plants, algae |
| **Chemosynthetic** (autotrophic) | Makes food using energy from oxidising inorganic chemicals | *Nitrosomonas*, *Nitrobacter*, sulphur bacteria |
| **Holozoic** (heterotrophic) | Takes in **solid** (or liquid) organic food into the body, digests it inside, absorbs and assimilates it, and egests the waste | Most animals: sheep, man, goat, dog, *Amoeba*, *Paramecium*, *Hydra* |
| **Parasitic** (heterotrophic) | Lives **on or in** another living organism (the **host**) and obtains food from it, usually harming it | Tapeworm, roundworm, *Loranthus*, dodder, lice, ticks, *Plasmodium* |
| **Saprophytic** (heterotrophic) | Feeds on **dead and decaying** organic matter by secreting enzymes onto it and absorbing the digested products | *Rhizopus*, mushrooms, bacteria of decay |
| **Symbiotic (mutualistic)** (heterotrophic) | Two organisms of different species live together closely and **both benefit**, often by exchanging food | *Rhizobium* bacteria and legumes; lichens (alga and fungus); termites and the microorganisms in their gut; cellulose-digesting microbes in the sheep's rumen |
| **Carnivorous (insectivorous) plants** (mixed) | Photosynthesise, but also trap and digest small animals for extra nitrogen and minerals | Sundew, bladderwort, pitcher plant, Venus flytrap |

**Textbook variation:** some textbooks list **commensalism** (one organism benefits and the other is neither harmed nor helped, e.g. the remora fish travelling with a shark) as a type of nutrition. Others treat commensalism, mutualism and parasitism together under "symbiotic relationships", which are covered again in Topic C2. Some textbooks also list **filter feeding** and **fluid feeding** separately, but these are kinds of holozoic feeding.

### Symbiotic (mutualistic) nutrition in brief

The syllabus content item "types of nutrition" includes symbiosis, although the named examples in objective iv do not. The important examples are:

- ***Rhizobium* and legumes:** *Rhizobium* bacteria live in **root nodules** on the roots of cowpea, groundnut, soya bean and other legumes. The bacteria **fix nitrogen** from the air into ammonium compounds, which the plant uses to make proteins. The plant gives the bacteria **sugars** and a protected home. This is why legumes enrich the soil and are used in crop rotation.
- **Lichens:** a fungus and an alga (or cyanobacterium) live together as one body on tree bark and rocks. The alga makes food by photosynthesis; the fungus absorbs water and minerals and protects the alga from drying out.
- **Termites and gut microorganisms:** protozoa and bacteria in the termite's gut digest the **cellulose** in the wood the termite eats; the microorganisms get food and shelter.
- **Ruminants and rumen microorganisms:** bacteria and protozoa in the rumen of the sheep, goat and cow digest **cellulose** (see the sheep below).

## Holozoic nutrition

**Holozoic nutrition** (from the Greek *holo*, "whole", and *zoon*, "animal") is the type of heterotrophic nutrition in which an organism takes **solid or liquid organic food** into its body, then breaks it down **inside** its body. It is the typical way animals feed.

### The stages of holozoic nutrition

1. **Ingestion:** taking food into the body through the mouth (or, in *Amoeba*, by surrounding it with pseudopodia).
2. **Digestion:** breaking down large, complex, insoluble food molecules into small, simple, soluble molecules. It has two parts:
   - **mechanical (physical) digestion:** cutting, tearing, grinding and churning the food into smaller pieces (by teeth and the muscular stomach wall), which increases its surface area;
   - **chemical digestion:** breaking the molecules down with **enzymes** (e.g. starch → maltose → glucose; proteins → amino acids; fats → fatty acids and glycerol).
3. **Absorption:** the small, soluble molecules pass through the wall of the gut (mainly the **ileum**) into the **blood** and **lymph**.
4. **Assimilation:** the absorbed food is carried to the cells and **used**: glucose is respired for energy; amino acids are built into proteins for growth and repair; extra food is stored (e.g. as glycogen in the liver and muscles, or as fat).
5. **Egestion:** the **undigested** remains (e.g. fibre) are passed out of the body through the anus as **faeces**.

**Note:** egestion (removing undigested food that was never inside the cells) is **not** the same as **excretion** (removing the waste products of the body's own chemical reactions, such as urea and CO₂). JAMB often tests this difference.

The details of each stage in mammals, including all the digestive enzymes, are in file B2e.

### Kinds of holozoic feeders

Holozoic animals are grouped by what they eat:

| Group | Food | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Herbivores** | Plants only | Sheep, goat, cow, rabbit, grasshopper, snail |

### Holozoic nutrition in the sheep

The sheep (*Ovis aries*) is a **herbivore** that feeds mainly on **grass** and other green plants. In Nigeria, sheep are kept all over the country, especially in the north (common breeds include the **Yankasa**, **Uda** and **Balami**), and rams are in high demand at Sallah (Eid) festivals.

Grass is hard to digest because its cell walls are made of **cellulose** and it contains a lot of tough fibre. No mammal makes the enzyme **cellulase**, which digests cellulose. The sheep overcomes this with special teeth, a special stomach and the help of microorganisms.

#### Feeding and teeth (ingestion and chewing)

1. The sheep has **no upper incisors and no upper canines**. Instead, the front of the upper jaw has a tough, horny **dental pad**.
2. The **lower incisors** (and the lower canines, which look like incisors) cut grass by pressing it against the horny pad, and the sheep jerks its head to tear it off.
3. A wide gap without teeth, the **diastema**, separates the front teeth from the cheek teeth. The tongue uses it to move and mix the grass.
4. The **premolars and molars** have broad surfaces with sharp, crescent-shaped **ridges** of enamel. The lower jaw moves **from side to side** in a circular grinding action, so the ridges grind the grass like millstones, breaking open the cellulose cell walls.
5. The cheek teeth have **open roots** (in many herbivores) or very tall crowns, so they keep working even as they wear down.

The structure of these teeth and the dental formula of the sheep are covered in file B2d.

#### The four-chambered stomach (rumination)

The sheep is a **ruminant**: it "chews the cud". Its stomach has **four chambers**: the **rumen**, **reticulum**, **omasum** and **abomasum**.

1. The sheep grazes quickly, chewing the grass only a little, and swallows it into the **rumen**, a very large chamber.
2. In the rumen, huge numbers of **bacteria** and **protozoa** digest the **cellulose** using their enzyme **cellulase**. They ferment the sugars into simple **fatty acids**, which are absorbed through the rumen wall into the blood and give the sheep most of its energy. **Gases** (CO₂ and methane) are also produced and are belched out.
3. The food passes into the **reticulum**, whose lining is folded like a **honeycomb**. Here it is formed into soft balls called **cud** (bolus).
4. Later, when the sheep is resting, the cud is **brought back up** (regurgitated) into the mouth and **chewed again** thoroughly. This is **rumination** ("chewing the cud"). It breaks the food into tiny pieces, giving the microorganisms a larger surface to work on.
5. The cud is swallowed again. Fine food passes into the **omasum**, whose lining has many leaf-like folds that **absorb water** and squeeze the food.
6. The food then enters the **abomasum**, the "**true stomach**", which produces **gastric juice** with hydrochloric acid and **pepsin**. Here, proteins, including the bodies of the microorganisms that pass along with the food, are digested.
7. Digestion is completed in the long **small intestine**, where the products are absorbed. A fairly large **caecum** carries out some further fermentation by microbes.
8. The sheep has a **very long intestine** compared with its body size, which gives a long time for the slow digestion of plant food.
9. The undigested material is egested as small, dry **pellets** of faeces.

**Advantages of rumination:** the sheep can eat a lot of grass quickly in the open, where predators might attack, and then chew it slowly in a safe place. Its gut microorganisms also make **proteins** and **B vitamins**, which the sheep obtains when it digests them.

**Textbook variation:** some textbooks describe cud as being formed in the rumen and reticulum together, and some say the abomasum is the "only true stomach" while others call all four chambers "the stomach". The order rumen → reticulum → (mouth) → omasum → abomasum is the same in all textbooks.

### Holozoic nutrition in man

Man (*Homo sapiens*) is an **omnivore**: humans eat both plant food (rice, yam, cassava products such as garri and fufu, beans, maize, fruits and vegetables) and animal food (meat, fish, eggs, milk). Humans also **cook** much of their food, which softens it and makes it easier to chew and digest.

1. **Ingestion:** food is taken in through the mouth, often after preparing it with the hands or with cutlery.
2. **Teeth:** the adult has **32 teeth** of all four types: **incisors** (biting and cutting), **canines** (tearing; small in humans), **premolars** and **molars** (crushing and grinding). The jaw moves up and down and a little sideways. (Details in file B2d.)
3. **Mouth:** **saliva** moistens the food and contains the enzyme **salivary amylase** (ptyalin), which begins the digestion of cooked starch into maltose. The tongue rolls the food into a **bolus**, which is swallowed.
4. **Oesophagus:** waves of muscle contraction (**peristalsis**) push the bolus to the stomach.
5. **Stomach:** a **single**, J-shaped, muscular bag. It churns the food with **gastric juice**, containing hydrochloric acid and **pepsin**, which begins protein digestion. (Many textbooks also say that the stomach of babies makes **rennin**, which clots milk; see file B2e.)
6. **Small intestine:** in the **duodenum**, bile from the liver emulsifies fats, and pancreatic juice (amylase, trypsin, lipase) continues digestion. The **ileum** completes digestion and **absorbs** the products through its millions of **villi**.
7. **Large intestine:** absorbs water and salts; the **caecum** and **appendix** are **small** and have no digestive role in man, because humans cannot digest cellulose. Undigested cellulose (dietary **fibre** or **roughage**) adds bulk and helps the gut muscles move food along, preventing constipation.
8. **Egestion:** faeces are stored in the rectum and passed out through the anus.

The full details of each part of the human gut and its enzymes are in file B2e.

## Parasitic nutrition

**Parasitic nutrition** is the type of heterotrophic nutrition in which an organism, the **parasite**, lives **on or inside** another living organism of a different species, the **host**, and obtains its food (and often shelter) from it. The parasite **benefits** and the host is usually **harmed**, but usually not killed quickly, because the parasite needs its host alive.

### Kinds of parasites

| Kind | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ectoparasite** | Lives on the **outside** of the host | Lice, ticks, fleas, bed bugs, mosquitoes (when feeding), dodder, *Loranthus* (on the host's branches) |
| **Endoparasite** | Lives **inside** the host | Tapeworm, roundworm, *Plasmodium*, liver fluke, hookworm |
| **Obligate parasite** | Can live **only** as a parasite | Tapeworm, *Plasmodium*, viruses |
| **Facultative parasite** | Can live as a parasite **or** as a saprophyte | Some fungi that attack crops and then live on the dead remains |
| **Total (holoparasite)** | Gets **all** its food from the host (a parasitic plant with no chlorophyll) | Dodder (*Cuscuta*) |
| **Partial (hemiparasite)** | Makes some of its own food by photosynthesis; takes **water and mineral salts** (and some food) from the host | *Loranthus*, *Striga* (witchweed) |

**Textbook variation:** some textbooks class *Loranthus* as an ectoparasite because it grows on the outside of its host's branches; others note that its absorbing organs (haustoria) grow inside the host's tissues. Both descriptions are used.

### General adaptations of parasites

1. **Attachment organs** (hooks, suckers, haustoria) to hold on to the host.
2. **Reduced organs** that are not needed in the host, such as sense organs, locomotory organs and, in tapeworms, the whole gut.
3. **Protection** against the host's defences, such as a thick cuticle or anti-enzymes.
4. **Huge numbers of eggs** or seeds, to make up for the small chance of reaching a new host.
5. **Resistant eggs or cysts** that survive outside the host.
6. Often an **intermediate (secondary) host** that carries the parasite from one main host to another.
7. Many endoparasites **respire anaerobically**, because the gut contains very little oxygen.

### The roundworm (*Ascaris lumbricoides*)

*Ascaris lumbricoides* is the large human **roundworm**, a nematode (see Topic A2c). It is one of the commonest human parasites, especially in children in places with poor sanitation. The disease it causes is **ascariasis**.

**Appearance:** a long (about 15 to 35 cm), cylindrical, pinkish-white worm, pointed at both ends, covered by a tough **cuticle**. The sexes are separate; the female is larger, and the male has a curved tail.

**How it feeds:**

1. The adult lives **freely in the cavity of the small intestine** (ileum) of humans, where it is surrounded by the host's **partly digested and digested food**.
2. It has a **mouth surrounded by three lips** at the front end.
3. A muscular **pharynx** acts as a pump and **sucks in** the semi-liquid food from the host's gut (it may also feed on the cells of the gut lining).
4. The food passes along its own simple, straight **intestine**, where it is absorbed. Much of the food is already digested by the host's enzymes, so little digestion is needed.
5. Waste leaves through its **anus** near the hind end.
6. The worm keeps its place in the gut by **swimming against the flow** (it has no hooks or suckers).

**Adaptations to its parasitic life:**

- A thick, tough **cuticle** protects it from the host's digestive enzymes.
- It produces substances (**enzyme inhibitors**, or "anti-enzymes") that help stop the host's enzymes from digesting it.
- It can respire **anaerobically** in the low-oxygen gut.
- It has **no need for sense organs** or complicated movement.
- The female produces **enormous numbers of eggs**: about 200,000 a day.
- The eggs have **thick, resistant shells** and can survive in soil for months or years.

**How it spreads (life cycle in brief):**

1. Eggs pass out with the host's **faeces** onto the soil (for example, where people defecate in the open or where untreated sewage is used on farms).
2. In the soil, an infective larva develops inside each egg in about 2 to 3 weeks.
3. A new person **swallows the eggs** in contaminated food or water, on dirty hands, or on unwashed vegetables and fruit.
4. The eggs hatch in the small intestine. The larvae burrow into the blood, travel to the **liver** and **lungs**, climb up the windpipe, and are coughed up and **swallowed** again.
5. They become adults in the small intestine.

There is **no intermediate host**.

**Harm to the host:** the worms take food that the host needs, leading to **malnutrition** and poor growth in children; large numbers can **block the intestine**; larvae in the lungs can cause coughing and a kind of pneumonia; worms sometimes wander into the bile duct.

**Prevention:** use of toilets (no open defecation); washing hands with soap before eating and after using the toilet; washing fruits and vegetables well; drinking clean water; regular deworming with drugs such as albendazole or mebendazole (as in school deworming programmes in Nigeria).

**Textbook variation:** the length of the adult and the number of eggs per day vary between textbooks (e.g. up to 200,000 or "about a quarter of a million").

### The tapeworm (*Taenia*)

The **tapeworms** that infect humans are the **pork tapeworm** (*Taenia solium*) and the **beef tapeworm** (*Taenia saginata*), flatworms of the phylum Platyhelminthes (see Topic A2c for their structure and life history).

**Appearance:** a long, flat, ribbon-like worm, usually several metres long, made up of a small **head** (**scolex**), a short **neck**, and a long chain of segments called **proglottids**.

**How it feeds:**

1. The adult lives in the **small intestine** of humans, attached to the wall, with its long body lying in the gut cavity.
2. It has **no mouth and no gut** of its own.
3. It lives in a "bath" of food that has **already been digested** by the host's enzymes: glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins and minerals.
4. It **absorbs** these soluble foods directly through its whole **body surface** (the **tegument**), which is covered with tiny finger-like projections (microvilli) that increase the surface area for absorption.
5. Because it is so long and flat, it has a **very large surface area** compared with its volume.

**Adaptations to its parasitic life:**

- **Scolex** with **hooks** (in *T. solium*) and four **suckers** grip the gut wall, so the worm is not carried away by peristalsis. (*T. saginata* has suckers only, no hooks.)
- **No gut**: it absorbs ready-digested food through its body surface.
- A thick tegument and **anti-enzymes** stop it from being digested by the host's enzymes; it also produces substances that resist the host's immune attack.
- It respires **anaerobically** in the low-oxygen gut.
- **No sense organs** and no locomotory organs.
- Each proglottid is **hermaphrodite** (has both male and female organs), so the worm can fertilise itself; a single worm can reproduce.
- Mature proglottids are full of **tens of thousands of eggs** and break off to pass out with the faeces.
- It uses an **intermediate host** (the **pig** for *T. solium*; the **cow** for *T. saginata*), in whose muscles the larvae form cysts (**bladder worms**, cysticerci). Humans are infected by eating **undercooked pork or beef** containing these cysts.

**Harm to the host:** abdominal discomfort, loss of appetite or hunger, loss of weight, and malnutrition. With the pork tapeworm, if a person swallows the **eggs** (not the cysts), the larvae can form cysts in the muscles, eyes and brain (**cysticercosis**), which can cause seizures and is serious.

**Prevention:** cook pork and beef thoroughly; meat inspection at abattoirs; good toilet hygiene so pigs and cattle do not eat human faeces; treatment of infected people.

### *Loranthus* (African mistletoe)

*Loranthus* is the name used in many Nigerian textbooks for the **African mistletoes**: flowering plants that grow as parasites on the **branches of trees and shrubs**. They are common in Nigeria on **cocoa**, **kola**, **citrus** (orange, lime), **shea butter** trees, **mango**, **avocado** and many other trees. In Yoruba, mistletoe is called **àfòmọ́**.

**Appearance:** a bushy plant growing from a branch of its host, with **green, thick, leathery leaves**, and clusters of colourful **tubular flowers** (often red, orange or yellow), which are pollinated by sunbirds. The fruits are small berries.

**How it feeds:**

1. *Loranthus* has **green leaves with chlorophyll**, so it can **photosynthesise** and make its own sugars. It is therefore a **partial parasite** (hemiparasite).
2. It has **no true roots in the soil**. Instead, it sends special absorbing roots called **haustoria** (singular: haustorium) into the branch of the host.
3. The haustoria grow through the bark and join with the host's **xylem**.
4. Through these connections, *Loranthus* takes **water and mineral salts** from the host (and some organic food).
5. It uses this water and these salts in its own photosynthesis.

**How it spreads:** birds eat the sticky berries. The seed is surrounded by a very sticky substance (viscin). When the bird wipes its beak on a branch, or passes the seed out in its droppings, the seed sticks to a branch, germinates and sends a haustorium into the host.

**Harm to the host:** the host loses water and minerals; branches beyond the mistletoe become weak, produce fewer fruits and often **die back**; heavy infections can kill whole trees. Mistletoe is a serious pest of cocoa, kola and citrus farms. It is controlled by **cutting off** infected branches below the point of attachment.

**Uses:** mistletoe leaves are widely used in Nigerian **traditional medicine**.

**Textbook variation:** many of the West African mistletoes once placed in the genus *Loranthus* are now placed in other genera, especially ***Tapinanthus*** (e.g. *Tapinanthus bangwensis* on cocoa and citrus). JAMB and most school textbooks still use the name *Loranthus*, so use it in the exam.

**Dodder (*Cuscuta*) compared:** dodder is a **total parasite**. It is a flowering plant with thin, twining, **yellow or orange stems**, **no leaves** (only tiny scales) and **no chlorophyll**. Its haustoria join both the host's **xylem** (for water and salts) and **phloem** (for food). It cannot make any of its own food.

## Saprophytic nutrition

**Saprophytic nutrition** (from the Greek *sapros*, "rotten", and *phyton*, "plant") is the type of heterotrophic nutrition in which an organism, a **saprophyte** (or **saprotroph**), feeds on **dead and decaying organic matter**, such as dead plants and animals, dung, rotting fruits and stale food.

**How a saprophyte feeds (extracellular digestion):**

1. The saprophyte grows **on or into** the dead material.
2. It **secretes digestive enzymes** (such as amylase, protease, lipase and cellulase) **out of its body** onto the food.
3. The enzymes digest the complex, insoluble food **outside** the organism into simple, soluble substances (e.g. sugars, amino acids). This is **extracellular** (external) digestion.
4. The saprophyte **absorbs** the soluble products through its body surface (its cell walls and membranes).
5. It **assimilates** them: uses them for energy and growth, and stores the extra (as glycogen and oil droplets in fungi).

**Importance of saprophytes:**

- They are **decomposers**: they break down dead organisms and wastes, so the ground does not become covered with dead bodies and dung.
- They **recycle nutrients**: carbon is returned to the air as CO₂ (in their respiration) and minerals such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are returned to the soil, where plants can use them again.
- They form **humus**, which improves soil fertility.
- Useful saprophytes are used in industry: **yeast** in bread and brewing (e.g. making palm wine ferment, beer and burukutu), *Penicillium* to make penicillin, mushrooms as food.
- **Harmful effects:** many saprophytes **spoil food** (bread, fruits, grains, garri), rot wood, leather and cloth, and some produce poisons in stored food (e.g. aflatoxin from moulds on badly stored groundnuts and maize).

### *Rhizopus* (bread mould)

*Rhizopus* (e.g. *Rhizopus stolonifer*, "black bread mould") is a common **fungus** (see Topic A2a). It grows as a white, fluffy cotton-like mass on **stale, moist bread**, and on rotting **tomatoes, oranges, yam, pawpaw** and other soft food, later turning **black** as its spores form.

**Structure:**

- The body is a **mycelium**: a network of fine, white, branching threads called **hyphae** (singular: hypha). The hyphae of *Rhizopus* have **no cross walls** (they are **coenocytic**, with many nuclei in a continuous cytoplasm).
- **Stolons:** horizontal hyphae that grow over the surface of the food and spread the mould.
- **Rhizoids:** short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down **into** the food at points where the stolons touch it. They anchor the mould and **secrete enzymes** and **absorb** food.
- **Sporangiophores:** upright hyphae growing up from the same points, each ending in a round, black **sporangium** containing thousands of **spores**.

**How it feeds:**

1. The **rhizoids** and other hyphae grow into the bread.
2. Their tips **secrete enzymes** into the bread: **amylase** (digests starch to maltose and glucose), **protease** (proteins to amino acids), **lipase** (fats to fatty acids and glycerol), and others.
3. The starch and other foods in the bread are digested **outside** the hyphae into soluble substances.
4. The hyphae **absorb** the soluble sugars and amino acids by diffusion and active transport.
5. The food is used for growth and respiration. Excess is stored as **glycogen** and **oil droplets**.
6. When the food runs out, it reproduces by **spores** from the sporangia, which are carried by air to new food.

**Why bread gets mouldy faster when moist and warm:** *Rhizopus* spores are everywhere in the air. They germinate on food that is **moist**, **warm** and rich in **carbohydrate**. Keeping bread dry and cool, or adding preservatives, slows its growth.

### The mushroom

A **mushroom** is the **fruiting body** (spore-producing part) of certain fungi. Examples: the cultivated button mushroom (*Agaricus*), the **oyster mushroom** (*Pleurotus*) and the **straw mushroom** (*Volvariella*), which are grown in Nigeria; and the large, edible **termite mushroom** (*Termitomyces*) that appears on termite mounds after rain. Many wild mushrooms are **poisonous**.

**Structure:**

- Most of the fungus is **hidden**: an extensive **mycelium** of fine hyphae growing through the soil, rotting leaves, dead wood, dung or straw. This is the feeding part.
- The **mushroom** we see is made of tightly packed hyphae and has:
  - a **stalk (stipe)** that lifts it up;
  - a **cap (pileus)**, umbrella-shaped;
  - **gills**: thin, radiating plates under the cap, on which the **spores** are produced (in the basidiomycete mushrooms);
  - often a **ring (annulus)** on the stalk, and sometimes a cup (**volva**) at the base;
  - thread-like **rhizomorphs** or hyphae at the base, joining it to the mycelium.

**How it feeds:**

1. The hyphae of the mycelium spread through **dead organic matter**, such as fallen leaves, rotting logs, dung, or (when cultivated) sawdust, straw or cotton waste.
2. They **secrete enzymes** onto it, including **cellulase** and enzymes that break down **lignin**, so mushrooms can digest the tough material of dead wood and leaves that few other organisms can use.
3. The digested, soluble products are **absorbed** by the hyphae.
4. The food is carried through the mycelium and used for growth. When enough food has been stored and conditions are right (usually after rain), the mycelium produces mushrooms to release spores.

**Importance:** mushrooms are important **decomposers** of dead wood and leaves in forests, returning nutrients to the soil. Edible mushrooms are a good source of **protein**, B vitamins and minerals, and mushroom farming on agricultural waste is a growing business in Nigeria. Some mushrooms are **poisonous** and can kill (see Topic A3).

**Note:** the termite mushroom (*Termitomyces*) lives in a mutualistic partnership with termites (see Topic A3). Some fungi that produce mushrooms also form **mycorrhizae** with the roots of trees (a mutualistic relationship), and a few are parasites that rot living trees. The syllabus uses the mushroom as an example of a **saprophyte**, and most familiar mushrooms are.

## Carnivorous plants

**Carnivorous (insectivorous) plants** are green flowering plants that **trap and digest small animals**, mainly insects and other tiny invertebrates.

**Why do they do this?** They usually grow in **wet, boggy, acidic or sandy soils** that are very **poor in nitrogen** (and often phosphorus). These minerals are needed to make proteins and nucleic acids. By digesting animals, which are rich in protein, the plants obtain extra **nitrogen** (as amino acids and ammonium) and other minerals.

**Important:** carnivorous plants have **chlorophyll** and make their **own carbohydrates** by **photosynthesis**. They do not trap insects for energy. Their nutrition is therefore **partly autotrophic and partly heterotrophic**. Their leaves are **modified** into traps.

**How carnivorous plants feed, in general:**

1. **Attract** prey with colour, scent or sweet nectar.
2. **Trap** it with sticky surfaces, moving leaves, pitfall pitchers or suction bladders.
3. **Digest** it using **enzymes** (especially **proteases**) secreted by glands on the leaf (sometimes with the help of bacteria).
4. **Absorb** the soluble products (amino acids, minerals) through the leaf surface.

### The sundew (*Drosera*)

Sundews are small plants that grow in bogs, wet sandy places and damp ground in many parts of the world. Some species, such as *Drosera indica*, grow in wet, open places in tropical Africa, including West Africa.

**Structure of the trap:** the leaves (round, spoon-shaped or long and thin, depending on the species) are covered with many red, hair-like **tentacles** (glandular hairs). Each tentacle ends in a swollen **gland** that produces a shiny drop of **sticky mucilage**. The drops glisten in the sun like dew, which gives the plant its name.

**How it catches and digests prey:**

1. An insect (such as a small fly or ant) is **attracted** by the red colour and the glistening drops, which look like nectar.
2. It lands and gets **stuck** in the sticky mucilage. Its struggles make it more stuck and touch more tentacles.
3. The touched tentacles send signals to the tentacles nearby, which **slowly bend inwards** (over minutes to hours) and press the insect against the centre of the leaf. In some species the whole leaf blade curls around the prey.
4. The insect dies, either from exhaustion or because the mucilage blocks its breathing holes (spiracles).
5. The glands secrete **digestive enzymes** (proteases and others) and an acid onto the insect.
6. The soft parts of the insect are digested into soluble **amino acids** and minerals, which are **absorbed** by the glands.
7. After a few days, the tentacles straighten again, and the hard, undigested **exoskeleton** (made of chitin) is left behind or blown away.

### The bladderwort (*Utricularia*)

Bladderworts are rootless plants that live **floating in fresh water** (ponds, swamps, slow streams and flooded rice fields) or in very wet soil. Several species grow in tropical Africa, including Nigeria. Above the water they produce small, two-lipped **yellow** (or purple) flowers on upright stalks.

**Structure of the trap:** the finely divided underwater leaves carry many tiny **bladders** (usually only a few millimetres across). Each bladder is a hollow, flattened sac with:

- a watertight **trapdoor** (valve) at its entrance, which opens only inwards;
- sensitive **trigger hairs** near the door;
- often branched **antennae-like bristles** that guide small animals towards the door;
- **glands** on the inside wall.

**How it catches and digests prey:**

1. **Setting the trap:** glands inside the bladder **pump water out**. This lowers the pressure inside, so the walls are pulled **inwards** (the bladder becomes slightly concave). The door stays tightly shut.
2. A tiny water animal, such as a **water flea** (*Daphnia*), a mosquito larva or a protozoan, swims near and touches a **trigger hair**.
3. The door is pushed **open** inwards. The walls spring back outwards, and water rushes in, **sucking the prey in** with it. This happens in a few thousandths of a second, one of the fastest movements known in plants.
4. The door **closes** again behind the prey.
5. Glands secrete **enzymes** that digest the animal (bacteria inside the bladder also help break it down).
6. The soluble products (amino acids, minerals) are **absorbed**.
7. The glands pump the water out again, and the trap is reset, ready in about 15 to 30 minutes.

**Note:** because bladderworts eat **mosquito larvae**, they have been studied as a natural way of reducing mosquitoes in ponds, though they cannot control them on their own.

**Textbook variation:** textbooks give different numbers for how fast the bladder closes and how long it takes to reset. All agree that the trap works by **suction** and is extremely fast.

## "Determine their nutritional value"

**Syllabus line under objective iv: "determine their nutritional value".**

As explained in the note at the start of this file, this line is printed inside the list of heterotrophic feeding types, but it does not belong there. It repeats **objective xi: "determine the nutritional value of food"**, which is about the **classes of food** (carbohydrates, proteins, fats and oils, vitamins, mineral salts and water) and how much of each a food contains.

In short, the **nutritional value** of a food is how much useful nourishment it gives the body: the amount of **energy** it supplies (measured in kilojoules or kilocalories) and the amounts of **proteins**, **vitamins**, **minerals** and other nutrients it contains. For example:

- **Yam, cassava (garri, fufu), rice and maize** are high in carbohydrate and give much energy, but little protein.
- **Beans, groundnuts, eggs, fish and meat** are rich in protein.
- **Palm oil** is rich in fat (energy) and in **vitamin A** (from its orange-red carotene).
- **Fruits and green leafy vegetables** (e.g. oranges, *Amaranthus*, ugwu, bitter leaf) supply vitamins, minerals and fibre.

Linked to the organisms in this file:

- **Mushrooms** are valued as food because they contain protein, B vitamins and minerals while being low in fat.
- **Mutton** (meat from the sheep) is a high-quality **protein** food, also supplying fat, iron and B vitamins.
- **Parasites** such as roundworms and tapeworms **reduce** the nutritional value a person gets from their food, because the worms take part of it.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Autotrophs make food; heterotrophs take ready-made food.** The raw materials of autotrophs are inorganic (CO₂, water, mineral salts).
- **Chemosynthesis is still autotrophic:** nitrifying bacteria make their own food without light. Do not call them heterotrophs.
- **Carnivorous plants are still autotrophic for energy:** they photosynthesise and trap insects only for **nitrogen** (and other minerals). The best answer to "why do insectivorous plants trap insects?" is "to obtain nitrogen (proteins), because they grow in nitrogen-poor soil".
- ***Loranthus* is a partial parasite:** it has green leaves and photosynthesises; it takes **water and mineral salts** from the host's **xylem** through **haustoria**. Dodder is a total parasite.
- **Tapeworm has no gut; roundworm has a gut.** The tapeworm absorbs digested food through its body surface.
- **Intermediate hosts:** pig (pork tapeworm), cow (beef tapeworm). The roundworm *Ascaris* has **no** intermediate host.
- **Saprophytes digest food outside their bodies** (extracellular digestion) and then absorb it. They feed on **dead** matter; parasites feed on **living** hosts.
- ***Rhizopus* structures:** rhizoids (anchor, secrete enzymes, absorb), stolons (spread), sporangiophores and sporangia (spores).
- **Sheep's stomach order:** rumen → reticulum → (back to mouth) → omasum → abomasum. The abomasum is the true (glandular) stomach. Cellulose is digested by **microorganisms**, not by the sheep's own enzymes.
- **Egestion is not excretion:** egestion removes undigested food; excretion removes metabolic wastes.
- **Sundew vs bladderwort:** sticky tentacles (sundew) vs suction bladders in water (bladderwort).

## Quick summary

1. Nutrition is the process of obtaining or making food and using it; the two modes are autotrophic (making food from simple inorganic substances) and heterotrophic (taking ready-made organic food).
2. Autotrophs are photosynthetic (green plants, algae) or chemosynthetic (nitrifying bacteria); they are the producers in food chains. Almost all flowering and non-flowering plants are autotrophic; a few flowering plants are parasitic or carnivorous.
3. Holozoic animals ingest, digest, absorb, assimilate and egest food: the sheep is a ruminant herbivore that relies on rumen microorganisms to digest cellulose; man is an omnivore with a simple stomach.
4. Parasites feed on living hosts: the roundworm sucks in the gut contents, the tapeworm absorbs digested food through its body surface using hooks and suckers to hold on, and *Loranthus* is a partial parasite that takes water and salts through haustoria.
5. Saprophytes such as *Rhizopus* and mushrooms secrete enzymes onto dead matter, digest it outside their bodies and absorb the products; they are decomposers that recycle nutrients.
6. Carnivorous plants such as the sundew (sticky tentacles) and bladderwort (suction bladders) photosynthesise but trap and digest small animals to obtain nitrogen in poor soils.
7. Nutritional value (how much energy and nutrients a food gives) is covered fully in file B2c.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following is an autotrophic organism?**

- A. *Rhizopus*
- B. *Nitrosomonas*
- C. Tapeworm
- D. Mushroom

**Answer: B.** *Nitrosomonas* is a chemosynthetic bacterium that makes its own food using energy from oxidising ammonium compounds. The others are heterotrophs.

**2. The main raw materials used by autotrophs to manufacture food are**

- A. carbon (IV) oxide and water
- B. glucose and oxygen
- C. amino acids and fatty acids
- D. starch and proteins

**Answer: A.** Autotrophs build complex food from simple inorganic substances, mainly carbon (IV) oxide and water (with mineral salts). Glucose and oxygen are products of photosynthesis.

**3. *Loranthus* is described as a partial parasite because it**

- A. lives on the outside of its host
- B. has haustoria
- C. kills its host slowly
- D. has chlorophyll and manufactures some of its own food

**Answer: D.** *Loranthus* has green leaves and photosynthesises, taking mainly water and mineral salts from its host. Having haustoria (B) is true of all parasitic plants, including total parasites like dodder.

**4. The tapeworm obtains its food by**

- A. absorbing digested food through its body surface
- B. sucking blood through its mouth
- C. secreting enzymes onto dead matter
- D. ingesting solid food through its scolex

**Answer: A.** The tapeworm has no mouth or gut; it absorbs food already digested by its host through its whole body surface.

**5. In the stomach of a sheep, cellulose is digested mainly in the**

- A. abomasum
- B. omasum
- C. rumen
- D. duodenum

**Answer: C.** Bacteria and protozoa in the rumen produce cellulase, which digests cellulose. The abomasum produces gastric juice that digests proteins.

**6. Saprophytes differ from parasites in that saprophytes**

- A. have chlorophyll
- B. feed on dead organic matter
- C. live inside other organisms
- D. have no enzymes

**Answer: B.** Saprophytes feed on dead and decaying matter; parasites feed on living hosts.

**7. In *Rhizopus*, the structures that penetrate the food, secrete enzymes and absorb digested food are the**

- A. sporangia
- B. sporangiophores
- C. stolons
- D. rhizoids

**Answer: D.** The rhizoids grow down into the food, secrete digestive enzymes and absorb the soluble products. Stolons spread the mould over the surface; sporangia produce spores.

**8. Insectivorous plants such as the sundew trap insects mainly to obtain**

- A. nitrogen
- B. carbon (IV) oxide
- C. energy
- D. water

**Answer: A.** They grow in soils poor in nitrogen and digest insects to obtain amino acids. They get their energy and carbohydrates from photosynthesis.

**9. The bladderwort catches its prey by**

- A. sticky tentacles that bend over the prey
- B. a slippery pitcher filled with fluid
- C. suction into small bladders with trapdoors
- D. hinged leaves that snap shut

**Answer: C.** Bladderworts pump water out of their bladders; when prey touches the trigger hairs, the door opens and the prey is sucked in. A is the sundew, B the pitcher plant and D the Venus flytrap.

**10. Which of the following correctly matches the organism with its type of heterotrophic nutrition?**

- A. Mushroom: parasitic
- B. Man: holozoic
- C. *Ascaris*: saprophytic
- D. Sheep: parasitic

**Answer: B.** Man ingests food and digests it inside the body (holozoic). The mushroom is saprophytic, *Ascaris* is parasitic and the sheep is holozoic.
