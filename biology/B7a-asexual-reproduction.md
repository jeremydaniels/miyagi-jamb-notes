## What is reproduction?

**Reproduction** is the process by which living organisms produce new individuals (offspring) of their own kind. It is one of the characteristics of living things (see `A1-living-organisms.md`).

**Why reproduction is important:**

1. Every organism eventually dies. Reproduction **replaces** individuals that die, so the species does not become extinct.
2. It **increases the number** of individuals of a species.
3. It **passes on characteristics** (genetic material) from parents to offspring.
4. Sexual reproduction produces **variation**, which allows species to adapt and evolve (see Section D and Section E).

There are two types of reproduction:

- **Asexual reproduction:** only **one parent** is involved, and there is **no fusion of gametes** (sex cells).
- **Sexual reproduction:** usually **two parents** are involved, and **male and female gametes fuse** (fertilisation) to form a zygote.

## Asexual reproduction

**Asexual reproduction** is the production of new individuals from **a single parent**, **without the formation and fusion of gametes**.

**Key features of asexual reproduction:**

1. Only **one parent** is needed.
2. **No gametes** are formed and there is **no fertilisation**.
3. New cells are made by **mitosis** (ordinary cell division), in which each new cell gets an exact copy of the parent's chromosomes. (Meiosis is not involved.)
4. The offspring are **genetically identical** to the parent and to each other. A group of genetically identical organisms produced from one parent is called a **clone**.
5. It is usually **rapid**, and large numbers of offspring can be produced in a short time.
6. It is common in **simple organisms** (bacteria, protists, fungi, *Hydra*) and in **plants**. It is rare in complex animals.

**Types of asexual reproduction:**

| Type | Organisms | Brief description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fission** (binary or multiple) | *Amoeba*, *Paramecium*, *Euglena*, bacteria, *Plasmodium* (multiple) | The parent cell divides into two (or more) new cells |
| **Budding** | Yeast, *Hydra* | A small outgrowth (bud) grows from the parent and becomes a new individual |
| **Spore formation** | *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*, *Penicillium*, mosses and ferns | Tiny single cells (spores) are released and grow into new individuals |
| **Fragmentation** | *Spirogyra*, some flatworms | The body breaks into pieces, each of which grows into a new individual |
| **Regeneration** | *Planaria*, starfish, *Hydra* | A lost or cut part of the body grows back; a piece can grow into a whole organism |
| **Parthenogenesis** | Drone honey bees, aphids, some lizards | An egg develops into a new individual without being fertilised |
| **Vegetative propagation** | Flowering plants: yam, cassava, onion, cocoyam, plantain, *Bryophyllum* | A part of the plant other than the seed (stem, root, leaf, bud) grows into a new plant |

## Fission

**Content a.i: fission (e.g. *Paramecium*).**

**Fission** is the division of a single-celled organism into two or more new organisms. It is the simplest type of asexual reproduction.

### Binary fission

In **binary fission**, the parent cell divides into **two equal** daughter cells. The parent organism no longer exists; it has become two new individuals. Binary fission happens when the organism has grown to its full size and conditions (food, water, temperature) are good.

#### Binary fission in *Paramecium*

*Paramecium* is a slipper-shaped, single-celled organism covered with cilia, common in ponds and stagnant water (see `A2a-evolution-monera-protista-fungi.md`). It has two kinds of nucleus: a large **macronucleus** (which controls the everyday activities of the cell) and a small **micronucleus** (which controls reproduction).

*Paramecium* divides by **transverse binary fission** (across its width):

1. The *Paramecium* stops feeding and grows to its full size.
2. The **micronucleus** divides by **mitosis** into two micronuclei, which move to opposite ends of the cell.
3. The **macronucleus** lengthens (elongates) and then divides into two by pinching in the middle (this division is not ordinary mitosis; it is sometimes called amitosis).
4. A new **oral groove** and new **contractile vacuoles** begin to form, so that each half will have a full set of organelles.
5. A **constriction** (groove) appears across the **middle** of the cell, at right angles to its long axis.
6. The constriction deepens until the cell is pinched into two.
7. Two **daughter** *Paramecia* are formed, each with one macronucleus, one micronucleus, an oral groove and contractile vacuoles. Each grows to full size and can divide again.

In good conditions, a *Paramecium* can divide by binary fission one to several times a day, so a population can grow very quickly.

**Note:** *Paramecium* also has a sexual-type process called **conjugation**, in which two individuals join temporarily and exchange micronuclear material. This produces variation. Conjugation is not reproduction in the strict sense, because it does not increase the number of individuals by itself; it is usually followed by fission.

#### Binary fission in *Amoeba*

*Amoeba* (a shapeless protozoan of ponds and damp soil) divides in **any plane**, because it has no fixed shape:

1. The *Amoeba* becomes rounded and stops moving and feeding.
2. Its **nucleus** divides by **mitosis** into two nuclei, which move apart.
3. The **cytoplasm** constricts (pinches in) between the two nuclei.
4. The cell splits into **two daughter amoebae**, each with one nucleus.
5. Each daughter grows and feeds, and can divide again.

#### Binary fission in *Euglena* and bacteria

- ***Euglena*** divides by **longitudinal binary fission**: the cell splits **lengthwise**, from the front (where the flagellum is) to the back.
- **Bacteria** divide by binary fission: the single circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and a cross wall forms in the middle. In ideal conditions, some bacteria can divide every 20 minutes or so. This is why food goes bad quickly and why an infection can spread rapidly.

### Multiple fission

In **multiple fission**, the nucleus divides **many times** to form many nuclei; each nucleus then gets a little cytoplasm, and the parent splits into **many** daughter cells at once.

- **Example:** the malaria parasite, ***Plasmodium***, divides by multiple fission (called **schizogony**) inside human **liver cells** and **red blood cells**. One parasite in a red blood cell produces many new parasites (merozoites), which burst out of the cell and infect new red blood cells. The bursting of red blood cells at the same time causes the regular fever of malaria.
- *Amoeba* can also undergo multiple fission inside a protective **cyst** when conditions are unfavourable (e.g. when its pond dries up).

**Binary and multiple fission compared:**

| Feature | Binary fission | Multiple fission |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of daughter cells | **Two** | **Many** |
| Division of nucleus | Once | Many times before the cytoplasm divides |
| Examples | *Paramecium*, *Amoeba*, *Euglena*, bacteria | *Plasmodium* (in liver and red blood cells); *Amoeba* in a cyst |
| Conditions | Usually favourable conditions | Often inside a host cell or a cyst |

## Budding

**Content a.ii: budding (e.g. yeast).**

**Budding** is a type of asexual reproduction in which a small **outgrowth (bud)** develops on the parent. The bud grows, receives a nucleus, and finally separates (or stays attached) as a new individual. Unlike binary fission, the parent **remains** and the bud is at first **smaller** than the parent.

### Budding in yeast

**Yeast** (e.g. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) is a single-celled fungus used in making bread, burukutu, pito, palm wine (natural yeasts ferment it) and beer (see Topic B4 for the economic importance of yeast).

**Steps in budding of yeast:**

1. In good conditions (sugar, warmth and moisture), a mature yeast cell forms a small **bulge (bud)** on one side of its cell wall.
2. The **nucleus** of the parent cell divides by **mitosis** into two.
3. One of the daughter nuclei moves into the **bud**, together with some **cytoplasm**.
4. The bud **grows** larger.
5. A **cross wall** forms at the base of the bud, separating it from the parent cell.
6. The bud **separates** and becomes a new yeast cell, which grows to full size. Each cell can bud again; a parent yeast cell can produce several buds during its life, each leaving a scar on its wall.
7. Sometimes the buds **do not separate** immediately and bud again themselves, forming a short **chain** or cluster of cells (sometimes called a **pseudomycelium**).

In a warm sugar solution, yeast multiplies quickly by budding. You can see buds and chains of cells under a microscope in a drop of fermenting palm wine or of yeast mixed with sugar solution.

**Note:** yeast can also reproduce **sexually**, by forming spores (ascospores) after meiosis, especially when conditions are poor. Budding is its usual method in good conditions.

### Budding in *Hydra*

*Hydra* is a small, multicellular freshwater animal (a cnidarian) with a tube-shaped body and tentacles.

1. When food is plentiful, a small **outgrowth** appears on the side of the body, usually about halfway down.
2. Cells in this region divide by mitosis, and the outgrowth grows into a small tube that contains an extension of the parent's body cavity.
3. A **mouth** and **tentacles** develop at the free end of the bud.
4. The young *Hydra* (bud) feeds and grows while still attached to the parent.
5. A constriction forms at its base, and the young *Hydra* **breaks off** and lives independently.

*Hydra* also reproduces sexually when conditions become unfavourable.

### Fission and budding compared

| Feature | Binary fission | Budding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens to the parent | Parent **divides** into two and no longer exists as a separate individual | Parent **remains**; the bud grows from it |
| Size of the new individuals | Two **equal** halves | Bud is **smaller** than the parent at first |
| Number of offspring at a time | Two | One or several buds |
| Can new individuals stay attached? | No | Yes, buds may stay attached and form chains (yeast) or the bud grows on the parent for a time (*Hydra*) |
| Examples | *Paramecium*, *Amoeba*, *Euglena*, bacteria | Yeast, *Hydra* |

## Other types of asexual reproduction (brief)

These are not named in the syllabus but often appear in textbooks and questions.

### Spore formation (sporulation)

**Spores** are tiny, usually single-celled reproductive bodies, produced in large numbers, that can grow into a new organism. They are light and easily carried by **wind** or water, and many have thick walls that let them survive dry or harsh conditions.

- ***Rhizopus*** (black bread mould) and ***Mucor***: special upright hyphae (sporangiophores) bear round **sporangia** at their tips. Inside each sporangium, thousands of spores form by mitosis. The sporangium wall bursts and the spores are blown away by the wind. When a spore lands on moist food (bread, rotting fruit), it germinates and grows into a new mycelium.
- ***Penicillium*** and *Aspergillus*: spores called **conidia** are formed in chains at the tips of special hyphae (not inside sporangia).
- **Mosses and ferns:** spores formed in capsules or sporangia grow into new plants (in their life cycles, these spores are formed by meiosis and the cycle also has a sexual stage; see `A2b-evolution-plants.md`).

**Textbook variation:** spores formed by **mitosis** (e.g. the sporangiospores of *Rhizopus*) are true asexual spores. Spores formed by **meiosis** (e.g. in mosses, ferns and the spores of mushrooms) are part of a life cycle that includes sexual reproduction. Some textbooks list all spore formation as asexual; others separate the two.

### Fragmentation

The body of the organism **breaks into two or more pieces** (fragments), and each piece grows into a complete new organism.

- **Example:** the filamentous green alga ***Spirogyra*** (found in ponds as green, slimy threads). The filament breaks into short pieces, and each piece grows by cell division into a new long filament.
- Some flatworms and some annelid worms also reproduce by breaking up.

### Regeneration

**Regeneration** is the ability of an organism to **regrow lost or damaged parts**. In some simple animals, a **piece** of the body can grow into a **whole new organism**, which is a form of asexual reproduction.

- ***Planaria*** (a flatworm): if cut into several pieces, each piece can grow into a complete flatworm. Some *Planaria* reproduce naturally by tearing themselves in two.
- **Starfish:** a single arm attached to part of the central disc can grow into a whole new starfish.
- ***Hydra*:** a small piece of the body can regenerate a whole animal.

**Note:** regrowing a lost part, such as a **lizard's tail** (the *Agama* lizard can shed its tail to escape a predator and grow a new one), is regeneration but **not** reproduction, because no new individual is produced.

### Parthenogenesis

**Parthenogenesis** is the development of an **egg** into a new individual **without fertilisation** by a male gamete.

- **Examples:** the **drones** (males) of the **honey bee** develop from unfertilised eggs (see `A3-adaptations.md`); **aphids** (greenflies) produce many generations of females from unfertilised eggs in good conditions; some lizards, fish and plants (e.g. dandelion) can also reproduce this way.

**Textbook variation:** some textbooks treat parthenogenesis as a form of asexual reproduction (only one parent; no fertilisation); others treat it as a modified form of **sexual** reproduction, because it involves an egg, a sex cell produced in a reproductive organ. Know both views.

## Asexual and sexual reproduction compared

**Objective i: differentiate between asexual and sexual reproduction.**

**Sexual reproduction** is the production of new individuals by the **fusion** of a **male gamete** and a **female gamete** (fertilisation) to form a **zygote**, which develops into a new individual. Gametes are made by **meiosis**, which halves the number of chromosomes, so the zygote has the normal (diploid) number. Sexual reproduction in flowering plants and mammals is explained in `B7b-reproduction-in-flowering-plants.md` and `B7c-reproduction-in-mammals.md`.

### Differences between asexual and sexual reproduction

| Feature | Asexual reproduction | Sexual reproduction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of parents | **One** | Usually **two** (one male, one female); a hermaphrodite or self-pollinated flower is one parent but still uses two gametes |
| Gametes | **No gametes** formed | **Male and female gametes** formed |
| Fertilisation (fusion of gametes) | **Absent** | **Present**: gametes fuse to form a zygote |
| Type of cell division | **Mitosis** only | **Meiosis** (to form gametes), then mitosis (growth of the zygote) |
| Genetic make-up of offspring | **Identical** to the parent and to each other (clones) | **Different** from both parents and from each other |
| Variation | **No variation** (except from rare mutations) | **Variation** (from meiosis and the combining of genes from two parents) |
| Speed | Usually **fast** | Usually **slower** |
| Number of offspring | Usually **large numbers** quickly | Often fewer |
| Energy and time needed | **Less** (no need to find a mate, form flowers or gametes) | **More** (courtship, flowers, pollination, finding a mate) |
| Reproductive organs | No special sex organs | Special sex organs (e.g. flowers; testes and ovaries) |
| Adaptation to changing environment | **Poor**: all offspring may be killed by the same disease or change | **Better**: some varied offspring may survive and adapt; leads to evolution |
| Typical organisms | Bacteria, protists, fungi, *Hydra*, many plants | Most animals and plants |
| Examples | Binary fission in *Paramecium*; budding in yeast; yam tubers; onion bulbs | Fertilisation in man, goat, hen; pollination and fertilisation in maize and beans |

### Similarities between asexual and sexual reproduction

1. Both produce **new individuals** of the same species.
2. Both ensure the **continuity** of the species.
3. Both involve **cell division** (mitosis) for the growth of the new individual.
4. In both, **genetic material (DNA)** is passed from parent(s) to offspring.

### Advantages and disadvantages of asexual reproduction

**Advantages:**

1. **Only one parent** is needed, so there is no need to find a mate. A single organism can start a new population in a new place.
2. It is **quick** and produces **large numbers** of offspring in a short time.
3. Offspring are **identical** to the parent, so **good qualities** (e.g. high yield, sweetness, disease resistance) are **kept**. This is very useful to farmers.
4. It uses **less energy**: no flowers, pollinators, gametes or courtship are needed.
5. In plants, the new plant gets **stored food** from the parent organ (e.g. yam tuber, onion bulb), so it grows quickly and establishes itself faster than a seedling.
6. It does not depend on external agents such as wind, insects or water for pollination or fertilisation.

**Disadvantages:**

1. **No variation:** offspring are identical, so they cannot adapt well to changes in the environment.
2. **A disease or pest** that attacks one individual can attack **all** of them (e.g. a virus disease can spread through a whole farm of plants grown from cuttings of one parent).
3. **Bad qualities** of the parent are also passed on.
4. **Overcrowding and competition:** offspring often grow close to the parent and compete for space, light, water and nutrients (in natural vegetative propagation).
5. In plants, there is **no dispersal** over long distances, as there is with seeds.
6. Diseases (especially viruses) present in the parent are passed on to the offspring.

### Advantages and disadvantages of sexual reproduction

**Advantages:**

1. Produces **variation** among the offspring, so some may be better adapted to changes in the environment; this is the basis of **evolution**.
2. New, useful combinations of characteristics can arise, which plant and animal breeders use to produce **improved varieties** (hybrids).
3. In plants, **seeds** can be **dispersed** far away, reducing competition with the parent and colonising new areas.
4. Seeds can remain **dormant** and survive unfavourable conditions (e.g. the dry season).
5. Harmful genes are less likely to show (they may be hidden by normal genes from the other parent).

**Disadvantages:**

1. Usually needs **two parents**, so a mate must be found.
2. It is **slower** and uses **more energy**.
3. Good combinations of characteristics in the parents may be **broken up** in the offspring.
4. In plants, it depends on **agents of pollination** (wind, insects, birds) and suitable conditions.

## Vegetative propagation

**Vegetative propagation** (vegetative reproduction) is a type of asexual reproduction in **plants** in which a **vegetative part** of the parent plant (a stem, root, leaf or bud, **not the seed**) grows into a new, independent plant.

- **Natural vegetative propagation** happens on its own in nature, usually through special organs that **store food** and have **buds**.
- **Artificial vegetative propagation** is done by **people** (farmers and gardeners), who cut, join or bend plant parts to produce new plants.

Many of the organs used in natural vegetative propagation are also **perennating organs**: they store food and survive unfavourable conditions (such as the dry season) underground, while the leaves above ground die. When the rains come, the buds use the stored food to grow new shoots. This is why yam, cocoyam, ginger and onion can be stored for months and still sprout.

## Natural vegetative propagation

**Content a.iii: natural vegetative propagation.**

### 1. Bulbs

**Example:** **onion** (*Allium cepa*); also garlic and some lilies.

**Structure:**

- A bulb is a **short underground shoot**.
- The true **stem** is a small, flat **disc** at the base of the bulb.
- Many thick, **fleshy scale leaves** surround each other in layers on top of the stem. They **store food** (mainly sugars) and water. (When you cut an onion across, the rings are the scale leaves.)
- The outer scale leaves are **dry, thin and papery**; they **protect** the inner fleshy ones.
- A **terminal (apical) bud** is in the centre, and **axillary (lateral) buds** are found in the axils of the scale leaves.
- **Adventitious roots** grow from the underside of the disc-shaped stem.

**How it reproduces:**

1. When conditions are suitable, the **terminal bud** uses the food stored in the fleshy leaves to grow into green leaves above the ground (and later a flower stalk).
2. The **axillary buds** also grow, each forming a new small bulb (a daughter bulb) at the base of the parent.
3. The old scale leaves shrivel as their food is used up.
4. The daughter bulbs separate and each grows into a new onion plant.

**Garlic** forms many daughter bulbs called **cloves** inside one papery covering; each clove can be planted to give a new plant.

### 2. Corms

**Examples:** **cocoyam** (both the old cocoyam or taro, *Colocasia esculenta*, and the new cocoyam, *Xanthosoma sagittifolium*); also gladiolus and crocus.

**Structure:**

- A corm is a **short, swollen, upright underground stem** that **stores food** (starch) in the stem itself (unlike the bulb, where food is stored in the leaves).
- It has **nodes** and **internodes**, which can be seen as rings on the surface.
- The nodes bear thin, dry **scale leaves**, with **axillary buds** in their axils.
- There is a large **terminal (apical) bud** at the top.
- **Adventitious roots** grow from the base.

**How it reproduces:**

1. The **terminal bud** uses stored food to grow into the leaves and flowers of the new season.
2. **Axillary buds** grow into small daughter corms, called **cormels** (in cocoyam, the edible "fingers" or side tubers around the main corm).
3. The parent corm is used up and shrinks; a new corm forms on top of the old one (in plants such as gladiolus), or the cormels become separate plants.
4. Each cormel can be planted to give a new plant.

**Textbook variation:** in *Xanthosoma* (new cocoyam), people mostly eat the cormels and use the central corm or the top of the corm (with the base of the leaf stalks) for planting. In *Colocasia* (old cocoyam, taro), the main corm is mostly eaten. Some textbooks call the cocoyam a "tuber"; botanically it is a **corm**.

### 3. Rhizomes

**Examples:** **ginger** (*Zingiber officinale*, grown widely in southern Kaduna State, e.g. around Kafanchan), turmeric, *Canna* lily, and the troublesome weed **spear grass** (*Imperata cylindrica*).

**Structure:**

- A rhizome is a **horizontal underground stem** that grows along just below the soil surface.
- It is often swollen with **stored food**.
- It has **nodes** and **internodes**, with **scale leaves** at the nodes and **axillary buds** in their axils.
- It has a **terminal bud** at the growing tip.
- **Adventitious roots** grow from the nodes on the underside.

**How it reproduces:**

1. The **terminal bud** grows forward and upward to form a new aerial shoot (leaves).
2. **Axillary buds** at the nodes grow into **new branches** of the rhizome and new aerial shoots.
3. As the rhizome spreads and the older parts die or are broken, the branches become **separate plants**.
4. Farmers plant small pieces of ginger rhizome ("seed pieces" or "fingers"), each with at least one bud.

**Note:** spear grass is very hard to control on farms because its rhizomes spread underground and **every piece** left in the soil after hoeing grows into a new plant. This shows how effective vegetative propagation can be.

### 4. Stem tubers

**Examples:** **Irish potato** (*Solanum tuberosum*, grown mainly on the Jos Plateau and in other cool highland areas) and **yam** (*Dioscorea* species, e.g. white yam and water yam; Nigeria is the world's largest producer of yam).

**Structure of the Irish potato tuber:**

- A stem tuber is the **swollen tip of an underground stem** (a stolon) that stores food (starch).
- It is a **stem** because it has **"eyes"**: each eye is a **node** with a small **scale leaf** (seen as a tiny eyebrow-like scar) and one or more **axillary buds**.
- The eyes are arranged in a **spiral** around the tuber, with a **terminal bud** (a group of eyes) at the end farthest from where it was attached.
- The skin has **lenticels**.

**How it reproduces:**

1. When planted (or stored in warm, moist conditions), the **buds in the eyes** sprout, using stored food.
2. Each bud grows into an **aerial shoot** with leaves, and **adventitious roots** grow from its base.
3. Underground side branches (**stolons**) grow out from the new stem; their tips swell with food made by the leaves, forming **new tubers**.
4. One seed tuber can produce many new tubers.

**Yam:**

- The yam tuber stores food (starch) and has a **"head"** end, where the old vine was attached, from which new shoots most readily sprout.
- When the dry season ends, a bud near the head sprouts and grows into a new vine, using the stored food.
- New tubers form underground from the base of the new vine.
- Small yam tubers (**seed yams**) or **pieces (setts)** of large tubers are planted to produce new plants (see objective ii).

**Textbook variation (yam):** most Nigerian textbooks list yam as a **stem tuber**. However, botanists do not fully agree on the nature of the yam tuber: it does not have obvious nodes and eyes like the Irish potato, and some describe it as developing from the hypocotyl (the region between the stem and the root), or as a tuberous organ that is neither a typical stem nor a typical root. For JAMB, the usual answer is **stem tuber**.

### 5. Root tubers

**Examples:** **sweet potato** (*Ipomoea batatas*); also *Dahlia*. **Cassava** (*Manihot esculenta*) also forms swollen root tubers, but see the important note below.

**Structure:**

- A root tuber is a **swollen adventitious root** (or lateral root) that stores food.
- It **has no nodes, internodes, eyes or scale leaves** (because it is a root, not a stem).
- Buds (if present) are **adventitious buds**, usually near the end attached to the stem.

**How it reproduces:**

- In **sweet potato**, the root tuber can produce **adventitious buds** that grow into new shoots ("slips") when the tuber is planted or kept moist. In practice, Nigerian farmers usually propagate sweet potato by planting **vine (stem) cuttings**, which root easily at the nodes.
- In **cassava**, the root tubers **cannot normally grow into new plants**, because they have **no buds**. They only store food (and are a major food source: garri, fufu, lafun, tapioca). **Cassava is propagated by stem cuttings** (artificial vegetative propagation, explained below), not by its tubers.

**Important for the exam:** if you are asked for an example of a root tuber, sweet potato is the safest answer; cassava's tubers are root tubers too, but cassava is **propagated by stem cuttings**.

**Stem tuber and root tuber compared:**

| Feature | Stem tuber (e.g. Irish potato) | Root tuber (e.g. sweet potato, cassava) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin | Swollen tip of an underground stem (stolon) | Swollen root |
| Nodes and internodes | Present | Absent |
| Eyes (buds) | Present, at the nodes, arranged in a spiral | Absent (sweet potato has adventitious buds; cassava has none) |
| Scale leaves | Present (small, at the eyes) | Absent |
| Lenticels on surface | Present | May be present |
| Used for propagation? | Yes, the tuber or pieces with eyes | Sweet potato: sometimes, but usually vine cuttings are used; cassava: no, stem cuttings are used |

### 6. Suckers

**Examples:** **banana** and **plantain** (*Musa* species), **pineapple** (*Ananas comosus*); also sisal and some trees.

**What a sucker is:** a **sucker** is a **new shoot** that grows from an **underground bud** at the base of the parent stem (or from a root), comes up through the soil near the parent and forms its own roots.

**Banana and plantain:**

1. The true stem of the banana or plantain is an **underground rhizome** (called the **corm** or bulb by farmers). What looks like a "trunk" above ground is a **pseudostem** made of tightly rolled **leaf sheaths**.
2. **Buds** on the underground stem grow up through the soil as **suckers** around the base of the parent plant.
3. Each sucker grows its own roots and leaves and eventually produces its own bunch of fruit. The parent plant dies after fruiting, and its suckers take over. This is why a banana "stool" (clump) can keep producing for many years.
4. Farmers distinguish **sword suckers** (with narrow, sword-shaped leaves and a large base, which grow vigorously and are preferred for planting) and **water suckers** (with broad leaves and a small base, which are weak and not good for planting).

**Pineapple:**

- Pineapple can be propagated using **suckers** (shoots from buds on the stem below ground level or in leaf axils), **slips** (shoots from the stalk just below the fruit) and the **crown** (the leafy shoot on top of the fruit). Suckers fruit soonest; crowns take longest.

### 7. Runners and stolons

**Runners:**

- A **runner** is a **long, thin stem that grows horizontally along the surface of the ground** from the base of the parent plant.
- It has long internodes. At the **nodes**, **adventitious roots** grow down into the soil and **buds** grow up into new shoots.
- When the runner between the nodes dies or is cut, each rooted part becomes a **new plant**.
- **Examples:** **Bahama/Bermuda grass** (*Cynodon dactylon*), used for lawns and found on many school compounds; strawberry; *Oxalis*; the creeping stems of **sweet potato**, which root at the nodes where they touch the soil, and of many lawn grasses.

**Stolons:**

- A **stolon** is a side branch that grows out from the base of the stem, **arches over** and touches the soil at its tip (or grows along or just under the surface). Where the tip touches the ground, it forms roots and a new shoot, producing a new plant.
- **Examples:** mint, some blackberries (brambles), and the underground stolons of the Irish potato, which form tubers at their tips.

**Textbook variation:** the words "runner" and "stolon" are used differently in different textbooks. Some use them as the same thing; others define a runner as a creeping stem above ground with long internodes, and a stolon as an arching branch that roots at its tip (or a creeping stem below or at ground level). The key idea is the same: a **horizontal or arching stem that roots at its nodes or tip** and forms new plants.

### 8. Leaves

**Example:** ***Bryophyllum*** (now usually called *Kalanchoe pinnata*; also known as the "life plant", "miracle leaf", "resurrection plant" or "air plant"), a succulent plant common in Nigerian gardens and used in traditional medicine. Also *Begonia* and the African violet (which can grow new plants from leaf pieces under artificial conditions).

**How it reproduces:**

1. The leaves of *Bryophyllum* are thick, fleshy and have **notches** along their edges (margins).
2. In each notch there is a group of meristem cells that can form an **adventitious bud**.
3. When a leaf falls to the moist ground (or even while still on the plant, or when a detached leaf is left on a table), the buds in the notches **grow into tiny plantlets**, each with small leaves and roots.
4. The plantlets take food and water from the leaf at first, then root in the soil and grow into **new independent plants**.
5. One leaf can produce many new plants.

## Applying natural vegetative propagation in crop production

**Objective ii: apply natural vegetative propagation in crop production and multiplication.**

Many of the most important food crops in Nigeria are grown, not from seed, but from **vegetative parts**. Farmers use the plant's own natural ability to grow from these parts to **produce and multiply** their crops.

### How natural vegetative organs are used to grow crops

| Crop | Planting material | How it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Yam** | Whole small **seed yams** or **setts** (pieces) of larger tubers | Seed yams or setts are planted in mounds or ridges at the start of the rains; each grows a vine and new tubers |
| **Irish potato** | **Seed tubers** (small whole tubers) or pieces each with at least one or two **eyes** | Planted in ridges on the Jos Plateau and other cool areas; each eye gives a shoot |
| **Cocoyam** | **Cormels** or **corm tops** (head setts) | Planted at the start of the rains in moist soil |
| **Ginger** | **Rhizome pieces** ("fingers") with at least one bud | Planted in beds or ridges; harvested after about 8–10 months |
| **Onion** | Small **bulbs** (onion sets) or daughter bulbs; also seeds | Planted in irrigated beds in the north (e.g. Kano, Sokoto) |
| **Garlic** | **Cloves** | Each clove planted separately |
| **Banana and plantain** | **Sword suckers** (also corm pieces, "bits") | Suckers are separated from the parent with part of the corm and planted in holes |
| **Pineapple** | **Suckers**, **slips** or **crowns** | Planted in rows; suckers fruit soonest |
| **Sweet potato** | **Vine cuttings** (stems that root at the nodes) or sprouts (slips) from tubers | Planted on ridges or mounds |
| **Lawn and pasture grasses** | **Runners** and stolons (sprigs) | Planted to establish lawns, sports fields and pastures quickly |

### Planting yam setts (step by step)

Seed yams are expensive and take up part of the harvest that could be eaten or sold. Farmers often cut large tubers into pieces called **setts**.

1. Choose healthy, disease-free tubers (no rot, no nematode or beetle damage).
2. Cut the tuber into pieces (setts). Traditional setts are fairly large; the **minisett technique**, developed by Nigerian research institutes (e.g. the National Root Crops Research Institute, Umudike, and IITA, Ibadan), uses small pieces of about 25 g so that one tuber can produce many more plants. (The exact recommended size varies between sources.)
3. Make sure each sett has a portion of the **skin (periderm)**, because the new buds form just under it. Setts from the **head** end sprout first; setts from the middle and tail end take longer.
4. **Treat** the cut surfaces with wood ash or a fungicide/insecticide mixture to prevent rotting and pest attack, and leave them to dry (cure) for a day or so.
5. Plant the setts (or pre-sprout them in a nursery) in **mounds or ridges** at the start of the rainy season.
6. **Stake** the vines when they appear, so they can climb towards the light.
7. Keep the farm weeded. Harvest new tubers (or seed yams, from minisetts) at the end of the season.

### Planting plantain or banana suckers (step by step)

1. Choose **sword suckers** about 1 to 1.5 m tall from healthy, high-yielding parent plants.
2. Use a cutlass or spade to **separate** the sucker from the parent, taking a good part of its underground corm.
3. **Pare** (trim) the roots and outer layers of the corm to remove pests such as banana weevils and nematodes; some farmers dip it in hot water or a pesticide. (Paring and treatment practices vary.)
4. Cut back the leaves to reduce water loss.
5. Plant in a well-prepared hole with manure or compost, at the start of the rains.
6. Later, allow only one or two followers (suckers) to grow per plant, and remove the rest (**desuckering**), so the plant puts its food into a big bunch.

### Advantages of natural vegetative propagation in crop production

1. **Offspring are identical to the parent** (true to type), so farmers can keep good varieties (e.g. a high-yielding yam variety, a disease-resistant plantain).
2. **Faster growth and earlier harvest** than plants grown from seed, because the young plant uses the **food stored** in the planting material.
3. **Higher survival rate:** the planting material is large and well fed, so it establishes easily and resists drought better than tiny seedlings.
4. Crops that **produce few or no viable seeds** can still be grown (e.g. cultivated banana, plantain, yam (most varieties set little seed), cassava, sugar cane, pineapple).
5. Planting materials can be **stored** through the dry season and planted when the rains come.
6. **Easy and cheap** for farmers: no special skill is needed.
7. Planting material is produced by the crop itself, so the farmer can **multiply** a variety from his own harvest.

### Disadvantages of natural vegetative propagation in crop production

1. **Diseases and pests are carried** in the planting material and passed on (e.g. yam mosaic virus, banana bunchy top, cassava mosaic disease in infected stems, nematodes in yam tubers).
2. **No variation**, so new and better varieties cannot arise (breeders must use sexual reproduction to create new varieties).
3. **Part of the harvest** that could be eaten or sold must be kept for planting (e.g. about a quarter or more of a yam harvest may be kept as seed yam; estimates vary).
4. Planting materials are **bulky, heavy** and expensive to transport and store, and can **rot** in storage.
5. **Overcrowding** if suckers or runners are not thinned.
6. Some vegetative organs make plants into **persistent weeds** (e.g. spear grass rhizomes, nut grass tubers).

### Modern method: tissue culture (micropropagation)

In **tissue culture**, tiny pieces of a plant (e.g. the shoot tip) are grown on a sterile nutrient jelly containing plant hormones in a laboratory. Each piece grows into many tiny plantlets, which are then planted out. This produces **large numbers** of **disease-free**, identical plants quickly. It is used for plantain, banana, yam, cassava, pineapple and oil palm (e.g. by IITA in Ibadan). It is a modern form of asexual (vegetative) propagation.

## Artificial vegetative propagation

**Content a.iv: artificial vegetative propagation.**

**Artificial vegetative propagation** is the production of new plants from vegetative parts by **human methods**: cutting, joining or bending plant parts. The main methods are **cuttings**, **grafting**, **budding** and **layering** (including **marcotting**). Grafting, budding and layering are explained in detail under objective iii below.

### Cuttings

A **cutting** is a piece of a plant (usually a **stem**, sometimes a root or leaf) that is cut off and planted so that it forms **adventitious roots** and grows into a new plant.

**Examples:** **cassava** (stem cuttings), **sugar cane** (stem cuttings called **setts**), **sweet potato** (vine cuttings), and ornamental plants such as **hibiscus**, **Ixora**, **croton**, **bougainvillea**, **rose** and **Acalypha**; also kola and cocoa (in research nurseries), and cuttings of *Gliricidia* and moringa used as live fences and stakes.

#### Cassava stem cuttings (step by step)

1. Choose **mature, healthy stems** (from plants about 8 to 18 months old) from a high-yielding, disease-free variety. Avoid stems showing **cassava mosaic disease** (yellow, twisted leaves) or attacked by mealybugs.
2. Cut the stems into pieces about **20–25 cm** long, each with about **5 to 7 nodes** (buds). (Recommended lengths vary between sources, from about 15 cm to 30 cm.)
3. Make clean cuts with a sharp cutlass to avoid crushing the stem.
4. Plant the cuttings in ridges or mounds at the start of the rains, either **vertically**, **slanting** (at an angle of about 45°) or **horizontally**, with about two-thirds of the cutting in the soil, and the buds pointing **upwards** (the right way up).
5. **Adventitious roots** grow from the buried nodes and the cut end; **shoots** grow from the buds above the soil.
6. Root tubers form from some of the adventitious roots and are ready to harvest after about 9 to 18 months, depending on the variety.

**Note:** planting cuttings upside down gives poor growth, because the buds point the wrong way.

#### Sugar cane setts

Sugar cane is grown from **stem cuttings (setts)**, each with **two or three nodes** (buds). The setts are laid **horizontally** in furrows and covered with soil. Shoots grow from the buds at the nodes and roots from the root bands near the nodes.

#### Using rooting hormones

Gardeners often dip the cut end of a cutting in a **rooting powder** containing a synthetic **auxin**, which makes roots form faster and more reliably (see `B6a-support-and-movement-in-plants.md`). Cuttings also root better when kept in moist, shady conditions and when some leaves are removed to reduce water loss.

## Applying grafting, budding and layering in agricultural practice

**Objective iii: apply grafting, budding and layering in agricultural practices.**

### Grafting

**Grafting** is the joining of a **shoot (the scion)** from one plant onto the **rooted stem (the stock or rootstock)** of another plant, so that they unite and grow as **one plant**.

- **Scion:** a short piece of stem (with several buds) taken from a plant with the **desired qualities of fruit or flower** (e.g. large, sweet, high-yielding fruits).
- **Stock (rootstock):** a young, rooted plant (usually a seedling) with a **strong, healthy root system**, often chosen for vigour and resistance to soil diseases, pests or drought.

**Conditions for successful grafting:**

1. The scion and stock must be **closely related**, usually the **same species** or closely related species of the same genus or family (compatible). For example, sweet orange can be grafted onto rough lemon or other citrus, and mango onto mango, but mango cannot be grafted onto orange.
2. The **cambium** layers of the scion and stock must be in **close contact**, because the cambium is the tissue that divides to form the new tissues that join them.
3. Both should be **healthy** and actively growing.
4. The union must be **tied firmly** and **covered** (with grafting tape, polythene strip or grafting wax) to prevent drying out and entry of water and germs.
5. The diameters of scion and stock should be about the **same**.
6. Use **clean, sharp tools** and work quickly so the cut surfaces do not dry.

#### Steps in grafting (cleft or whip-and-tongue method)

1. **Raise the rootstock:** grow seedlings (e.g. of rough lemon for citrus, or of a local mango) in a nursery until they are about pencil-thick (several months to a year).
2. **Collect the scion:** cut a short shoot (about 10–15 cm long, with several healthy buds) from a selected high-yielding parent tree (the "mother tree"). Remove the leaves (leave the leaf stalks) to reduce water loss.
3. **Prepare the stock:** cut off the top of the rootstock at a smooth part of the stem.
   - **Cleft grafting:** make a vertical **split (cleft)** about 3–5 cm deep down the middle of the cut stock.
   - **Whip (splice) grafting:** make a long, slanting cut across the stock.
4. **Prepare the scion:** cut the base of the scion into a **wedge** (for a cleft graft) or a matching long slanting cut (for a whip graft).
5. **Join them:** insert the scion into the cleft (or place its slanting cut against the stock's) so that the **cambium layers line up** on at least one side.
6. **Tie** the union firmly with grafting tape or a polythene strip, and **seal** or cover the top of the scion (e.g. with a small polythene bag) to keep it moist.
7. **Keep in shade** and water the plant. Remove any shoots that grow from the rootstock below the graft.
8. After a few weeks, the scion's buds start to grow, showing that the graft has **taken** (the tissues have united). The tape is then loosened and removed.
9. When the grafted plant is strong, it is **transplanted** to the field.

**Other types of grafting:**

- **Approach grafting (inarching):** the scion is **not cut off** from its parent plant until it has united with the stock. Both plants are grown side by side (the stock in a pot raised up to the branch), a slice of bark and wood is cut from each where they touch, the cut surfaces are bound together, and after union the scion is cut below the joint and the stock is cut above it. Used for mango and some other trees that are difficult to graft.
- **Side grafting and veneer grafting:** the scion is inserted into a cut in the side of the stock; widely used for **mango** and **cashew** nurseries.
- **Top working:** grafting new scions onto the cut branches of an old, low-yielding tree to change its variety without replanting.

**Crops commonly propagated by grafting:** **citrus** (oranges, tangerines, grapefruit, lemon), **mango**, **cashew**, **avocado (pear)**, **guava**, **sweetsop/soursop** and **cocoa** (in improved nurseries).

### Budding (bud grafting)

**Budding** is a type of grafting in which a **single bud** (with a small piece of bark), taken from a plant with desired qualities, is inserted into the bark of a **rootstock**, where it unites and grows into the new shoot.

**Note:** this is different from budding in **yeast** and *Hydra* (natural asexual reproduction). See the comparison table below.

#### Steps in T-budding (shield budding)

1. **Raise the rootstock** seedling in a nursery until the stem is about pencil-thick, with bark that **peels easily** (when the plant is actively growing, usually in the rainy season).
2. **Collect bud wood** from the selected parent plant: a young, healthy shoot with well-developed buds.
3. **Make a T-shaped cut** in the bark of the rootstock, about 15–25 cm above the soil: a horizontal cut about 1–2 cm long, and a vertical cut about 2–3 cm long down from its middle. Cut only through the **bark**, not deep into the wood.
4. **Gently lift** the two flaps of bark at the corners of the "T".
5. **Remove the bud:** with a sharp budding knife, slice off a bud from the bud wood together with a **shield-shaped** piece of bark (about 2–3 cm long) beneath it. Cut off the leaf, leaving a short stalk to hold it by.
6. **Insert** the bud shield under the bark flaps of the rootstock, pushing it down until it fits snugly, with the **bud pointing upwards**. The cambium of the bud shield now touches the cambium of the stock.
7. **Tie** firmly with budding tape or a polythene strip, **leaving the bud itself exposed** (or covering it loosely).
8. After about **2 to 4 weeks**, check: if the bud and shield are still **green** and fresh, the bud has **taken**; if brown and shrivelled, it has failed.
9. Remove the tape. Then **cut back** (or bend over) the top of the rootstock **above** the bud, to force the new bud to grow (this removes apical dominance of the stock's shoot tip).
10. Remove all other shoots that grow from the rootstock, so all the food goes to the new bud. The bud grows into the main shoot of the new plant.

**Other types of budding:** **patch budding**, in which a rectangular patch of bark with a bud is fitted into a matching rectangular gap cut in the bark of the stock. Patch budding is the standard method used for **rubber** (*Hevea brasiliensis*) in Nigeria's rubber plantations (e.g. in Edo and Delta States), and is also used for cashew and some other thick-barked trees.

**Crops commonly propagated by budding:** **citrus** (oranges, tangerines, grapefruit; T-budding is the usual nursery method), **rubber** (patch budding), **roses**, mango (sometimes), avocado and cocoa.

**Advantages of budding over grafting:** it uses **only one bud** for each new plant, so a small amount of bud wood from a valuable tree can produce **many** new plants; it is quick and needs less material; the union is often stronger.

### Advantages and uses of grafting and budding

1. **Combine good qualities of two plants:** high-quality fruits from the scion and a strong, disease-resistant, drought-tolerant root system from the stock. For example, citrus scions are budded onto rootstocks that resist soil diseases.
2. **Early fruiting:** grafted or budded trees bear fruit much **sooner** than trees grown from seed (e.g. grafted mango and citrus may start bearing in about 3 to 5 years, while seedlings may take several more years; figures vary with variety and care).
3. **True-to-type fruits:** the fruits are identical to those of the parent tree, so quality is uniform, which is good for marketing.
4. **Propagating plants that do not root easily** from cuttings, or that produce few seeds or seedless fruits (e.g. seedless oranges).
5. **Changing the variety** of an established tree (top working), or growing **several varieties** on one tree.
6. **Smaller, more manageable trees** can be produced with dwarfing rootstocks, making harvesting and spraying easier.
7. **Repairing damaged trees** (bridge grafting over damaged bark).

**Disadvantages:** requires **skill**, training and special tools; the plants must be **compatible**; diseases (especially viruses) can be passed from the scion or stock; grafted plants usually cost more; the union can be a weak point.

### Layering

**Layering** is a method in which a **branch** (or stem) is made to **form roots while it is still attached to the parent plant**. When roots have formed, the rooted branch (the **layer**) is cut off and planted as a new plant. Because the branch stays attached to the parent, it continues to receive **water and minerals** from the parent while the roots develop, so the method is very reliable.

#### Simple (ground) layering (step by step)

Used for plants with long, flexible, low branches.

1. Choose a **long, healthy, flexible branch** near the ground.
2. **Wound** the branch at a point about 20–30 cm from its tip: remove a small ring or slice of bark, or make a slanting cut, on the underside. (Wounding interrupts the downward flow of food and hormones in the phloem, which collect there and encourage root formation.) Rooting powder may be applied.
3. **Bend** the branch down so that the wounded part touches the soil.
4. **Bury** the wounded part in a shallow hole (about 5–10 cm deep) of moist soil, and **peg** it down with a forked stick or wire, leaving the **tip** of the branch above the soil and pointing upwards (it may be tied to a small stake).
5. Keep the soil **moist**.
6. After some weeks (time varies with the plant), **adventitious roots** form at the wounded, buried part.
7. When enough roots have formed, **cut** the branch from the parent plant, between the parent and the new roots.
8. Lift the new plant with its roots and soil and **transplant** it.

**Examples:** hibiscus, Ixora, bougainvillea, jasmine, grape vine, guava, citrus, coffee, and many shrubs and climbers.

**Other forms of ground layering:**

- **Tip layering:** the tip of the shoot itself is buried and roots (e.g. blackberry).
- **Serpentine (compound) layering:** a long, flexible stem is bent in and out of the soil several times, so several new plants form along it (used for climbers).
- **Mound (stool) layering:** the parent plant is cut back near the ground, and soil is heaped around the base of the new shoots, which root in the soil.

#### Air layering (marcotting)

**Air layering**, also called **marcotting** (or **marcottage**, or Chinese layering), is used for **branches high up** on a tree, or for stiff branches that **cannot be bent** to the ground. Instead of bringing the branch to the soil, the soil (rooting medium) is brought to the branch.

**Steps in marcotting:**

1. Choose a healthy, **mature branch** (about pencil to finger thickness, or thicker, about one year old) from a high-yielding parent tree.
2. About 20–30 cm (or more) from the tip of the branch, **remove a ring of bark** (girdling) about 2–4 cm wide, all the way round the branch. Scrape off the cambium layer on the exposed wood, so that the bark does not simply grow back over the gap.
3. Optionally, apply **rooting hormone** (auxin powder) to the upper edge of the cut.
4. **Wrap** the ringed area with a ball of **moist rooting medium**, such as moist soil mixed with manure, **coconut fibre (coir)**, moss or sawdust.
5. **Cover** the ball with a sheet of **polythene** (clear or dark) or a piece of sacking, and tie it tightly at both ends so that the medium stays **moist**.
6. Check the medium from time to time and add water if it becomes dry.
7. After several weeks to a few months (depending on the plant), **adventitious roots** grow from the upper edge of the ring into the moist medium. They can often be seen through clear polythene.
8. When plenty of roots have formed, **cut off** the branch **just below** the ball of roots.
9. Remove the polythene carefully and **plant** the new plant (with its medium) in a pot or nursery bag; keep it in the **shade** and water it until it is established, then transplant it to the field.

**Why ringing works:** removing the ring of bark (including the **phloem**) stops sugars and hormones (auxins) made in the leaves from moving down past the ring. They **accumulate** just above the ring, where they stimulate the formation of roots. The **xylem** (wood) is not cut, so water and minerals still pass up to the leaves, keeping the branch alive.

**Examples of plants propagated by marcotting:** **guava**, **citrus** (lime, lemon, orange), **pear (avocado)**, **sweet orange**, **breadfruit**, **cashew**, **mango** (with variable success), litchi, **hibiscus**, **Ixora**, croton, rubber plant (*Ficus elastica*) and many ornamental shrubs.

**Textbook variation:** "layering" in some textbooks means only ground layering, and "marcotting" is treated as a separate method; others treat marcotting (air layering) as one type of layering. The success of marcotting differs greatly between species: guava, citrus and many ornamentals root easily, while mango is harder, so grafting is the usual method for mango.

### Advantages and disadvantages of layering

**Advantages:**

1. The layer stays **attached to the parent** while rooting, so it does not dry out or starve; the **success rate is high**.
2. Produces a **fairly large plant** quickly, which may flower and fruit sooner than a seedling.
3. Offspring are **true to type** (identical to the parent).
4. Simple layering needs **little skill or equipment**.
5. Useful for plants that are **difficult to propagate by cuttings**.

**Disadvantages:**

1. Only a **few new plants** can be produced from one parent at a time (limited by the number of suitable branches).
2. It is **slow** compared with cuttings, and the parent plant loses branches.
3. Marcotted plants usually have **no tap root**; their adventitious roots may give **poorer anchorage**, so the trees can be blown over by strong winds.
4. Diseases of the parent are passed on.

### Grafting, budding and layering compared

| Feature | Grafting | Budding | Layering (simple and air) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Number of plants involved | **Two** (scion and stock) | **Two** (bud and stock) | **One** (the parent plant) |
| Part used from the desired plant | A **shoot** (scion) with several buds | A **single bud** with a shield or patch of bark | A **branch** still attached to the parent |
| Is the part detached before it roots? | Yes (except in approach grafting) | Yes | **No**: it roots while attached, then is cut off |
| Source of roots of new plant | The **rootstock** | The **rootstock** | **Adventitious roots** formed by the branch itself |
| Main tissue that must unite | Cambium of scion and stock | Cambium of bud shield and stock | No union needed |
| Skill needed | High | High | Low (simple layering) to moderate (marcotting) |
| Number of new plants from limited material | Moderate | **Many** (one bud per plant) | Few |
| Can it combine qualities of two plants? | **Yes** | **Yes** | No |
| Examples | Mango, cashew, citrus, avocado, guava | Citrus (T-budding), rubber (patch budding), rose | Hibiscus, Ixora, guava, citrus, bougainvillea (simple and air layering) |

### Natural budding (yeast) and horticultural budding compared

| Feature | Budding in yeast and *Hydra* | Budding in agriculture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type | **Natural** asexual reproduction | **Artificial** vegetative propagation (a kind of grafting) |
| Who does it | The organism itself | A farmer or gardener |
| What is the "bud" | An outgrowth of the parent cell or body | An axillary bud cut from a parent plant |
| Number of organisms involved | One | Two (bud source and rootstock) |
| Result | A new individual that may separate from the parent | One combined plant: bud variety on top, rootstock below |
| Examples | Yeast, *Hydra* | Citrus, rubber, roses |

### General advantages of artificial vegetative propagation

1. **Preserves desirable characteristics** (true-to-type plants of good varieties).
2. **Faster production** of mature, fruiting plants than from seed.
3. Allows the propagation of **seedless** varieties and plants that produce few viable seeds (e.g. seedless oranges, banana, cassava, sugar cane).
4. **Combines** the good qualities of two plants (grafting and budding).
5. Produces **uniform** plants, which are easier to manage and harvest, and give uniform produce for the market.
6. Allows **large numbers** of plants to be produced from a few parents (cuttings, budding, tissue culture).

**General disadvantages:** no variation; risk of spreading diseases; some methods need skill, time and tools; plants may have weaker, shallower root systems (cuttings and marcots).

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Asexual = one parent, no gametes, mitosis, identical offspring (clones), no variation.** Sexual = gametes, fertilisation, meiosis, variation. These differences are tested very often.
- ***Paramecium* divides by transverse binary fission; *Euglena* by longitudinal binary fission; *Plasmodium* by multiple fission; yeast and *Hydra* by budding.**
- **Do not confuse the two meanings of "budding":** natural budding in yeast is asexual reproduction; budding in agriculture is a kind of grafting.
- **Bulb stores food in leaves; corm, rhizome and stem tuber store food in the stem; root tuber stores food in the root.** Onion = bulb; cocoyam = corm; ginger = rhizome; Irish potato (and yam, in most textbooks) = stem tuber; sweet potato = root tuber.
- **Cassava is propagated by stem cuttings, not by its root tubers,** which have no buds. Sugar cane is propagated by stem cuttings (setts).
- **The "eyes" of an Irish potato are nodes with buds;** this is the proof that it is a stem, not a root.
- **Banana, plantain and pineapple are propagated by suckers;** *Bryophyllum* by leaves (plantlets in the leaf notches).
- **In grafting, the cambium of scion and stock must be in contact,** and the plants must be closely related.
- **The scion provides the fruit quality; the rootstock provides the roots.**
- **Marcotting (air layering)** involves removing a ring of bark and wrapping the branch in moist medium; the branch roots while still on the parent tree.
- **A main disadvantage of vegetative propagation** is that diseases are passed on and there is no variation; a main advantage is that good qualities are kept and plants mature quickly.

## Quick summary

1. Asexual reproduction involves one parent and no gametes; offspring are produced by mitosis and are genetically identical clones. Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of gametes, produces variation and is usually slower.
2. Fission is the division of a single cell into two (binary, e.g. *Paramecium*, *Amoeba*, bacteria) or many (multiple, e.g. *Plasmodium*); budding produces a new individual from an outgrowth of the parent (yeast, *Hydra*).
3. Other asexual methods include spore formation (*Rhizopus*), fragmentation (*Spirogyra*), regeneration (*Planaria*, starfish) and parthenogenesis (drone bees, aphids).
4. Natural vegetative propagation uses bulbs (onion), corms (cocoyam), rhizomes (ginger), stem tubers (Irish potato, yam), root tubers (sweet potato), suckers (banana, plantain, pineapple), runners (Bermuda grass) and leaves (*Bryophyllum*).
5. Farmers apply natural vegetative propagation by planting seed yams and setts, cocoyam cormels, ginger rhizome pieces, onion bulbs, plantain sword suckers and pineapple crowns, slips and suckers; this keeps good varieties and gives early harvests, but spreads diseases and gives no variation.
6. Artificial vegetative propagation includes cuttings (cassava, sugar cane, hibiscus), grafting (mango, cashew, citrus), budding (T-budding of citrus, patch budding of rubber) and layering, including marcotting or air layering (guava, citrus, hibiscus).
7. Grafting and budding combine a scion with good fruits and a rootstock with good roots, giving early, true-to-type fruiting; layering roots a branch while it is still on the parent plant, giving a high success rate but few plants.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following is a characteristic of asexual reproduction?**

- A. Offspring are genetically identical to the parent
- B. Two parents are always involved
- C. Gametes fuse to form a zygote
- D. Meiosis occurs to form the offspring

**Answer: A.** Asexual reproduction involves one parent and mitosis only, so the offspring are identical clones. The other options describe sexual reproduction.

**2. *Paramecium* reproduces asexually by**

- A. budding
- B. spore formation
- C. binary fission
- D. fragmentation

**Answer: C.** *Paramecium* divides by transverse binary fission into two equal daughter cells.

**3. In yeast, the process in which a small outgrowth develops from the parent cell and later separates is**

- A. fission
- B. budding
- C. sporulation
- D. conjugation

**Answer: B.** Yeast reproduces by budding: the nucleus divides and one daughter nucleus passes into a bud, which separates as a new cell.

**4. Food is stored in fleshy scale leaves in**

- A. a corm
- B. a rhizome
- C. a stem tuber
- D. a bulb

**Answer: D.** In a bulb such as the onion, the food is stored in the fleshy scale leaves around a small disc-shaped stem. Corms, rhizomes and stem tubers store food in the stem.

**5. The "eyes" of an Irish potato show that it is a**

- A. stem tuber
- B. root tuber
- C. bulb
- D. corm

**Answer: A.** The eyes are nodes bearing scale leaves and axillary buds, which are features of a stem.

**6. Cassava is usually propagated by**

- A. root tubers
- B. stem cuttings
- C. suckers
- D. seeds

**Answer: B.** Cassava root tubers have no buds and cannot grow into new plants, so farmers plant pieces of mature stem with several nodes.

**7. Which pair of crops is propagated mainly by suckers?**

- A. Yam and cocoyam
- B. Onion and garlic
- C. Plantain and pineapple
- D. Ginger and turmeric

**Answer: C.** Plantain, banana and pineapple produce suckers from underground buds near the base of the parent plant. Yam uses tubers or setts, cocoyam corms or cormels, onion and garlic bulbs, and ginger and turmeric rhizomes.

**8. For a graft to be successful, it is most important that**

- A. the scion is larger than the stock
- B. the scion and stock are from different families
- C. the leaves of the scion are left on
- D. the cambium of the scion and the stock are in close contact

**Answer: D.** The cambium divides to form the tissue that joins the scion and stock, so the two cambium layers must touch. The plants must also be closely related.

**9. In marcotting, a ring of bark is removed from a branch mainly to**

- A. cause food and hormones to accumulate above the ring and stimulate root formation
- B. stop water from reaching the leaves
- C. kill the branch so that it can be removed
- D. allow insects to pollinate the branch

**Answer: A.** Removing the ring of bark (and phloem) stops sugars and auxins from moving down; they collect above the ring and stimulate adventitious roots, while the uncut xylem keeps supplying water.

**10. Which of the following is a disadvantage of vegetative propagation in crop production?**

- A. The plants mature quickly
- B. Desirable characteristics of the parent are kept
- C. Diseases of the parent plant are easily passed on to the offspring
- D. Seedless varieties can be propagated

**Answer: C.** Planting material from an infected parent carries the disease to the new plants, and all the plants are equally susceptible because there is no variation. The other options are advantages.
