## The meaning of homeostasis

**Homeostasis** is the **maintenance of a constant internal environment** in the body of an organism, within narrow limits, even when conditions outside the body or inside it change.

The word comes from two Greek words: *homoios* ("same" or "similar") and *stasis* ("standing still"). It was introduced in the 1920s by the American scientist **Walter Cannon**. The idea behind it came earlier, from the French scientist **Claude Bernard** (in the nineteenth century), who said that a constant "internal environment" (*milieu intérieur*) is the condition for a free and independent life.

**Important:** "constant" does not mean that the conditions never change at all. They change a little all the time, but they are kept **within narrow limits** around a normal value (a **set point**). For example, human body temperature rises and falls slightly during the day but stays close to 37 °C.

### The internal environment

The cells of a multicellular animal are not in contact with the outside world. They are bathed in **tissue fluid** (intercellular fluid), which forms from the blood plasma that leaks out of capillaries. The **blood plasma and tissue fluid** together make up the **internal environment**. Cells take in food and oxygen from the tissue fluid and pass their wastes into it.

If the internal environment changes too much, the cells cannot work properly. That is why homeostasis is so important.

### Factors kept constant by homeostasis

| Factor | Normal value or range in humans (approximate) | Why it must be kept constant | Main organs and hormones involved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Body (core) temperature** | About **37 °C** (normally between about 36.5 and 37.5 °C) | Enzymes work best at this temperature; too high destroys (denatures) them; too low slows them | Skin, hypothalamus, muscles, liver; thyroxine and adrenaline |
| **Water content** of the blood and tissue fluid (its concentration or osmotic pressure) | Fairly constant | If the blood is too dilute, water enters cells by osmosis and they swell and may burst; if too concentrated, cells lose water and shrink | Kidneys, hypothalamus, pituitary; ADH |
| **Salt (mineral ion) content**, especially sodium | Fairly constant | Affects osmosis, nerve impulses, muscle contraction and blood pressure | Kidneys, adrenal cortex; aldosterone |
| **Blood glucose** | About 4 to 7 millimoles per litre (roughly 70 to 125 mg per 100 cm³); figures vary slightly between books | Too low: cells (especially the brain) are starved of energy; too high: water is drawn out of cells and glucose is lost in urine | Pancreas, liver; insulin and glucagon |
| **pH of the blood** | About **7.4** (between about 7.35 and 7.45) | Enzymes and proteins work only within a narrow pH range | Lungs, kidneys, buffers in the blood |
| **Carbon (IV) oxide (CO₂) concentration** | Fairly constant | Too much CO₂ makes the blood acidic | Lungs, breathing centre in the brain |
| **Blood pressure** | Fairly constant (a typical resting value in a young adult is about 120/80 mmHg) | Too low: organs get too little blood; too high: damages blood vessels, heart and kidneys | Heart, blood vessels, kidneys; ADH and aldosterone |
| **Urea and other wastes** | Kept low | They are poisonous if they build up | Liver (makes urea), kidneys (remove it) |

**Textbook variation:** textbooks give slightly different "normal" values for body temperature (36.8 °C, 36.9 °C or 37 °C), blood glucose and blood pressure, because these vary from person to person, with the time of day and with how they are measured. In the exam, use **37 °C** for normal human body temperature.

### Why homeostasis is important

1. **Enzymes** work best at a particular temperature and pH. Homeostasis keeps the conditions right for the enzymes that control all the chemical reactions in cells.
2. Keeping the **water and salt** balance right prevents cells from bursting or shrinking by **osmosis**.
3. A steady supply of **glucose** gives the cells (especially brain cells) a constant source of energy for respiration.
4. Removing **poisonous wastes** (urea, CO₂) prevents them from harming cells.
5. It makes an organism more **independent of its surroundings**. A mammal can stay active in the cold harmattan morning or the hot afternoon, by day or by night, while a lizard must wait for the sun to warm it.

## How homeostasis works: control systems and negative feedback

Every homeostatic control system has the same basic parts:

| Part | What it does | Example in temperature control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Normal value (set point)** | The level the body tries to keep | 37 °C |
| **Stimulus** | A change away from the normal value | Body temperature rises to 37.5 °C on a hot afternoon |
| **Receptors (detectors)** | Detect the change | Temperature receptors in the **hypothalamus** and the **skin** |
| **Control centre (coordinator)** | Receives information from the receptors, compares it with the set point, and sends out instructions | The **hypothalamus** in the brain |
| **Effectors** | Organs that carry out the correction | **Sweat glands**, **arterioles** in the skin, hair erector muscles, skeletal muscles |
| **Response (corrective mechanism)** | The action that brings the value back to normal | Sweating and vasodilation cool the body |
| **Feedback** | Information about the new level goes back to the receptors | As the temperature returns to 37 °C, the receptors detect this and the response is switched off |

Messages pass from the control centre to the effectors either by **nerves** (fast) or by **hormones** (slower), or both.

### Negative feedback, step by step

**Negative feedback** is the mechanism by which a change in a factor away from its normal value **causes responses that reverse the change** and bring the factor back to normal. It is called "negative" because the response is **opposite** to the change.

1. A factor (for example, body temperature or blood glucose) is at its **normal level**.
2. Something causes the factor to **change** (rise or fall).
3. **Receptors** detect the change.
4. They send information (by nerve impulses or chemical signals) to the **control centre**.
5. The control centre sends instructions (by nerves or hormones) to the **effectors**.
6. The effectors carry out a **corrective response** that acts in the **opposite direction** to the change: if the level rose, the response lowers it; if it fell, the response raises it.
7. The factor **returns towards normal**.
8. The receptors detect that the level is back to normal, and the corrective response is **switched off** (this is the "feedback").
9. If the level overshoots and moves too far the other way, the **opposite** correction is switched on.

Because the corrections keep switching on and off, the level **fluctuates slightly** around the normal value instead of staying perfectly still.

**An everyday comparison:** an air conditioner with a **thermostat**. You set it to 24 °C. When the room becomes warmer than 24 °C, the thermostat detects this and switches the cooling on. When the room cools to 24 °C, the thermostat switches the cooling off. The hypothalamus works like the thermostat, and the sweat glands and blood vessels work like the cooling unit.

### Negative and positive feedback compared

Not all feedback in the body is negative. In **positive feedback**, a change causes responses that make the change **even bigger**. Positive feedback is rare and is used only for processes that must be completed quickly.

| Feature | Negative feedback | Positive feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effect of the response | **Reverses** the change | **Increases** (reinforces) the change |
| Result | Keeps a factor **stable** near a set point | Drives a process to completion quickly; the factor moves further from the starting level |
| Role in homeostasis | The **main mechanism** of homeostasis | Not used for normal homeostasis |
| Examples | Control of body temperature, blood glucose, water content, thyroxine level | **Childbirth**: contractions of the uterus cause release of **oxytocin**, which causes stronger contractions, until the baby is born; **blood clotting**; ripening of fruits by **ethylene** in plants (see `B9c-hormonal-control.md`) |

### Organs involved in homeostasis

| Organ | Main homeostatic roles |
| --- | --- |
| **Skin** | Temperature regulation (sweating, changes in blood flow, hair erection, insulation by fat); contains temperature receptors; loses some water and salts in sweat |
| **Kidneys** | Regulate the **water** and **salt** content of the blood (**osmoregulation**); remove urea and other wastes; help control blood **pH** and blood pressure |
| **Liver** | Regulates **blood glucose** (stores excess glucose as **glycogen** and releases glucose when needed); breaks down excess amino acids (**deamination**) to make **urea**; breaks down old hormones, drugs, alcohol and poisons (**detoxification**); produces a lot of **heat** from its many chemical reactions, which helps keep the body warm |
| **Hypothalamus** (part of the brain) | The main **control centre**: contains **temperature receptors** that monitor the blood temperature, and **osmoreceptors** that monitor the water content of the blood; controls sweating, shivering and the blood vessels of the skin; makes **ADH**; controls thirst; controls the pituitary gland |
| **Pituitary gland** | Releases **ADH** (made in the hypothalamus) to control water reabsorption; releases **TSH** and **ACTH**, which control the thyroid and adrenal cortex |
| **Pancreas** (islets of Langerhans) | Detects changes in **blood glucose** and secretes **insulin** and **glucagon** |
| **Adrenal glands** | The cortex makes **aldosterone** (salt and water balance) and **cortisol**; the medulla makes **adrenaline** (raises blood glucose and heat production) |
| **Thyroid gland** | Makes **thyroxine**, which controls the metabolic rate and therefore heat production |
| **Lungs** | Remove **CO₂**, helping to keep blood pH constant; lose some water vapour |

## Body temperature regulation

### Endotherms and ectotherms

Animals are divided into two groups according to how they get and control their body heat.

- **Endotherms** (also called **homoiotherms** or **homeotherms**; commonly "warm-blooded" animals) produce most of their body heat **internally** from **respiration** and other chemical reactions (metabolism), and **keep their body temperature almost constant** whatever the temperature of the surroundings. **Birds** and **mammals** are endotherms.
- **Ectotherms** (also called **poikilotherms**; commonly "cold-blooded" animals) get most of their body heat from the **surroundings** (the sun, warm rocks, warm water). Their body temperature **changes with the temperature of the surroundings**. **Fish, amphibians, reptiles** and all **invertebrates** (e.g. insects, snails, earthworms) are ectotherms.

| Feature | Endotherms (homoiotherms) | Ectotherms (poikilotherms) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main source of body heat | **Internal**: metabolism (respiration), especially in the liver and muscles | **External**: the sun and the surroundings |
| Body temperature | **Kept almost constant** (about 37 °C in humans; about 40 to 42 °C in many birds) | **Varies with the surroundings** |
| Control of temperature | **Physiological** (sweating, shivering, changing blood flow, metabolic rate) as well as behavioural | Mainly **behavioural** (basking, seeking shade, burrowing) |
| Insulation | Hair or fur (mammals), feathers (birds), fat under the skin | Usually none (scales, moist skin or shell) |
| Metabolic rate | **High** | **Low** |
| Food needed | **Large amounts** (much food energy is used to make heat) | **Much less** for the same body size |
| Activity in cold conditions | Can stay **active** in the cold, at night and in cold seasons | Become **sluggish** in the cold; may become dormant (hibernation in cold climates) |
| Activity in very hot, dry conditions | Can stay active, but lose water by sweating or panting | May shelter or become dormant (aestivation) |
| Range of habitats | Can live in a very wide range of climates, from deserts to the poles | More limited by climate |
| Examples | Humans, goats, cattle, rats, bats, hens, pigeons, eagles | Fish (*Tilapia*, catfish), toads, frogs, *Agama* lizards, snakes, crocodiles, insects, snails |

**Textbook variation:** "warm-blooded" and "cold-blooded" are popular terms but are misleading: a lizard basking in the midday sun may have a **warmer** body than a mammal. Most modern textbooks prefer **endotherm** and **ectotherm** (or homoiotherm and poikilotherm). Some textbooks also note exceptions: some mammals (e.g. bats and some rodents) let their body temperature fall during torpor or hibernation, and some large fish and insects can warm parts of their body by muscle activity.

**How ectotherms regulate their temperature (by behaviour):** an *Agama* lizard **basks** in the morning sun to warm up, turning its body side-on to the sun; at midday it moves into the **shade** or into crevices to avoid overheating; at night it shelters in cracks (see `A3-adaptations.md`). Crocodiles lie with their mouths open in the sun and cool off in water; desert snakes and lizards burrow into cooler sand. Many ectotherms become **dormant** when it is too cold or too hot and dry (hibernation and aestivation).

### Heat gain and heat loss

To keep its temperature constant, an endotherm must balance **heat gained** against **heat lost**.

**Ways the body gains heat:**

1. **Metabolism:** chemical reactions in cells, especially **respiration**, release heat. The **liver** and the **muscles** (especially during exercise) produce most heat.
2. **Absorbing heat** from the surroundings: from sunlight, a fire, hot food and drinks, or warm air.

**Ways the body loses heat:**

| Method | How heat is lost | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Radiation** | Heat given off from the warm skin surface to cooler surroundings as infrared rays | Heat lost from the skin at night |
| **Conduction** | Direct transfer of heat to cooler objects in contact with the body | Sitting on a cold cement floor; swimming in a cool stream |
| **Convection** | Warm air next to the skin rises and is replaced by cooler air; a breeze or fan speeds this up | Feeling cool under a fan or in the wind |
| **Evaporation** | Water (sweat, or moisture in the breath) changes into vapour, taking heat (latent heat) from the body | Sweating; panting in dogs |
| **In urine and faeces, and in exhaled air** | Small amounts of warm material leave the body | — |

**Important:** when the surroundings are **hotter** than the body (as on a very hot afternoon in Maiduguri or Sokoto in the hot season), the body **gains** heat by radiation and conduction instead of losing it. Then **evaporation of sweat** is the **only** effective way to lose heat. If the air is also very **humid** (as in the coastal south in the rainy season), sweat evaporates slowly, and the body finds it hard to cool down; people feel hot and sticky.

### The skin and temperature regulation

The **skin** is the main organ through which body temperature is regulated. (Its structure is also described in `B5-excretion.md` and, as a sense organ, in `B9b-sense-organs.md`.)

| Structure in the skin | Role in temperature regulation |
| --- | --- |
| **Sweat glands** | Coiled tubes in the dermis that produce **sweat** (water, salts and a little urea) from the blood; sweat flows along the **sweat duct** to a **pore** on the surface. The evaporation of sweat cools the body |
| **Blood vessels (arterioles and capillaries)** | Arterioles supplying the capillaries near the skin surface can **widen (vasodilation)** to let more warm blood flow near the surface and lose heat, or **narrow (vasoconstriction)** to reduce blood flow there and keep heat in. **Shunt vessels** deeper in the skin can carry blood past the surface capillaries when heat must be conserved |
| **Hairs and hair erector muscles** | Each hair has a tiny **erector (arrector pili) muscle**. When it **contracts**, the hair stands up and traps a layer of still **air**, which is a good **insulator**. When it relaxes, the hair lies flat. (In humans, who have little body hair, this only produces "goose pimples") |
| **Subcutaneous fat (adipose tissue)** | A layer of fat under the dermis that acts as an **insulator**, reducing heat loss. Thick in animals of cold places (blubber in whales and seals) |
| **Temperature receptors (thermoreceptors)** | Nerve endings that detect **heat and cold** at the skin surface and send impulses to the hypothalamus, giving early warning of changes in the surroundings |
| **Sensory nerves and motor nerves** | Carry impulses from the receptors to the brain and from the brain to the sweat glands, arterioles and erector muscles |

### The hypothalamus: the body's thermostat

The **hypothalamus** is the **temperature control centre**.

1. **Receptors in the hypothalamus** itself monitor the temperature of the **blood** flowing through the brain. This tells the brain the **core** body temperature.
2. **Receptors in the skin** monitor the temperature of the **surroundings** and send impulses to the hypothalamus. This gives early warning before the core temperature changes.
3. The hypothalamus compares this information with the **set point** (about 37 °C).
4. It sends **nerve impulses** to the effectors (sweat glands, skin arterioles, hair erector muscles, skeletal muscles) and **hormonal signals** (via the pituitary and thyroid, and via the adrenal glands) to bring the temperature back to normal.
5. It also makes us **feel** hot or cold, which leads to **behavioural** responses.

### When the body is too hot, step by step

Example: a student playing football at midday in the hot season.

1. Muscle activity and the hot sun cause the body temperature to **rise** above 37 °C.
2. **Temperature receptors** in the skin and the **hypothalamus** detect the rise.
3. The hypothalamus sends impulses to the effectors, causing these responses:
   - **Sweating increases:** the sweat glands produce more sweat, which spreads over the skin. As the water in the sweat **evaporates**, it takes **latent heat** from the skin, **cooling** the body.
   - **Vasodilation:** the **arterioles** supplying the capillaries near the skin surface **widen (dilate)**, and the shunt vessels close. More warm blood flows **near the surface**, and more heat is **lost by radiation** (and by conduction and convection). This is why the skin of a hot, light-skinned person looks **flushed** (red), and why the skin of anyone feels warm when they are hot.
   - **Hair erector muscles relax:** the hairs **lie flat**, so very little air is trapped, and heat escapes more easily.
   - **Metabolic rate decreases** slightly (less thyroxine is released over time), so less heat is produced.
   - **No shivering.**
4. **Behavioural responses:** the person feels hot and seeks **shade**, rests, uses a **fan**, removes some clothes or wears light-coloured, loose cotton clothes, drinks **cold water**, and stretches out (exposing more surface).
5. Heat loss now exceeds heat gain, so the body temperature **falls** back to 37 °C.
6. The receptors detect the normal temperature, and the hypothalamus **reduces** these responses (negative feedback).

**Note:** a lot of sweating means losing a lot of **water and salt**. This is why people must drink plenty of water (and replace salt) in hot weather, and why the kidneys produce less urine on hot days (see salt and water regulation below).

### When the body is too cold, step by step

Example: a person walking to school early on a cold harmattan morning in Jos or Kano.

1. Body temperature **falls** below 37 °C (or the skin detects cold air).
2. **Temperature receptors** in the skin and the hypothalamus detect the fall.
3. The hypothalamus sends impulses to the effectors, causing these responses:
   - **Vasoconstriction:** the arterioles supplying the surface capillaries **narrow (constrict)**, and the shunt vessels open. **Less blood** flows near the skin surface, so **less heat is lost** by radiation. The skin looks **pale** (in light-skinned people) and feels **cold**, and the fingers and toes get cold first. The warm blood is kept in the **core** of the body, where the vital organs are.
   - **Sweating stops** (or is greatly reduced), so no heat is lost by evaporation of sweat.
   - **Hair erector muscles contract:** the hairs **stand up**, trapping a thicker layer of still **air**, which **insulates** the body. In humans, this produces **goose pimples**; in furry mammals and birds (which fluff up their feathers), it is very effective.
   - **Shivering:** the hypothalamus causes rapid, involuntary **contractions of skeletal muscles**. These contractions release a lot of **heat** from respiration. Teeth may chatter.
   - **Increased metabolic rate:** the hypothalamus causes the release of more **thyroxine** (through TSH from the pituitary) and **adrenaline**, which increase the rate of respiration in cells, especially in the **liver**, so more heat is produced (see the role of hormones below).
4. **Behavioural responses:** the person feels cold and puts on **warm clothes** (sweaters, jackets and wrappers during the harmattan), sits near a fire or in the sun, drinks hot tea or *pap* (*akamu*), moves about or rubs the hands together, and **curls up** to reduce the surface exposed. Animals **huddle** together (e.g. chicks under a hen, piglets together) and birds fluff out their feathers.
5. Heat production and conservation now exceed heat loss, so the body temperature **rises** back to 37 °C.
6. The receptors detect the normal temperature, and the responses are **switched off** (negative feedback).

### Summary of responses to heat and cold

| Response | When too hot | When too cold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sweat glands** | Produce **more sweat**; evaporation cools the skin | Produce **little or no sweat** |
| **Arterioles to the skin capillaries** | **Vasodilation**: more blood near the surface, more heat lost; skin flushed | **Vasoconstriction**: less blood near the surface, less heat lost; skin pale |
| **Hair erector muscles** | **Relax**: hairs lie flat; little insulating air | **Contract**: hairs stand up; trap insulating air ("goose pimples") |
| **Skeletal muscles** | No shivering | **Shivering** produces heat |
| **Metabolic rate** (thyroxine, adrenaline) | Decreases slightly | **Increases**: more heat produced |
| **Behaviour** | Seek shade, fans, light clothes, cold drinks, rest | Warm clothes, fire, sun, hot drinks, exercise, curl up, huddle |

**Common mistake:** capillaries **do not move** up and down in the skin, and they have no muscles to widen or narrow themselves. It is the **arterioles** (small arteries) supplying them that widen or narrow, changing **how much blood flows** through the surface capillaries.

### Temperature regulation in other animals

- **Dogs** have few sweat glands (mainly on the pads of their feet), so they **pant** with their tongues out; water evaporating from the tongue and mouth cools them.
- **Pigs** have few working sweat glands and **wallow** in mud or water to cool down.
- **Elephants** flap their large, thin ears, which have many blood vessels, to lose heat.
- **Birds** have no sweat glands; they pant, hold their wings away from their bodies, and flutter their throats when hot; they fluff up their feathers when cold.
- **Cattle and goats** seek shade at midday and sweat or pant; farmers provide shade and water for livestock in the hot season.
- **Camels** let their body temperature rise during the hot day (storing heat) and lose it in the cool night, so they need to sweat less and save water.

### Fever

A **fever** is a body temperature **above normal** (usually taken as above about 37.5 to 38 °C), caused by infection or illness, such as **malaria**, typhoid or a chest infection. Chemicals released during the infection (**pyrogens**) **reset the hypothalamus's set point** to a higher temperature.

1. With the set point raised, the body "thinks" it is too cold. The person **shivers**, feels cold (has **chills** or rigors), and the skin is pale from vasoconstriction, so the body temperature rises to the new set point.
2. When the infection is treated (e.g. with antimalarial drugs) or the fever-reducing drug (e.g. paracetamol) acts, the set point returns to 37 °C. Now the body is too hot for its set point, so the person **sweats heavily** and the skin is flushed, until the temperature falls back to normal.

A moderate fever may help fight infection, but a very high fever is dangerous, especially in young children, in whom it can cause **convulsions (febrile seizures)**. Tepid sponging, fluids and paracetamol are used to bring it down, while the cause is treated.

## Salt and water regulation (osmoregulation)

**Osmoregulation** is the control of the **water content** and **salt (solute) concentration** of the body fluids, so that their **osmotic pressure** (concentration) stays constant.

### Why osmoregulation is necessary

Cells are surrounded by **selectively permeable** membranes, so water moves into or out of them by **osmosis** depending on the concentration of the fluid around them.

| If the blood and tissue fluid are... | Effect on cells (e.g. red blood cells) |
| --- | --- |
| **Too dilute** (hypotonic: too much water, too little salt) | Water **enters** the cells by osmosis; they **swell** and may **burst** (red blood cells burst: **haemolysis**) |
| **Normal** (isotonic) | No net movement of water; cells keep their normal shape |
| **Too concentrated** (hypertonic: too little water, too much salt) | Water **leaves** the cells by osmosis; they **shrink** (red blood cells become shrivelled: **crenation**) |

Both swelling and shrinking stop cells from working properly. Brain cells are especially sensitive. The concentration of salts (especially **sodium**) also affects **nerve impulses**, **muscle contraction** and **blood pressure**.

### Water balance in humans

Every day, the body gains and loses water. To keep its water content constant, the **water gained must equal the water lost**.

| Water gained (approximate, per day) | | Water lost (approximate, per day) | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drinks | About 1.5 litres | Urine | About 1.5 litres |

The body can control water loss in the **urine** much more than in sweat, breath or faeces. So the **kidneys** are the main organs of osmoregulation in mammals. The **hypothalamus** controls **thirst** and the release of **ADH**, and the **adrenal cortex** releases **aldosterone**.

### Osmoregulation by the kidney in mammals

The kidney's working unit is the **nephron**. (Its structure and how urine is formed by **ultrafiltration** and **selective reabsorption** are explained in `B5-excretion.md`.) In outline:

1. Blood is filtered at the **glomerulus**; a large volume of filtrate (water, glucose, salts, urea) enters the **Bowman's capsule**.
2. In the **proximal convoluted tubule**, all the glucose, amino acids, and most of the water and salts are **reabsorbed** into the blood.
3. The **loop of Henle** creates a high salt concentration in the medulla of the kidney, which allows water to be drawn out of the collecting ducts.
4. In the **distal convoluted tubule** and **collecting duct**, the **final amounts of salt and water** reabsorbed are adjusted, under the control of **aldosterone** (salt) and **ADH** (water).
5. The remaining fluid is **urine**, which flows to the bladder.

By changing how much water and salt are reabsorbed in step 4, the kidneys can produce **a small volume of concentrated urine** (to save water) or **a large volume of dilute urine** (to get rid of extra water).

### The role of ADH in water regulation, step by step

**ADH (antidiuretic hormone)** is made in the **hypothalamus** and released from the **posterior pituitary gland**. It makes the walls of the **distal convoluted tubules** and **collecting ducts** of the kidney **more permeable to water**, so more water is **reabsorbed** into the blood.

**When the body is short of water** (e.g. after sweating heavily on a hot day, eating salty food such as salted groundnuts, or not drinking enough):

1. The blood becomes **more concentrated** (its water content falls, so its solute concentration rises).
2. **Osmoreceptors** in the **hypothalamus** detect this.
3. The hypothalamus makes the person feel **thirsty**, so they drink.
4. It also causes the **posterior pituitary** to release **more ADH** into the blood.
5. ADH travels in the blood to the **kidneys**.
6. ADH makes the walls of the **distal convoluted tubules and collecting ducts more permeable** to water.
7. **More water is reabsorbed** from the filtrate back into the blood.
8. A **small volume of concentrated (dark yellow) urine** is produced.
9. The water content of the blood **rises back to normal**.
10. The osmoreceptors detect this, and ADH release **falls** (negative feedback).

**When the body has too much water** (e.g. after drinking a lot of water, *zobo* or soft drinks, or on a cool day with little sweating):

1. The blood becomes **more dilute** (its water content rises).
2. **Osmoreceptors** in the hypothalamus detect this.
3. The person does not feel thirsty.
4. The posterior pituitary releases **less ADH** (or none).
5. With little ADH, the walls of the distal tubules and collecting ducts become **less permeable** to water.
6. **Less water is reabsorbed**; more stays in the tubules.
7. A **large volume of dilute (pale, almost colourless) urine** is produced.
8. The water content of the blood **falls back to normal**.
9. The osmoreceptors detect this, and ADH release **rises** back to its usual level (negative feedback).

**Alcohol and caffeine** (in beer, coffee, strong tea and many energy drinks) **reduce ADH release**, so people pass more urine after drinking them and can become dehydrated.

### The role of aldosterone in salt regulation, step by step

**Aldosterone** is made by the **adrenal cortex**. It acts on the **distal convoluted tubules and collecting ducts** of the kidney, increasing the **reabsorption of sodium ions (Na⁺)** from the filtrate into the blood (and the secretion of potassium ions into the urine). Because **water follows sodium** by osmosis, aldosterone also helps keep **water** in the body and maintains **blood volume and blood pressure**.

**When the body is short of salt (sodium), or blood volume or pressure is low** (e.g. after heavy sweating, diarrhoea, vomiting or bleeding):

1. The **sodium** level in the blood falls, or the blood volume and blood pressure fall.
2. This is detected (mainly by special cells in the **kidney**, which release an enzyme called **renin**; this starts a chain of reactions in the blood that produces a hormone called **angiotensin**).
3. These signals stimulate the **adrenal cortex** to release **more aldosterone**.
4. Aldosterone travels in the blood to the **kidneys**.
5. The distal tubules and collecting ducts **reabsorb more sodium** ions into the blood.
6. **Water follows** the sodium back into the blood by osmosis.
7. **Less sodium is lost** in the urine, and blood volume and pressure **rise**.
8. When the sodium level and blood pressure return to normal, aldosterone release **falls** (negative feedback).

**When the body has too much salt** (e.g. after eating very salty food), less aldosterone is released, so **less sodium is reabsorbed**, and the extra salt is **excreted in the urine**. At the same time, the salty blood stimulates thirst and ADH release, so the person drinks water and keeps water, diluting the salt until the kidneys can remove it.

**Textbook variation:** many school textbooks simply say "aldosterone is released when the sodium level of the blood falls" without mentioning renin and angiotensin. The renin–angiotensin step is included here for completeness; you will not usually be asked about it at JAMB level.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Stimulus for release | Blood **too concentrated** (too little water), detected by osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus; also low blood volume | **Low sodium** in the blood, or **low blood volume or pressure** |
| Target | Distal convoluted tubules and collecting ducts of the kidney | Distal convoluted tubules and collecting ducts of the kidney |

| Effect on urine | **Small volume** of **concentrated** urine | Less sodium in the urine |

### Osmoregulation in fish

Fish live in water, but they face opposite problems depending on whether the water is **fresh** or **salty**.

#### Freshwater fish

Examples: ***Tilapia***, **catfish** (*Clarias*), **Nile perch**, found in Nigerian rivers, lakes, dams and fish ponds (e.g. Lake Kainji and the Niger and Benue rivers).

- The body fluids of a freshwater fish are **more concentrated** (hypertonic) than the fresh water around it.
- So **water constantly enters** the body by **osmosis**, mainly through the thin, permeable **gills** (and the lining of the mouth), and **salts are lost** by diffusion into the water.
- **The problem:** the fish is in danger of **swelling with water** and **losing salts**.

**How freshwater fish osmoregulate:**

1. They **do not drink** (or drink very little) water.
2. Their kidneys have **many large glomeruli**, so a lot of fluid is filtered from the blood.
3. Their kidney tubules **reabsorb salts** from the filtrate back into the blood.
4. They produce a **large volume of very dilute urine**, which gets rid of the extra water.
5. Special **chloride (salt) cells in the gills actively absorb salts** (sodium and chloride ions) from the water, using energy (active transport), to replace the salts lost.
6. They also obtain some salts from their **food**.
7. Their skin is covered with **scales and mucus**, which reduce the amount of water entering through the body surface.

#### Marine (sea-water) bony fish

Examples: **croakers**, **mackerel**, **tuna**, **sardines** and other fish caught off the Nigerian coast in the Atlantic Ocean.

- The body fluids of a marine bony fish are **less concentrated** (hypotonic) than sea water.
- So **water constantly leaves** the body by **osmosis**, mainly through the gills, and **salts enter** by diffusion.
- **The problem:** the fish is in danger of **dehydration** (losing water) and **gaining too much salt**.

**How marine bony fish osmoregulate:**

1. They **drink large amounts of sea water** to replace the water lost.
2. Their gut absorbs the water (together with salts).
3. Special **chloride (salt) cells in the gills actively secrete (excrete) the excess salts** (sodium and chloride ions) into the sea, using energy.
4. Their kidneys have **few, small glomeruli** (some have none), so little fluid is filtered.
5. They produce a **very small volume of urine**, saving water. Some salts (such as magnesium and sulphate ions) are excreted in this urine and in the faeces.
6. Their scales and mucus reduce water loss through the skin.

**Note:** the kidneys of bony fish **cannot** make urine more concentrated than their blood (they have no loop of Henle). That is why marine bony fish depend on their **gills** to get rid of excess salt.

**Cartilaginous fish (sharks and rays)** solve the problem differently: they keep a high concentration of **urea** in their blood, which makes their body fluids **slightly more concentrated** than sea water. So they do not lose water by osmosis and do not need to drink. Excess salt is removed by a special **rectal gland**.

**Fish that move between fresh and salt water** (e.g. salmon and eels, and some fish of brackish lagoons and estuaries) can switch their gill cells from absorbing salt to secreting it, and adjust their drinking and urine output.

| Movement of water by osmosis | Water **enters** the body | Water **leaves** the body |
| Movement of salts by diffusion | Salts are **lost** | Salts **enter** |
| Drinking | **Little or none** | **Drinks a lot** of sea water |
| Glomeruli in kidneys | **Many and large** | **Few and small** (or absent) |
| Volume of urine | **Large** | **Very small** |
| Concentration of urine | **Very dilute** | About as concentrated as the blood |

| Main danger | Swelling with water; losing salts | Dehydration; too much salt |
| Examples | *Tilapia*, *Clarias* (catfish), Nile perch | Croaker, mackerel, tuna, sardine |

### Osmoregulation in *Amoeba*: the contractile vacuole

*Amoeba* is a single-celled organism that lives in **fresh water** (ponds and puddles). (The contractile vacuole is also described in `B5-excretion.md`.)

**The problem:** the cytoplasm of *Amoeba* is **more concentrated** than the fresh water around it, and its cell membrane is selectively permeable, so **water constantly enters** the cell by **osmosis**. *Amoeba* has **no cell wall**, so if the water were not removed, the cell would swell and **burst**.

**How the contractile vacuole works, step by step:**

1. Water enters the *Amoeba* by **osmosis** through the cell membrane.
2. Small vesicles in the cytoplasm collect the **excess water** and empty it into the **contractile vacuole**.
3. The contractile vacuole **gradually fills** and grows larger (this filling stage is called **diastole**).
4. When it reaches a certain size, the vacuole moves to the **cell membrane**.
5. It **contracts** and **bursts**, releasing the water **outside** the cell (the emptying stage is called **systole**).
6. A new contractile vacuole begins to form, and the cycle repeats.
7. This process needs **energy** from respiration (ATP), so **mitochondria** are often found around the contractile vacuole.

**Evidence that the contractile vacuole is for osmoregulation:**

- If *Amoeba* is placed in **slightly salty** water (which is closer to the concentration of its cytoplasm), **less water** enters by osmosis, and the contractile vacuole fills and empties **more slowly**.
- In **distilled water**, more water enters, and it empties **faster**.
- **Marine** protozoa, whose cytoplasm has about the same concentration as sea water, usually have **no** contractile vacuole, or one that works very slowly.

Other freshwater protists, such as *Paramecium* (two contractile vacuoles with radiating canals), *Euglena* and *Chlamydomonas*, use contractile vacuoles in the same way.

### Osmoregulation in plants (in brief)

Plants also need to control their water balance, but they face different problems from animals.

1. **Cell walls prevent bursting:** when a plant cell takes in water by osmosis, its vacuole swells and pushes the cytoplasm against the **cellulose cell wall**. The wall resists further swelling, so the cell becomes firm (**turgid**) but does not burst. Turgid cells keep soft plant parts, such as leaves, firm and upright.
2. **Water loss and wilting:** if a plant loses more water by **transpiration** than it absorbs through its roots, its cells lose water and become **flaccid**, and the plant **wilts**. In severe cases, the cell contents shrink away from the wall (**plasmolysis**).
3. **Control of water loss through stomata:** in dry conditions, the hormone **abscisic acid (ABA)** causes the **stomata to close**, reducing transpiration (see `B9c-hormonal-control.md`). Many plants also close their stomata at night or in the hottest part of the day.
4. **Adaptations to habitat:**
   - **Xerophytes** (plants of dry places, e.g. cacti, *Euphorbia*, and many plants of the Sahel) have thick waxy cuticles, few or sunken stomata, small or spiny leaves, water-storage tissue and long roots (see `A3-adaptations.md`).
   - **Hydrophytes** (water plants, e.g. water lily and water lettuce) have plenty of water; they have thin cuticles, stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves, and air spaces for floating.
   - **Halophytes** (plants of salty places, e.g. the **mangroves** of the Niger Delta coast) must take in water from salty soil. Some mangroves keep most of the salt out at their roots; others (such as the white mangrove, *Avicennia*) get rid of excess salt through **salt glands** on their leaves. Their cell sap is very concentrated, so they can still absorb water by osmosis.

## The role of hormones in homeostasis

**Objective ix: relate the function of hormones in homeostasis.**

Hormones are the chemical messengers that carry the "corrective instructions" in many homeostatic control systems. Each hormone below is released in response to a change in the internal environment and acts to reverse that change (negative feedback).

### Overview of the hormones of homeostasis

| Hormone | Gland | Factor controlled | Released when... | Effect that restores the normal level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ADH** | Hypothalamus / posterior pituitary | **Water content** of blood | The blood is too concentrated (short of water) | Kidney reabsorbs more water; small volume of concentrated urine |
| **Aldosterone** | Adrenal cortex | **Sodium (salt)** level, blood volume and blood pressure | Sodium is low, or blood volume/pressure is low | Kidney reabsorbs more sodium; water follows |
| **Insulin** | Pancreas (β cells of the islets of Langerhans) | **Blood glucose** | Blood glucose is **too high** (e.g. after a meal) | Lowers blood glucose: cells take up glucose; liver and muscles store it as glycogen |
| **Glucagon** | Pancreas (α cells of the islets of Langerhans) | **Blood glucose** | Blood glucose is **too low** (e.g. during fasting or exercise) | Raises blood glucose: liver changes glycogen into glucose |
| **Thyroxine** | Thyroid (under the control of TSH from the pituitary) | **Metabolic rate** and **heat production** (body temperature) | The body is cold (over a longer period) | Increases respiration in cells, producing more heat |
| **Adrenaline** | Adrenal medulla | **Blood glucose** and **heat production** in emergencies and cold | Stress, danger, exercise, sudden cold | Raises blood glucose (glycogen → glucose); increases metabolic rate and heat production; constricts skin arterioles |

ADH and aldosterone are explained step by step above under salt and water regulation. The control of blood glucose and the role of hormones in temperature regulation are explained below.

### Insulin and glucagon: regulation of blood sugar (glucose)

**Why blood glucose must be controlled:** glucose is the main fuel for respiration. The **brain** uses glucose almost exclusively and cannot store it, so it needs a steady supply. If blood glucose falls too low (**hypoglycaemia**), a person becomes weak, confused and may faint or go into a coma. If it rises too high (**hyperglycaemia**) for long, water is drawn out of cells by osmosis, glucose is lost in the urine, and blood vessels and organs are damaged over time.

The level of blood glucose rises after a **meal** rich in carbohydrates (e.g. rice, yam, garri, bread, sweet drinks) and falls during **fasting**, **sleep** and **exercise**. The **pancreas** and **liver** work together to keep it steady.

**The liver as a glucose store:** the liver (and muscles) can change glucose into an insoluble storage carbohydrate, **glycogen**, and back again:

glucose ⇌ glycogen

- **Insulin** promotes the change of **glucose → glycogen** (storage).
- **Glucagon** (and adrenaline) promote the change of **glycogen → glucose** (release).

**When blood glucose rises too high (e.g. after eating a plate of jollof rice), step by step:**

1. Digested carbohydrates are absorbed from the small intestine, and the **blood glucose level rises** above normal.
2. The **beta (β) cells** of the **islets of Langerhans** in the pancreas detect the rise.
3. They secrete **more insulin** into the blood (and the alpha cells secrete less glucagon).
4. Insulin is carried in the blood to its target organs, especially the **liver** and **muscles**, and causes:
   - body cells (especially muscle and fat cells) to **take up more glucose** from the blood;
   - the **liver and muscles** to change glucose into **glycogen** and store it;
   - cells to use more glucose in **respiration**;
   - excess glucose to be changed into **fat** for storage.
5. The **blood glucose level falls** back to normal.
6. The pancreas detects the normal level and **reduces insulin** secretion (negative feedback).

**When blood glucose falls too low (e.g. after missing breakfast or during a long football match), step by step:**

1. Glucose is used up in respiration, and the **blood glucose level falls** below normal.
2. The **alpha (α) cells** of the islets of Langerhans detect the fall.
3. They secrete **more glucagon** into the blood (and the beta cells secrete less insulin).
4. Glucagon travels to the **liver** and causes:
   - the liver to change stored **glycogen into glucose** and release it into the blood;
   - the liver to make new glucose from **amino acids and fats**.
5. The **blood glucose level rises** back to normal.
6. The pancreas detects the normal level and **reduces glucagon** secretion (negative feedback).

**Other hormones that raise blood glucose:** **adrenaline** (in emergencies and exercise, it rapidly changes liver glycogen into glucose) and **cortisol** (during long-term stress or starvation). Insulin is the **only** hormone that lowers blood glucose.

| Feature | Insulin | Glucagon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Made by | **Beta (β) cells** of the islets of Langerhans | **Alpha (α) cells** of the islets of Langerhans |
| Released when blood glucose is | **High** | **Low** |
| Main target | Liver, muscles, fat cells (most body cells) | **Liver** |
| Effect on glycogen | Glucose → **glycogen** (storage) | Glycogen → **glucose** (release) |
| Effect on blood glucose | **Lowers** it | **Raises** it |
| Disorder if lacking | **Diabetes mellitus** | Low blood sugar (rare) |

Insulin and glucagon are a classic **antagonistic pair**: they have opposite effects, and together they keep blood glucose within narrow limits.

**Failure of blood sugar regulation:** in **diabetes mellitus**, the body makes too little insulin, or its cells do not respond to insulin, so the blood glucose stays **high** after meals and glucose appears in the urine. This is described fully in `B9c-hormonal-control.md` and summarised under disorders below.

### Thyroxine and adrenaline: regulation of body temperature

Nerves control the **fast** responses to temperature change (sweating, vasodilation and vasoconstriction, shivering, hair erection). Hormones control the **amount of heat produced** by the body's metabolism.

**Thyroxine (from the thyroid gland), step by step, in the cold:**

1. When the body is exposed to cold for some time (e.g. during the cold harmattan season), the **hypothalamus** detects the fall in temperature.
2. It releases a releasing hormone (TRH) that stimulates the **anterior pituitary** to release more **TSH**.
3. TSH stimulates the **thyroid gland** to release more **thyroxine**.
4. Thyroxine increases the **metabolic rate** of almost all body cells: they carry out more **respiration**, and much of the energy released is given off as **heat**.
5. The extra heat helps to **raise** the body temperature back to normal.
6. In warm conditions, thyroxine release is reduced, so less heat is produced.

Thyroxine acts **slowly** (over hours to days), so it is important in **long-term adjustment** to cold or warm conditions (acclimatisation), rather than in quick responses. People with too much thyroxine always feel **hot** and sweat a lot; people with too little feel **cold** (see `B9c-hormonal-control.md`).

**Adrenaline (from the adrenal medulla), step by step, in sudden cold:**

1. Sudden cold (or stress) is detected, and the hypothalamus sends impulses along **sympathetic nerves** to the **adrenal medulla**.
2. The adrenal medulla releases **adrenaline** (and noradrenaline) into the blood within seconds.
3. Adrenaline (and noradrenaline from sympathetic nerves) causes **vasoconstriction** of the arterioles in the skin, reducing heat loss.
4. Adrenaline changes **glycogen into glucose** in the liver and raises the **metabolic rate**, so cells release more energy, and therefore more **heat**.
5. These effects help to raise the body temperature quickly.

| Feature | Thyroxine | Adrenaline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gland | Thyroid | Adrenal medulla |
| Controlled by | TSH from the pituitary (and the hypothalamus) | Sympathetic nerves from the hypothalamus/brain |
| Speed of action | **Slow**, long-lasting | **Fast**, short-lived |
| Effect on heat production | Raises the **basal metabolic rate** of most cells over a long period | Quickly raises metabolic rate; releases glucose for respiration |
| Other effects in temperature control | — | Constricts skin arterioles, reducing heat loss |
| Role | Long-term adjustment to cold (acclimatisation) | Short-term, emergency response to cold or stress |

### How nerves and hormones work together in homeostasis

| Factor controlled | Nervous part | Hormonal part |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body temperature | Hypothalamus → nerves → sweat glands, skin arterioles, erector muscles, skeletal muscles (shivering) | Thyroxine and adrenaline raise heat production |
| Water content | Hypothalamus detects concentration; thirst | ADH from the posterior pituitary |
| Salt content and blood pressure | Nerves adjust the heart and blood vessels | Aldosterone from the adrenal cortex |
| Blood glucose | Nerves stimulate adrenaline release in emergencies | Insulin and glucagon from the pancreas; adrenaline; cortisol |

The **hypothalamus** is the key link between the two systems: it receives nerve impulses, contains its own receptors, sends nerve impulses to effectors, and controls the pituitary gland.

## Disorders of homeostasis

When homeostatic mechanisms are overwhelmed or fail, illness follows. The syllabus does not list these by name, but they show clearly why homeostasis matters.

### Heat stroke and heat exhaustion

**Heat exhaustion** happens when a person loses a lot of **water and salt** through heavy sweating in hot conditions (e.g. farm work under the sun, long marches, sports in the hot season), without replacing them.

- **Signs:** heavy sweating, pale and clammy skin, tiredness, weakness, headache, dizziness, nausea, muscle cramps, fast pulse; body temperature normal or a little raised.
- **First aid:** move the person to a **cool, shaded** place; lie them down with legs raised; loosen tight clothing; give **cool water** or an oral rehydration solution to drink; fan them. If not treated, it can lead to heat stroke.

**Heat stroke** is a **medical emergency** in which the body's temperature control **fails** and the core temperature rises very high (above about **40 °C**).

- **Causes:** very hot weather (especially in heat waves in northern Nigeria during March to May), hard physical work in the heat, dehydration (so sweating can no longer continue), heavy clothing, very humid air (sweat cannot evaporate), and staying in hot, poorly ventilated places such as closed vehicles. Babies, the elderly and people who are ill are at greater risk.
- **What happens:** the body cannot lose heat fast enough; sweating may stop as the body runs out of water; the hypothalamus itself is affected by the heat, and the temperature control system breaks down. High temperatures **denature enzymes** and damage the brain and other organs. (This is an example of a **positive feedback** situation: the hotter the body gets, the faster its reactions produce heat and the worse its control becomes.)
- **Signs:** very high body temperature; hot skin, which may be **dry** (no sweating) or still sweaty; **confusion**, slurred speech, strange behaviour; headache; fast, strong pulse; **fainting**, **convulsions** and **coma**. It can cause death.
- **First aid and treatment:** call for medical help at once; move the person to a **cool place**; remove excess clothing; **cool them rapidly** by wetting the skin with cool (not ice-cold) water and fanning, placing cold packs in the armpits and groin; give fluids if they are conscious and able to swallow.
- **Prevention:** drink plenty of water in hot weather; wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing; avoid hard work in the hottest part of the day; rest in the shade; never leave children or animals in a closed car.

### Hypothermia

**Hypothermia** is a fall in the core body temperature **below about 35 °C**, when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it.

- **Causes:** being in cold water for a long time (e.g. after a boat capsizes or a flood); being outdoors in cold, wet, windy weather in wet clothes (e.g. on the Jos, Mambilla or Obudu plateaus on cold nights); old people in cold rooms, as they produce less heat; newborn babies, especially **premature** and low-birth-weight babies, who have a large surface area compared with their volume and little fat; and alcohol, which causes vasodilation and increases heat loss.
- **What happens:** at first, the body responds by **shivering** and **vasoconstriction**. As the temperature falls further, shivering stops, enzymes and nerve impulses slow down, and the brain and heart stop working properly.
- **Signs:** shivering (early), cold, pale skin, slurred speech, clumsiness, drowsiness and confusion, slow breathing and pulse; in severe cases, unconsciousness and death.
- **First aid:** move the person to a **warm, sheltered** place; remove wet clothes and wrap them in dry **blankets** (covering the head); give **warm, sweet drinks** if they are conscious; warm them **gradually** (body-to-body contact is useful for a baby); do not rub the limbs or give alcohol; seek medical help.
- **Prevention in newborn babies:** keep the room warm, dry the baby immediately after birth, place the baby skin-to-skin on the mother's chest (**kangaroo mother care**), cover the head, and delay the first bath.

### Diabetes mellitus

**Diabetes mellitus** is a failure of **blood glucose regulation** because of lack of **insulin** or failure of cells to respond to insulin (see `B9c-hormonal-control.md` for full details).

- **Effect on homeostasis:** after a meal, blood glucose rises and stays **high**, because cells cannot take up glucose and the liver does not store it as glycogen. The kidneys cannot reabsorb all the glucose, so **glucose appears in the urine**; it takes water with it, so the person passes a lot of urine, becomes **dehydrated** and very **thirsty**. Because cells are starved of glucose, the body breaks down fat and protein, causing **weight loss** and tiredness.
- **Detection:** glucose in urine (Benedict's test or test strips); blood glucose tests.
- **Control:** **insulin injections** (type 1); healthy diet, exercise, weight control and tablets (type 2). People with diabetes must watch for both **high** blood sugar and **low** blood sugar (hypoglycaemia from too much insulin or missed meals).

### Dehydration

**Dehydration** is a loss of too much **water** (and usually **salts**) from the body, so the body fluids become too concentrated and the blood volume falls.

- **Causes:**
  - **Diarrhoea and vomiting**, e.g. from **cholera**, gastroenteritis (stomach infections, often from contaminated food and water), food poisoning; these are the most dangerous causes, especially in young children. **Cholera** outbreaks, which cause severe watery diarrhoea ("rice-water stools"), occur in parts of Nigeria, especially in the rainy season and where water and sanitation are poor.
  - Heavy **sweating** in hot weather or during fever.
  - Not drinking enough water.
  - Uncontrolled **diabetes** (mellitus or insipidus).
  - Excessive use of alcohol.
- **Signs:** thirst; dry mouth, lips and tongue; passing little, **dark yellow** urine; tiredness; headache; dizziness. In severe dehydration: very little or no urine, **sunken eyes**, loss of skin elasticity (a pinch of skin on the belly goes back slowly), a **sunken fontanelle** (soft spot on the head) in babies, rapid weak pulse, drowsiness, and finally shock and death.
- **Homeostatic response:** the hypothalamus detects concentrated blood, causes **thirst** and releases **ADH**; aldosterone is released; the kidneys produce a small volume of concentrated urine. But these responses cannot replace water and salts that are being lost faster than they are taken in. Lost fluid and salts must be **replaced**.

#### Oral rehydration solution (ORS)

**Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)** is the giving of an **oral rehydration solution (ORS)** by mouth to replace the water and salts lost in diarrhoea and vomiting. It is simple, cheap and has saved millions of lives, especially of children.

**What ORS contains:** clean water, **sodium chloride (salt)**, **glucose (sugar)**, and in the standard sachets, potassium chloride and sodium citrate.

**Why both salt and sugar are needed:** in the small intestine, **glucose and sodium are absorbed together** by special carriers in the cell membranes of the lining. When glucose and sodium are absorbed, **water follows by osmosis**. So a drink containing both sugar and salt is absorbed **much better** than plain water, even during diarrhoea, and it replaces the lost salts as well as the water.

**How to use ORS:**

1. Use the **ORS sachets** sold in pharmacies and patent medicine stores, or given free at health centres. Dissolve one sachet in the **exact volume of clean water** printed on the packet (usually 1 litre). Use boiled and cooled water if possible.
2. Give small amounts often: **sips from a cup** or spoon for children, after every loose stool and in between. If the child vomits, wait 10 minutes and give it more slowly.
3. Continue **breastfeeding** and normal feeding.
4. For children with diarrhoea, health workers also give **zinc** tablets for 10 to 14 days, which reduce the length and severity of diarrhoea.
5. Seek medical help at once if the person cannot drink, has blood in the stool, is very drowsy, keeps vomiting, or does not improve. Severe dehydration needs **intravenous fluids (a drip)** in hospital.

**Home-made salt-sugar solution:** if ORS sachets are not available, a home-made solution can be used: a commonly recommended recipe is **six level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt dissolved in one litre of clean drinking water**.

**Textbook variation and safety note:** different books and health programmes give slightly different home-made recipes and measuring methods. The amounts must be measured **carefully**: **too much salt is dangerous**, especially for babies, and too much sugar can make diarrhoea worse. Taste it before use: it should taste no saltier than tears. Prepared ORS should be used within 24 hours and then thrown away. Whenever possible, use the standard ORS sachets.

**Prevention of dehydration from diarrhoea:** safe drinking water (boiled, filtered or treated), hand washing with soap after using the toilet and before eating or preparing food, proper toilets, safe food hygiene, exclusive breastfeeding of babies for the first six months, and vaccination (e.g. against rotavirus and measles).

### Summary of disorders of homeostasis

| Disorder | Factor out of control | Main cause | Key sign | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Heat stroke** | Body temperature too **high** (above about 40 °C) | Heat and dehydration overwhelm temperature control | Very hot skin, confusion, collapse | Rapid cooling; emergency medical care |
| **Hypothermia** | Body temperature too **low** (below about 35 °C) | Cold, wet conditions; small babies and old people at risk | Shivering, then drowsiness, slow pulse | Gradual warming, dry blankets, warm drinks |
| **Diabetes mellitus** | Blood glucose too **high** | Lack of insulin or resistance to it | Glucose in urine, thirst, much urine | Insulin, diet, exercise, tablets |
| **Diabetes insipidus** | Water content of blood | Lack of ADH | Very large volumes of dilute urine | Synthetic ADH |
| **Dehydration** | Water and salt content too **low** | Diarrhoea, vomiting, heavy sweating | Thirst, dark urine, sunken eyes | **ORS**; IV fluids if severe |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Homeostasis | The maintenance of a constant internal environment within narrow limits |
| Internal environment | The blood plasma and tissue fluid that surround the body's cells |
| Tissue fluid | Fluid that leaks out of capillaries and bathes the cells |
| Set point | The normal value around which a factor is kept, e.g. 37 °C for body temperature |
| Negative feedback | A control mechanism in which a change from normal causes responses that reverse it |
| Positive feedback | A mechanism in which a change causes responses that increase it further, e.g. in childbirth |
| Receptor | Structure that detects a change in a factor |
| Effector | Organ (muscle or gland) that carries out the corrective response |
| Endotherm (homoiotherm) | An animal that produces its own body heat and keeps a constant body temperature, e.g. birds and mammals |
| Ectotherm (poikilotherm) | An animal whose body temperature changes with the surroundings, e.g. lizards and fish |
| Vasodilation | Widening of arterioles, increasing blood flow near the skin surface and heat loss |
| Vasoconstriction | Narrowing of arterioles, reducing blood flow near the skin surface and heat loss |
| Shivering | Rapid involuntary contraction of skeletal muscles that produces heat |
| Hypothalamus | Part of the brain that acts as the control centre for temperature and water balance |
| Osmoregulation | The control of the water and salt concentration of the body fluids |
| Osmoreceptors | Receptors in the hypothalamus that detect the water concentration of the blood |
| ADH (antidiuretic hormone) | Hormone that increases water reabsorption by the kidney, producing concentrated urine |
| Aldosterone | Hormone from the adrenal cortex that increases sodium reabsorption by the kidney |
| Insulin | Hormone from the pancreas that lowers blood glucose |
| Glucagon | Hormone from the pancreas that raises blood glucose by changing glycogen into glucose |
| Glycogen | Storage carbohydrate made from glucose in the liver and muscles |
| Contractile vacuole | Organelle in freshwater protists that collects and expels excess water |
| Chloride (salt) cells | Cells in fish gills that actively absorb or secrete salts |
| Heat stroke | Dangerous rise in body temperature when temperature control fails |
| Hypothermia | Dangerous fall in core body temperature below about 35 °C |
| Dehydration | Excessive loss of water (and salts) from the body |
| Oral rehydration solution (ORS) | A solution of salts and glucose in water, drunk to replace water and salts lost in diarrhoea |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Homeostasis keeps conditions within narrow limits,** not perfectly fixed. Negative feedback reverses changes; positive feedback (e.g. childbirth) does not maintain homeostasis.
- **The hypothalamus is the control centre** for both body temperature and water balance. It is not the pituitary, although the pituitary releases ADH.
- **Vasodilation cools; vasoconstriction keeps heat in.** Arterioles widen or narrow; capillaries do not move up or down.
- **Hairs stand up in the cold** (trapping air), and **lie flat in the heat**. Shivering happens only in the cold.
- **Sweating cools the body only when the sweat evaporates.** In very humid air, cooling by sweating is poor.
- **Endotherms vs ectotherms:** birds and mammals are endotherms; fish, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates are ectotherms. Lizards control their temperature mainly by behaviour (basking and seeking shade).
- **ADH:** more ADH = more water reabsorbed = **less, more concentrated** urine. Short of water → more ADH. Too much water → less ADH.
- **Aldosterone acts on sodium (salt), and water follows;** ADH acts on water directly.
- **Insulin lowers blood glucose** (glucose → glycogen); **glucagon raises it** (glycogen → glucose). Don't confuse glucagon with glycogen. The liver is the main store of glycogen.
- **Freshwater fish:** water enters; no drinking; large volume of dilute urine; gills absorb salt. **Marine bony fish:** water leaves; drink sea water; little urine; gills excrete salt.
- ***Amoeba*'s contractile vacuole removes excess water** that enters by osmosis; it works faster in more dilute water and needs energy.
- **Thyroxine** raises metabolic rate slowly (long-term); **adrenaline** acts fast (short-term).
- **ORS contains both salt and sugar** because glucose helps sodium, and therefore water, to be absorbed in the intestine.

## Quick summary

1. Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment (blood plasma and tissue fluid) within narrow limits; it keeps temperature, water and salt content, blood glucose, pH and CO₂ steady so that cells and enzymes can work.
2. Homeostasis works mainly by negative feedback: receptors detect a change, a control centre (often the hypothalamus) sends messages by nerves or hormones, and effectors reverse the change. The skin, kidneys, liver, hypothalamus, pituitary, pancreas and adrenal glands are the main organs involved.
3. Endotherms (birds and mammals) keep a constant body temperature by internal heat production and physiological control; ectotherms (fish, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates) depend on the surroundings and control temperature mainly by behaviour.
4. When the body is too hot, sweating increases, skin arterioles dilate and hairs lie flat; when too cold, arterioles constrict, hairs stand up, shivering occurs and thyroxine and adrenaline raise heat production; behaviour helps in both cases.
5. The kidneys regulate salt and water: ADH (from the posterior pituitary) increases water reabsorption when the blood is too concentrated, and aldosterone (from the adrenal cortex) increases sodium reabsorption when sodium or blood pressure is low.
6. Freshwater fish gain water and lose salts, so they do not drink, produce much dilute urine and absorb salts through their gills; marine bony fish lose water and gain salts, so they drink sea water, produce little urine and excrete salts through their gills; *Amoeba* expels excess water with its contractile vacuole; plants rely on cell walls, stomata and ABA.
7. Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose and the liver store it as glycogen; glucagon raises blood glucose by making the liver change glycogen into glucose.
8. Failure of homeostasis causes disorders such as heat stroke, hypothermia, diabetes mellitus and dehydration; dehydration from diarrhoea is treated with oral rehydration solution containing salt and sugar.

## Practice questions

**1. Homeostasis can best be defined as the**

- A. removal of metabolic wastes from the body
- B. maintenance of a constant internal environment
- C. coordination of body activities by nerves
- D. production of heat by the liver

**Answer: B.** Homeostasis is keeping the internal environment (temperature, water, salts, glucose, pH) constant within narrow limits. Excretion (option A) is only one part of it.

**2. Which of the following happens in the skin of a person on a very hot day?**

- A. Vasoconstriction of the skin arterioles
- B. Contraction of the hair erector muscles
- C. Shivering
- D. Increased sweating

**Answer: D.** On a hot day, more sweat is produced, and its evaporation cools the body. Vasoconstriction, raised hairs and shivering all help to keep heat in, so they happen in the cold.

**3. The part of the brain that acts as the control centre for body temperature is the**

- A. hypothalamus
- B. cerebellum
- C. medulla oblongata
- D. olfactory lobe

**Answer: A.** The hypothalamus contains temperature receptors that monitor the blood and controls sweating, shivering and blood flow in the skin.

**4. Which of these animals is an endotherm?**

- A. *Agama* lizard
- B. *Tilapia*
- C. Domestic fowl
- D. Toad

**Answer: C.** Birds and mammals are endotherms (homoiotherms). Lizards, fish and toads are ectotherms whose body temperature changes with their surroundings.

**5. When a person is short of water, the posterior pituitary releases more ADH, which causes the kidneys to produce**

- A. a large volume of dilute urine
- B. urine containing glucose
- C. a small volume of concentrated urine
- D. urine with no urea

**Answer: C.** ADH makes the collecting ducts more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and less, more concentrated urine is produced.

**6. The hormone that increases the reabsorption of sodium ions by the kidney tubules is**

- A. insulin
- B. aldosterone
- C. thyroxine
- D. glucagon

**Answer: B.** Aldosterone from the adrenal cortex increases sodium reabsorption in the distal tubules and collecting ducts; water follows the sodium by osmosis.

**7. A freshwater fish such as *Tilapia* regulates its water balance by**

- A. drinking large amounts of water and producing little urine
- B. secreting salts through its gills and producing concentrated urine
- C. retaining urea in its blood
- D. producing a large volume of dilute urine and absorbing salts through its gills

**Answer: D.** Water constantly enters a freshwater fish by osmosis, so it drinks little, produces a lot of dilute urine and actively absorbs salts through its gills. Option A is wrong; option B describes a marine fish's gills; option C describes sharks.

**8. When the blood glucose level rises after a meal, the pancreas secretes**

- A. insulin, which causes the liver to convert glucose to glycogen
- B. glucagon, which causes the liver to convert glycogen to glucose
- C. adrenaline, which raises the blood glucose level
- D. insulin, which causes the liver to convert glycogen to glucose

**Answer: A.** Insulin from the beta cells lowers blood glucose by causing cells to take up glucose and the liver and muscles to store it as glycogen.

**9. If an *Amoeba* is transferred from fresh water to a slightly salty solution, its contractile vacuole will**

- A. burst
- B. empty more slowly
- C. empty more quickly
- D. stop moving to the cell membrane

**Answer: B.** In a slightly salty solution, less water enters the cell by osmosis, so the contractile vacuole fills and empties more slowly.

**10. Oral rehydration solution contains both glucose and salt because**

- A. glucose kills the germs that cause diarrhoea
- B. salt makes the solution taste better
- C. glucose provides energy to stop vomiting
- D. glucose helps the absorption of sodium, and water follows by osmosis

**Answer: D.** In the small intestine, glucose and sodium are absorbed together, and water follows them by osmosis, so the solution replaces both water and salts more effectively than plain water.
