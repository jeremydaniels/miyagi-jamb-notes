# JAMB Accounting Notes: Manufacturing Accounts

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A, Topic 10 of the JAMB Principles of Accounts syllabus: Manufacturing Accounts. It has four main content items:

*   **a. Purpose of manufacturing account**
*   **b. Cost classification**
*   **c. Cost apportionment**
*   **d. Preparation of manufacturing account**

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1.  Identify the reason for preparing a manufacturing account.
2.  Calculate prime cost, overhead cost, production cost and total cost.
3.  Determine the basis of cost apportionment among production, administration, selling and distribution.

## Purpose of a manufacturing account

**Objective i: identify the reason for preparing manufacturing account.**
**Content a: Purpose of manufacturing account.**

Most trading businesses (like a provision store in Mushin) buy finished goods and sell them at a profit. Their final accounts start directly with a Trading Account to find the Gross Profit.

However, a manufacturing business (like a bakery in Kano or a furniture maker in Benin City) does not just buy finished goods. It buys raw materials and turns them into finished goods before selling them. Before such a business can find its Gross Profit, it must first find out exactly **how much it cost to make the goods**.

A **Manufacturing Account** is prepared to:
1.  **Ascertain the cost of production:** It calculates the total cost of making the finished goods during the accounting year.
2.  **Compare costs:** It allows management to compare the cost of producing goods internally against the cost of buying similar finished goods from an outside supplier.
3.  **Fix selling prices:** Management needs to know the exact cost of production to set a profitable selling price.
4.  **Control factory costs:** By separating factory expenses from office (administrative) expenses, the business can monitor and reduce waste in the factory.

Once the Manufacturing Account determines the **Cost of Production**, this figure is transferred to the Trading Account, where it takes the place of "Purchases".

## Cost classification

**Objective ii: calculate prime cost, overhead cost, production cost and total cost.**
**Content b: Cost classification.**

To prepare a manufacturing account, you must group (classify) costs correctly. Costs are divided into **Direct Costs** and **Indirect Costs (Overheads)**.

### 1. Direct Costs (Prime Cost)
Direct costs are expenses that can be easily and directly traced to the creation of a single unit of a product. The sum of all direct costs is called the **Prime Cost**.

$ \text{Prime Cost} = \text{Direct Materials} + \text{Direct Labour} + \text{Direct Expenses} $

*   **Direct Materials:** The raw materials that actually become part of the finished product. For a tailor in Aba, this is the Ankara fabric. For a bakery, it is flour, yeast, and sugar.
    *   *Calculation:* Cost of raw materials consumed = Opening Stock of Raw Materials + Purchases of Raw Materials + Carriage Inwards – Returns Outwards – Closing Stock of Raw Materials.
*   **Direct Labour (Direct Wages):** The wages paid to the workers who are directly involved in making the product (e.g., the tailors sewing the clothes, the bakers mixing the dough, machine operators).
*   **Direct Expenses:** Other expenses directly linked to a specific product. These are rare but often tested in exams. A common example is **royalties** (paying a fee to the owner of a special design or recipe for every unit produced) or the hire of a special piece of machinery used for just one job.

### 2. Indirect Costs (Factory Overheads)
Indirect costs are factory expenses that cannot be easily traced to a single unit of product. They are necessary for production but are shared across all units made. The sum of all indirect factory costs is the **Overhead Cost**.

*   **Indirect Materials:** Small items where it is impossible to calculate exactly how much goes into one unit, such as lubricating oil for factory machines, cleaning materials, or thread used in a large garment factory.
*   **Indirect Labour:** Wages paid to factory staff who do not make the products themselves, such as factory supervisors, security guards at the factory gate, and factory cleaners.
*   **Indirect Expenses:** General factory running costs, such as factory rent, factory electricity (power), depreciation of factory plant and machinery, and factory insurance.

When you add the Overhead Cost to the Prime Cost, you get the **Total Factory Cost**.

$ \text{Total Factory Cost} = \text{Prime Cost} + \text{Factory Overheads} $

### 3. Adjusting for Work-in-Progress (WIP) to find Production Cost
**Work-in-Progress (WIP)** refers to partly finished goods. At the end of the year, a bakery will have some dough that hasn't been baked yet. This is the Closing WIP.

To find the actual cost of goods that were completely finished this year (the **Production Cost**), we must adjust the Total Factory Cost:
1.  **Add Opening WIP:** These are goods started last year but finished this year.
2.  **Subtract Closing WIP:** These are goods started this year but not yet finished, so their cost shouldn't be included in this year's completed goods.

$ \text{Cost of Production} = \text{Total Factory Cost} + \text{Opening WIP} - \text{Closing WIP} $

### 4. Total Cost
The syllabus requires you to calculate **Total Cost**. In a manufacturing business, while "Cost of Production" stops at the factory door, the business still has to run an office and sell the goods.

To find the final **Total Cost (of Sales)**, we take the Cost of Goods Sold (which includes the production cost and stock of finished goods) and add the operating expenses from the Profit or Loss Account.

$ \text{Total Cost of Sales} = \text{Cost of Goods Sold} + \text{Administrative Overheads} + \text{Selling \& Distribution Overheads} $

## Cost apportionment

**Objective iii: determine the basis of cost apportionment among production, administration, selling and distribution.**
**Content c: Cost apportionment.**

Many expenses in a business are shared. For example, a business might rent one large building where the factory is at the back and the administrative office is at the front. The landlord issues one rent receipt for ₦60,000. 

We cannot charge all ₦60,000 to the Manufacturing Account (because the office uses part of the building), nor can we charge it all to the Profit or Loss Account. We must **apportion** (split) the shared cost using a fair and logical basis.

Here are the standard bases of apportionment tested by JAMB:

| Shared Expense | Basis of Apportionment | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rent, Rates, Heating, General Lighting, Building Insurance** | **Floor Area (Square meters)** | These costs relate to the physical space occupied by each department. |
| **Canteen expenses, Medical welfare, Staff recreation** | **Number of employees** | These are personnel costs, so they are split based on how many workers are in each department. |
| **Depreciation of equipment, Insurance of machinery** | **Book value (or Cost) of assets** | The higher the value of the machinery a department holds, the higher its share of depreciation and insurance. |
| **Power / Electricity for machines** | **Meter readings or Horsepower** | Machines that require more power (higher horsepower) should bear more of the electricity cost. |

### Worked Example: Cost Apportionment
Alhaji Musa Bakery pays a total rent of ₦60,000 for the year. The total floor area of the building is 600 square meters. The factory occupies 400 sq m, and the office occupies 200 sq m. How much rent goes to the Manufacturing Account, and how much to the Profit or Loss Account?

**Step 1:** State the basis of apportionment. Rent is apportioned by **floor area**.
**Step 2:** Find the fractions for each department.
*   Factory fraction = 400 / 600 = $ \frac{2}{3} $
*   Office fraction = 200 / 600 = $ \frac{1}{3} $

**Step 3:** Calculate the apportioned amounts.
*   **Factory Rent** = $ \frac{2}{3} \times ₦60,000 = ₦40,000 $. (This goes to Factory Overheads).
*   **Office Rent** = $ \frac{1}{3} \times ₦60,000 = ₦20,000 $. (This goes to the Profit or Loss Account).

## Preparation of a manufacturing account

**Content d: Preparation of manufacturing account.**

The Manufacturing Account is usually prepared in a vertical statement format, though it can also be prepared as a T-account (with all costs debited, and closing WIP and Production Cost credited). The vertical format is best for seeing the step-by-step calculation from Prime Cost to Production Cost.

Let's look at a full worked example for **Alhaji Musa Bakery** for the year ended 31st December 2023.

**Given Figures:**
*   **Raw Materials (Flour, Sugar, Yeast):** Opening stock ₦45,000; Purchases ₦250,000; Carriage inwards ₦15,000; Closing stock ₦30,000.
*   **Direct Labour & Expenses:** Bakers' wages ₦120,000; Royalties paid on a special bread recipe ₦20,000.
*   **Factory Overheads:** Factory rent (apportioned) ₦40,000; Depreciation of baking plant ₦35,000; Indirect wages (cleaners/security) ₦25,000; Factory power ₦18,000.
*   **Work-in-Progress (WIP):** Opening WIP ₦12,000; Closing WIP ₦17,000.
*   **Finished Goods (Bread):** Opening stock ₦65,000; Closing stock ₦50,000; Sales ₦800,000.
*   **Office & Selling Expenses:** Admin salaries ₦60,000; Office rent (apportioned) ₦20,000; Selling expenses ₦35,000; Depreciation of office equipment ₦10,000.

> **Note on Manufacturing Profit (Market Value Transfer):** 
> In advanced questions, a business may transfer goods from the factory to the trading department at *market value* rather than at cost, to see if the factory is operating efficiently. 
> Market Value = Cost of Production + Manufacturing Profit (a percentage mark-up). 
> If this happens, the "Manufacturing Profit" is added in the Manufacturing Account (increasing the transfer price) and then credited to the Profit or Loss Account as a gain. However, for standard UTME questions, you will generally transfer goods strictly at the Cost of Production as shown in the example above.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct Costs** | Costs that can be easily and directly traced to a specific unit of a product (e.g., raw materials). |
| **Indirect Costs (Overheads)** | Factory costs that cannot be traced to a single unit, but are shared across all production (e.g., factory rent). |
| **Prime Cost** | The sum of all direct costs (Direct Materials + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses). |
| **Production Cost** | Total Factory Cost adjusted for Work-in-Progress. This is the final cost transferred to the Trading Account. |
| **Work-in-Progress (WIP)** | Semi-finished goods that are still on the production line at the end of the accounting period. |
| **Apportionment** | The splitting of shared expenses (like rent or electricity) between the factory and the administrative office using a logical basis. |
| **Royalties** | A fee paid per unit produced to the owner of a patent, recipe, or copyright. It is a Direct Expense. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Common mistake:** Treating **Carriage Outwards** as a manufacturing or purchasing expense. Carriage outwards is the cost of delivering finished goods to customers. It is a **selling and distribution expense** and goes straight to the Profit or Loss Account.
*   **Common mistake:** Putting Finished Goods in the Manufacturing Account. Finished goods belong *only* in the Trading Account. The Manufacturing Account deals with Raw Materials and Work-in-Progress.
*   **WIP Rule:** Always **ADD Opening WIP** and **SUBTRACT Closing WIP**. Remember, closing balances represent things not yet used or finished, so they must be removed from this year's cost.
*   **Order of calculations:** Do not adjust for WIP until you have added the Factory Overheads. WIP is adjusted against the *Total Factory Cost*, not the Prime Cost.
*   **Apportionment questions:** If JAMB asks you to apportion an expense, make sure you answer for the specific department they ask for. If they ask for "Office Rent," do not accidentally select the answer for "Factory Rent."

## Quick summary

1.  A Manufacturing Account is prepared to ascertain the exact **Cost of Production** of goods before they are transferred to the Trading Account.
2.  **Prime Cost** is the total of Direct Materials Consumed, Direct Labour, and Direct Expenses.
3.  **Total Factory Cost** is calculated by adding Factory Overheads (indirect materials, indirect labour, indirect expenses) to the Prime Cost.
4.  **Cost of Production** is the Total Factory Cost plus Opening Work-in-Progress, minus Closing Work-in-Progress.
5.  Shared expenses are apportioned fairly: Rent and rates by floor area; canteen costs by number of employees; depreciation by asset book value.
6.  The final Cost of Production replaces "Purchases" in the Trading Account.
7.  **Total Cost (of Sales)** combines the Cost of Goods Sold with all Administrative, Selling, and Distribution expenses from the Profit or Loss Account.

## Practice questions

**1. A manufacturing account is primarily prepared to ascertain the**
- A. gross profit on goods manufactured
- B. cost of raw materials consumed
- C. net profit of the factory
- D. cost of production of goods

**Answer: D.** The main purpose of the manufacturing account is to calculate the cost of production, which is then transferred to the trading account.

**2. Which of the following is classified as a direct expense in a manufacturing account?**
- A. Depreciation of factory machinery
- B. Factory cleaner's wages
- C. Royalties paid per unit produced
- D. Carriage outwards

**Answer: C.** Royalties are directly tied to the specific product being made, making them a direct expense. Depreciation and cleaner's wages are factory overheads, and carriage outwards is a selling expense.

**3. The most suitable basis for apportioning factory rent between production and administration departments is**
- A. number of employees
- B. book value of machinery
- C. units produced
- D. floor area occupied

**Answer: D.** Rent is a space-related cost, so it is apportioned based on the square meters (floor area) occupied by each department.

**4. Use the following information to answer the question:**
Opening stock of raw materials: ₦20,000
Purchases of raw materials: ₦60,000
Carriage inwards: ₦3,000
Returns outwards: ₦2,000
Closing stock of raw materials: ₦15,000
**What is the cost of raw materials consumed?**
- A. ₦66,000
- B. ₦68,000
- C. ₦70,000
- D. ₦96,000

**Answer: A.** 
Step 1: Opening stock (20,000) + Purchases (60,000) + Carriage inwards (3,000) = 83,000.
Step 2: Deduct returns outwards (2,000) = 81,000.
Step 3: Deduct closing stock (15,000) = ₦66,000. Option D is incorrect because it adds closing stock instead of subtracting it.

**5. Which of the equations correctly defines Prime Cost?**
- A. Direct materials + Direct labour + Factory overheads
- B. Direct materials + Direct labour + Direct expenses
- C. Total factory cost + Opening WIP - Closing WIP
- D. Raw materials consumed + Indirect wages

**Answer: B.** Prime cost is the sum of all direct costs: direct materials, direct labour, and direct expenses. Option C describes the Cost of Production.

**6. Use the following information to calculate the Prime Cost:**
Raw materials consumed: ₦110,000
Direct wages: ₦40,000
Indirect wages: ₦10,000
Factory power: ₦8,000
- A. ₦110,000
- B. ₦150,000
- C. ₦160,000
- D. ₦168,000

**Answer: B.** 
Prime Cost = Raw materials consumed (₦110,000) + Direct wages (₦40,000) = ₦150,000. Indirect wages and factory power are overheads, so they are not included in prime cost. Option C incorrectly includes indirect wages.

**7. Following from Question 6, if Opening Work-in-Progress is ₦10,000 and Closing Work-in-Progress is ₦12,000, what is the Cost of Production?**
- A. ₦150,000
- B. ₦166,000
- C. ₦168,000
- D. ₦170,000

**Answer: B.** 
Step 1: Find Total Factory Cost = Prime Cost (₦150,000) + Overheads (₦10,000 + ₦8,000) = ₦168,000.
Step 2: Add Opening WIP (₦10,000) = ₦178,000.
Step 3: Subtract Closing WIP (₦12,000) = ₦166,000. Option C is the Total Factory Cost before adjusting for WIP.

**8. A business pays a total electricity bill of ₦120,000. The factory uses machinery with a total of 3,000 horsepower, while the administrative office uses equipment totaling 1,000 horsepower. How much of the electricity bill should be apportioned to the factory?**
- A. ₦30,000
- B. ₦40,000
- C. ₦90,000
- D. ₦120,000

**Answer: C.** 
Step 1: Total horsepower = 3,000 + 1,000 = 4,000.
Step 2: Factory fraction = 3,000 / 4,000 = $ \frac{3}{4} $.
Step 3: Factory electricity = $ \frac{3}{4} \times ₦120,000 = ₦90,000 $. 

**9. In a manufacturing business, the closing stock of finished goods is**
- A. subtracted in the manufacturing account
- B. added in the manufacturing account
- C. subtracted in the trading account
- D. debited to the profit or loss account

**Answer: C.** Finished goods are dealt with entirely in the Trading Account. The closing stock of finished goods is deducted from the cost of goods available for sale to find the Cost of Goods Sold.

**10. A business has a Cost of Production of ₦400,000. The opening stock of finished goods is ₦50,000 and the closing stock of finished goods is ₦30,000. If administrative expenses are ₦45,000 and selling expenses are ₦25,000, calculate the Total Cost of Sales.**
- A. ₦490,000
- B. ₦470,000
- C. ₦420,000
- D. ₦550,000

**Answer: A.** 
Step 1: Find Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) = Opening finished goods (50,000) + Cost of Production (400,000) - Closing finished goods (30,000) = ₦420,000.
Step 2: Find Total Cost of Sales = COGS (420,000) + Admin expenses (45,000) + Selling expenses (25,000) = ₦490,000. Option C is only the COGS, missing the operating expenses. Option B incorrectly forgets to adjust for finished goods inventory.
