## Differentiating public and private sector accounting

**Objective i: differentiate between public and private sector accounting.**

The **public sector** refers to all organizations created, owned, and financed by the government on behalf of the citizens. This includes the Federal Government, State Governments, Local Governments (like Mushin or Aba South Local Government), and government-owned agencies and parastatals. 

The **private sector** consists of businesses owned by individuals or groups of private citizens, ranging from a sole trader's provision store to a large public limited company listed on the Nigerian Exchange Group (NGX).

Accounting in these two sectors differs significantly because their primary goals are completely different.

### Comparison Table: Public vs. Private Sector Accounting

| Feature | Public Sector Accounting | Private Sector Accounting |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Motive** | To provide public services, ensure welfare, and demonstrate accountability (no profit motive). | To maximize profit and increase shareholders' wealth. |
| **Ownership** | Owned by the citizens/public as a whole. | Owned by individuals, partners, or shareholders. |
| **Source of Finance** | Primarily funded by taxes, royalties, fines, and government borrowing. | Funded by owners' capital, retained profits, shares, and bank loans. |
| **Basis of Accounting** | Traditionally uses the **cash basis** or modified cash basis (though IPSAS is shifting it to accrual). | Strictly uses the **accrual basis** to match revenues and expenses accurately. |
| **Budgeting Role** | The budget is a strict legal document (Appropriation Act). Spending beyond it without approval is an offense. | The budget is a flexible internal planning tool; changes do not require a legal act. |
| **Final Accounts Prepared** | Consolidated Revenue Fund, Capital Development Fund, Statement of Assets and Liabilities. | Statement of Profit or Loss, Statement of Financial Position, Cash Flow Statement. |
| **Audit** | Audited by the Auditor-General (an independent constitutional officer). | Audited by private external audit firms appointed by shareholders. |

> **Common mistake:** Students often think government agencies prepare an "Income Statement" or "Statement of Profit or Loss." Government accounts are designed to show how budgeted funds were spent (stewardship), not to calculate a profit.

## Accounting basis in the public sector

**Content a: Accounting basis in public sector.**

An "accounting basis" is the set of rules that determines *when* financial transactions are recognized and recorded in the books. The public sector uses distinct bases depending on the specific government's policies and level of modernization.

1. **Cash Basis:** In a pure cash basis, revenue is recorded only when actual cash is received, and expenditure is recorded only when cash is paid out. It ignores amounts owed to the government (debtors) or amounts the government owes (creditors). 
   * *Advantage:* Very simple to understand and implement.
   * *Disadvantage:* It does not show a true picture of the government's total liabilities or the value of its long-term assets. This was the traditional basis used by the Nigerian government for decades.
2. **Accrual Basis:** Revenue is recognized when it is earned (even if cash has not been received), and expenditure is recognized when it is incurred (even if cash has not been paid).
   * Under the ongoing global reforms (IPSAS), the Nigerian public sector is transitioning to the accrual basis to improve transparency.
3. **Modified Cash Basis:** The books are kept on a cash basis during the year, but at the end of the year, they are held open for a short period (e.g., 30 days) to record cash receipts and payments that relate to the just-concluded financial year.
4. **Modified Accrual Basis:** Revenue is recognized on a cash basis (since it is hard to guarantee tax collection until it is received), but expenditure is recognized on an accrual basis (when the liability is incurred).

## Sources of government revenue

**Content b: Sources of government revenue.**

Government operations require massive funding. The government generates income from multiple channels. In Nigeria, revenues collected by the Federal Government are generally paid into the **Federation Account** and then shared among the three tiers of government (Federal, State, and Local) by the Federation Account Allocation Committee (FAAC). 

Revenues are broadly divided into Tax Revenues and Non-Tax Revenues.

### Identifying government revenue sources

**Objective ii: identify the sources of government revenue.**

1. **Tax Revenues:**
   * **Direct Taxes:** Taxes levied directly on the income or wealth of individuals and companies. Examples include Personal Income Tax (collected via PAYE - Pay As You Earn) and Companies Income Tax (CIT).
   * **Indirect Taxes:** Taxes levied on goods and services, which the final consumer pays. Examples include Value Added Tax (VAT), custom duties (import and export tariffs), and excise duties (taxes on locally manufactured goods).

2. **Non-Tax Revenues:**
   * **Royalties and Rents:** Income from multinational companies extracting natural resources. In Nigeria, oil and gas royalties are a major source of revenue.
   * **Licenses and Permits:** Fees paid for legal permissions, such as driving licenses, business registration fees, and broadcasting licenses.
   * **Fines and Penalties:** Money collected from citizens or businesses that break the law (e.g., traffic fines).
   * **Earnings and Sales:** Income from government-owned enterprises, sales of government property, and fees for government services (like court fees or passport fees).
   * **Grants and Loans:** Financial assistance from international bodies (like the World Bank) or foreign governments, as well as domestic borrowing (issuing treasury bills or government bonds).

> **Common mistake:** Do not confuse Direct and Indirect taxes. If a person pays it directly based on their salary, it is Direct. If it is hidden in the price of a bag of rice or a bottle of water, it is Indirect.

## Capital and recurrent expenditure

**Content c: Capital and recurrent expenditure.**

Government spending (expenditure) is carefully classified into two categories to ensure that day-to-day survival does not eat up the funds meant for long-term development.

### Differences between capital and recurrent expenditure

**Objective iii: differentiate between capital and recurrent expenditure.**

| Feature | Recurrent Expenditure | Capital Expenditure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Expenses incurred on the day-to-day running of the government. | Expenses incurred on acquiring, constructing, or upgrading long-term physical assets. |
| **Lifespan** | Consumed within one financial year. | Benefits the country for many years. |
| **Examples** | Payment of civil servants' salaries, pensions, office stationery, electricity bills, and fueling of government vehicles (overheads). | Construction of roads, building new schools and hospitals, purchasing heavy machinery, and rural electrification projects. |
| **Impact on Assets** | Does not create any lasting asset. | Creates or significantly improves a permanent public asset. |

> **Common mistake:** Paying a contractor to *fix a broken window* in a hospital is recurrent expenditure (maintenance). Paying a contractor to *build a brand-new wing* of the hospital is capital expenditure.

## The Consolidated Revenue Fund (CRF)

**Content d: Consolidated Revenue Fund.**

The **Consolidated Revenue Fund (CRF)** is the main, overarching bank account of the government. 

According to Section 80 of the 1999 Constitution of Nigeria, all revenues or other moneys raised or received by the Federation (except those designated by law for a specific purpose, like the Education Tax Fund) must be paid into the Consolidated Revenue Fund. 

Similarly, no money can be withdrawn from the CRF unless the expenditure is specifically authorized by an Appropriation Act (the Budget) passed by the National Assembly, or directly authorized by the Constitution (such as the salaries of the Auditor-General and judges, which are a "direct charge" on the CRF).

## Statement of assets and liabilities

**Content e: Statement of assets and liabilities.**

In public sector accounting, the **Statement of Assets and Liabilities** serves the same purpose as the Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet) in a private business. It shows the financial position of the government at the end of the financial year.

Key components:
* **Assets:** Include Cash balances, Bank balances, Investments, and **Advances**. In government accounting, an "Advance" is money given out (such as a salary advance to a civil servant or a mobilization fee to a contractor) that must be repaid or accounted for. Because it is money owed *to* the government, an Advance is an **asset** (like a debtor).
* **Liabilities:** Include public debt (domestic and foreign loans) and **Deposits**. A "Deposit" in government accounting is money received by the government on behalf of third parties (such as retention money withheld from contractors, or unpaid wages). Because the government owes this money back to others, a Deposit is a **liability** (like a creditor).

---

### Calculating the CRF and determining net assets

**Objective iv: calculate consolidated revenue fund and determine the values of assets and liabilities.**

In the traditional accounting system, the balance of the Consolidated Revenue Fund represents the government's net accumulated surplus. It must exactly equal the Net Assets (Total Assets minus Total Liabilities) in the Statement of Assets and Liabilities.

**Worked Example:**
The following figures relate to the accounts of a State Government for the year ended 31 December.

* Opening balance of CRF: ₦800,000
* Taxes and Customs Duties: ₦500,000
* Royalties and Rents: ₦200,000
* Fines and Fees: ₦50,000
* Salaries and Wages: ₦300,000
* Overhead Costs: ₦150,000
* Cash and Bank balances: ₦750,000
* Investments: ₦350,000
* Advances: ₦100,000
* Deposits: ₦100,000

**Step 1: Calculate Total Revenue**
Taxes (₦500,000) + Royalties (₦200,000) + Fines (₦50,000) = ₦750,000

**Step 2: Calculate Total Recurrent Expenditure**
Salaries (₦300,000) + Overheads (₦150,000) = ₦450,000

**Step 3: Calculate the Surplus for the year**
Total Revenue (₦750,000) - Total Expenditure (₦450,000) = ₦300,000

**Step 4: Determine the Closing Balance of the CRF**
Opening CRF Balance (₦800,000) + Surplus (₦300,000) = ₦1,100,000

We can present this as a Ledger Account:

**Consolidated Revenue Fund Account**

| Dr (Expenditure) | ₦ | Cr (Revenue) | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Salaries and Wages | 300,000 | Balance b/f | 800,000 |
| Overhead Costs | 150,000 | Taxes and Customs Duties | 500,000 |
| Balance c/d | 1,100,000 | Royalties and Rents | 200,000 |
| | | Fines and Fees | 50,000 |
| **Total** | **1,550,000** | **Total** | **1,550,000** |
| | | Balance b/d | 1,100,000 |

**Step 5: Prepare the Statement of Assets and Liabilities to verify**
* Total Assets = Cash (₦750,000) + Investments (₦350,000) + Advances (₦100,000) = ₦1,200,000
* Total Liabilities = Deposits (₦100,000)
* Net Assets = ₦1,200,000 - ₦100,000 = ₦1,100,000

**Statement of Assets and Liabilities as at 31 December**

| Assets | ₦ | Liabilities & Funds | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cash and Bank balances | 750,000 | Consolidated Revenue Fund | 1,100,000 |
| Investments | 350,000 | Deposits | 100,000 |
| Advances | 100,000 | | |
| **Total** | **1,200,000** | **Total** | **1,200,000** |

Both the closing CRF and the Net Assets result in exactly ₦1,100,000.

> **Common mistake:** Deducting Advances as a liability or treating Deposits as an asset. Remember: Advances are monies owed to the government (Assets); Deposits are third-party monies held by the government (Liabilities).

## Statutory officers in government accounting

**Content f: Responsibilities and powers of statutory officers.**

The financial administration of the government is handled by several key officials, each with distinct powers granted by the Constitution and Financial Regulations.

### Duties of key government officers

**Objective v: identify the duties of the Accountant General, Auditor General, Minister of Finance and Treasurer of Local Government.**

1. **The Accountant-General (of the Federation or State):**
   * Acts as the Chief Accounting Officer of the government.
   * Responsible for the collection and custody of government funds.
   * Prepares the final accounts of the government (including the Statement of Assets and Liabilities and the CRF).
   * Issues accounting guidelines and ensures the accounting system across Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs) is functioning properly.

2. **The Auditor-General (for the Federation or State):**
   * An independent constitutional officer who acts as the Chief Audit Officer.
   * Examines and audits the accounts prepared by the Accountant-General.
   * Reports directly to the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of the Legislature (National or State Assembly) on any waste, fraud, or irregularities.
   * **Crucial Rule:** The Auditor-General *never* prepares the accounts. They only check the accounts prepared by others to ensure independence.

3. **The Minister of Finance:**
   * The political head of the Ministry of Finance.
   * Formulates broad fiscal and economic policies for the country.
   * Prepares the annual budget to be presented to the Legislature.
   * Issues **Warrants** authorizing the release of funds from the Consolidated Revenue Fund.
   * Manages public debt and government borrowing.

4. **The Treasurer of Local Government:**
   * The Chief Financial Officer at the local government tier.
   * Responsible for receiving revenues and making approved payments on behalf of the local government.
   * Keeps safe custody of local government financial records and cash.
   * Advises the Local Government Chairman on financial matters.
   * Prepares the annual accounts of the local government.

## Instruments of financial regulation

**Content g: Instruments of financial regulation.**

To prevent corruption and ensure money is spent exactly as the law intended, the government uses strict control documents and procedures.

### Distinguishing elements of financial control

**Objective vi: distinguish between elements of control in government accounting procedures e.g. warrant, votes, budget and due process certificate.**

[IMAGE: A flowchart showing the government financial control process. Top box: "Minister of Finance prepares the Budget". Arrow pointing down to a document: "Legislature approves the Budget". Arrow pointing down to an open safe: "Minister of Finance issues a Warrant". Arrow pointing down to a desk with ledgers: "Accountant-General releases funds from the Consolidated Revenue Fund". Arrow pointing to a building: "Ministries and Agencies spend money according to their allocated Votes". Clean flat textbook illustration, white background, using blue and green pastel colors for the boxes.]

1. **Budget (Appropriation Act):**
   * The master financial plan of the government showing estimated revenues and proposed expenditures for the upcoming financial year.
   * It is an instrument of macroeconomic control. No money can be legally spent unless it is included in the approved budget.

2. **Votes:**
   * A "Vote" is the specific maximum amount of money allocated in the budget for a particular expenditure item (e.g., the Vote for the Ministry of Education's stationery). 
   * It acts as a strict spending ceiling. MDAs use a "Vote Book" to monitor their spending and ensure they do not exceed their limit.

3. **Warrant:**
   * A legal document issued and signed by the **Minister of Finance**, authorizing the Accountant-General to release funds from the Consolidated Revenue Fund. 
   * Even if a project is in the budget, the Accountant-General cannot release the money without a warrant.
   * *Types of warrants include:*
     * **Annual General Warrant:** Authorizes recurrent expenditure for the year.
     * **Provisional Warrant:** Issued at the start of the year (if the new budget is delayed) allowing the government to run for up to 6 months based on the previous year's budget figures.
     * **Virement Warrant:** Authorizes the transfer of excess funds from one vote (where there is a surplus) to another vote (where there is a shortage) within the same ministry.

4. **Due Process Certificate:**
   * A document issued by the Bureau of Public Procurement (BPP) in Nigeria.
   * It certifies that a government contract was awarded transparently, through competitive bidding, and represents "value for money."
   * Funds will not be released to a contractor unless a Due Process Certificate is attached to the payment request. It is a powerful tool to curb contract inflation and corruption.

## Current issues in public sector Accounting

**Content h: Current issues in public sector Accounting.**

In recent years, the Nigerian government has implemented modern IT systems and global accounting standards to block financial leakages, improve transparency, and modernize public finance. 

### Understanding current trends in government accounting

**Objective vii: understand current trends in public sector Accounting.**

[IMAGE: A diagram comparing the old banking system with the Treasury Single Account (TSA) system. Left side (Old System): Several government ministries (Education, Health, Works) each connected by arrows to different commercial banks, showing scattered funds. Right side (TSA System): The same ministries all pointing arrows to one large central vault labeled "Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) - Treasury Single Account", showing consolidated funds. Clean flat textbook style, white background, contrasting red for scattered banks and green for the unified central bank.]

1. **Treasury Single Account (TSA):**
   * **What it is:** A unified structure of government bank accounts that gives a consolidated view of government cash resources. 
   * **The Problem it solved:** Previously, thousands of MDAs kept their revenues in separate commercial bank accounts. Many of these funds lay idle while the Federal Government simultaneously borrowed money at high interest rates to fund its activities. 
   * **The Solution:** TSA requires all government revenues to be swept directly into a single account (or a set of linked accounts) maintained centrally at the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN). This maximizes the use of government cash and heavily reduces borrowing costs.

2. **International Public Sector Accounting Standards (IPSAS):**
   * **What it is:** A set of global accounting standards issued by the IPSAS Board for use by public sector entities worldwide.
   * **The Impact:** The primary goal of IPSAS is to transition governments from the traditional cash basis to the **accrual basis** of accounting. 
   * By adopting IPSAS, Nigerian government financial statements now show a more accurate, transparent, and internationally comparable view of all government assets and liabilities, not just cash movements.

3. **Integrated Payroll and Personnel Information System (IPPIS):**
   * **What it is:** A centralized, computerized payroll system used by the Federal Government of Nigeria to pay the salaries of civil servants directly into their personal bank accounts.
   * **The Problem it solved:** For decades, government payrolls were inflated by "ghost workers"—fictitious names inserted into payrolls by corrupt officials to steal salaries.
   * **The Solution:** IPPIS uses biometric data (fingerprints and Bank Verification Numbers - BVN) to ensure that only genuine, living employees are paid. It has successfully eliminated tens of thousands of ghost workers, saving the government billions of naira.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Accountant vs. Auditor:** Never confuse their roles. The **Accountant-General** prepares the accounts. The **Auditor-General** audits (checks) the accounts. The Auditor-General does not prepare accounts.
* **Who signs warrants?** Warrants are authorized by the **Minister of Finance**, not the Accountant-General. The Accountant-General acts *on* the warrant.
* **Assets vs. Liabilities:** In government accounts, an **Advance** is an Asset. A **Deposit** is a Liability. This is the exact opposite of how a private citizen views a bank deposit!
* **TSA purpose:** Remember that the TSA is maintained at the **Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)**, not commercial banks.
* **IPPIS keyword:** Whenever an exam question mentions stopping "ghost workers" or biometric payroll, the answer is IPPIS.
* **Accrual Basis in IPSAS:** The main shift promoted by IPSAS is moving from the cash basis to the accrual basis.

## Quick summary

1. Public sector accounting focuses on stewardship and welfare rather than profit-making.
2. The government raises revenue through taxes (direct and indirect) and non-tax sources (royalties, licenses, and fines).
3. Expenditure is divided into recurrent (day-to-day running costs like salaries) and capital (long-term infrastructure like roads).
4. The Consolidated Revenue Fund (CRF) is the main government account. Its closing balance equals the government's Net Assets.
5. The Statement of Assets and Liabilities lists government assets (Cash, Investments, Advances) and liabilities (Deposits, Public Debt).
6. Financial control is maintained using Budgets (the plan), Votes (the limit), Warrants (the release authority), and Due Process Certificates (value for money).
7. Current reforms include the TSA (consolidating funds at the CBN), IPSAS (shifting to accrual accounting), and IPPIS (using biometrics to eliminate ghost workers).

## Practice questions

**1. The primary motive of public sector accounting is to**
- A. maximize the wealth of the government's shareholders
- B. calculate the annual profit of government agencies
- C. ensure accountability, stewardship, and public welfare
- D. compete with private sector businesses

**Answer: C.** The primary goal of the public sector is service provision and accountability to citizens, not profit-making.

**2. Which of the following is classified as a non-tax revenue to the government?**
- A. Value Added Tax
- B. Royalties from oil companies
- C. Companies Income Tax
- D. Pay As You Earn (PAYE)

**Answer: B.** Royalties, fines, and licenses are non-tax revenues. Options A, C, and D are all forms of taxation.

**3. Which of the following is an example of capital expenditure for the Ministry of Health?**
- A. Payment of monthly salaries to nurses
- B. Purchase of a brand-new MRI machine for a teaching hospital
- C. Payment of electricity bills for the ministry headquarters
- D. Purchase of office stationery

**Answer: B.** Purchasing heavy machinery creates a long-term asset, which is a capital expenditure. A, C, and D are day-to-day recurrent expenditures.

**4. A state government recorded the following: Opening CRF balance ₦3,000,000; Total Revenue ₦4,500,000; Recurrent Expenditure ₦2,000,000. Calculate the closing balance of the Consolidated Revenue Fund.**
- A. ₦5,500,000
- B. ₦9,500,000
- C. ₦7,500,000
- D. ₦2,500,000

**Answer: A.** 
Step 1: Calculate the surplus: Total Revenue (₦4,500,000) - Recurrent Expenditure (₦2,000,000) = ₦2,500,000.
Step 2: Add surplus to opening balance: ₦3,000,000 + ₦2,500,000 = ₦5,500,000.
*Mistake in Option C:* Adding revenue to the opening balance without subtracting expenditure (3M + 4.5M = 7.5M).

**5. The statutory officer responsible for preparing the final accounts of the government is the**
- A. Auditor-General
- B. Minister of Finance
- C. Accountant-General
- D. Governor of the Central Bank

**Answer: C.** The Accountant-General is the Chief Accounting Officer who prepares the accounts.
*Mistake in Option A:* The Auditor-General audits the accounts but never prepares them.

**6. In government accounting, an "Advance" given to a civil servant is treated as a/an**
- A. Liability
- B. Asset
- C. Revenue
- D. Recurrent expenditure

**Answer: B.** An advance is money owed to the government that must be repaid, so it is treated as a debtor (Asset).

**7. The document issued by the Minister of Finance that gives legal authority for the release of funds from the Consolidated Revenue Fund is called a**
- A. Warrant
- B. Vote
- C. Due process certificate
- D. Budget

**Answer: A.** A warrant authorizes the Accountant-General to release the funds. 
*Mistake in Option B:* A vote is the approved amount allocated in the budget, not the document authorizing release.

**8. The policy introduced by the government to consolidate all its cash resources into a single bank account at the Central Bank of Nigeria is known as**
- A. IPPIS
- B. IPSAS
- C. BVN
- D. TSA

**Answer: D.** TSA stands for Treasury Single Account, which consolidates government funds to prevent idle cash in commercial banks.

**9. Which of the following systems was specifically designed to eliminate "ghost workers" from the Nigerian civil service?**
- A. IPSAS
- B. IPPIS
- C. TSA
- D. FAAC

**Answer: B.** IPPIS (Integrated Payroll and Personnel Information System) uses biometric data to ensure only genuine workers receive salaries.

**10. A major aim of the International Public Sector Accounting Standards (IPSAS) is to encourage governments to shift from**
- A. accrual basis to cash basis accounting
- B. public sector to private sector accounting
- C. cash basis to accrual basis accounting
- D. centralized to decentralized budgeting

**Answer: C.** IPSAS pushes for the adoption of the accrual basis of accounting to ensure a complete and transparent view of all government assets and liabilities.
*Mistake in Option A:* This has the direction backward. Governments are moving *away* from cash basis *to* accrual basis.
