# JAMB Accounting Notes: Bank Transactions and Reconciliation Statements

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A, Topic 5 of the JAMB Principles of Accounts syllabus: Bank Transactions and Reconciliation Statements. It covers the following content items:

* a. Instrument of bank transactions
* b. e-banking system
* c. Causes of discrepancies between cash book and bank statement
* d. Bank reconciliation statement
* e. Adjusted cash book

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. identify various instruments of bank transactions such as cheques, pay-in-slips, credit cards, debit cards, internet banking and their uses;
2. assess the impact of automated credit system, credit transfers, interbank transfers and direct debit on cash balances;
3. identify factors that cause discrepancies between cash book balance and bank statement; and
4. determine adjusted cash book balance.

## Instruments of bank transactions

Every time a business deposits money into or withdraws money from its bank account, it uses a banking instrument. These are the formal documents or tools that authorize the bank to move money. 

### Identifying various instruments and their uses

The syllabus requires you to know several traditional and modern instruments of bank transactions:

**1. Cheques**
A cheque is a written order made by an account holder instructing their bank to pay a specific sum of money to a named person or to the bearer. It is the most common instrument used by businesses for payments.
* **Drawer:** The person or business who writes and signs the cheque (the account holder).
* **Drawee:** The bank on which the cheque is drawn (e.g., Zenith Bank or GTBank) that holds the drawer's money.
* **Payee:** The person or business whose name is written on the cheque to receive the money.

**2. Pay-in-slips (Tellers)**
A pay-in-slip is a paper form provided by the bank used to deposit cash or cheques into a bank account. It contains details like the account name, account number, date, the amount of cash in various denominations, and cheque details. A stamped duplicate is given to the depositor as proof of payment (a source document for the cash book).

**3. Debit Cards and Credit Cards**
Banks issue plastic payment cards that allow customers to make purchases or withdraw cash from Automated Teller Machines (ATMs). 

| Feature | Debit Card | Credit Card |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source of funds** | Linked directly to the customer's own bank account. | Linked to a line of credit (a loan) approved by the bank. |
| **When money leaves** | Deducted instantly from your account balance. | You borrow the money to pay now, and repay the bank later. |
| **Interest** | No interest charged (you are spending your own money). | Interest is charged if the monthly balance is not paid in full. |
| **Nigerian examples** | Verve, Mastercard Debit, Visa Debit (standard ATM cards). | Visa Credit, Mastercard Credit (less common for average retail users). |

**4. Internet Banking**
Internet banking allows customers to perform financial transactions on a secure website operated by their bank. Its uses include checking balances, viewing bank statements, transferring funds, and paying bills without visiting a physical bank branch.

## The e-banking system

Electronic banking (e-banking) refers to banking services provided through electronic channels rather than paper-based instruments like cheques. E-banking systems in Nigeria process millions of transactions instantly through networks like the Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System (NIBSS). 

Common e-banking channels include:
* **Automated Teller Machines (ATMs):** Used for cash withdrawals, transfers, and bill payments using a debit card.
* **Point of Sale (POS) Terminals:** Used by merchants to receive card payments directly from customers.
* **Mobile Banking Apps:** Applications on smartphones that allow users to manage their accounts and transfer money on the go.
* **USSD Codes:** Unstructured Supplementary Service Data (e.g., *737#) allows banking on simple mobile phones without internet access.

### Impact of electronic and automated systems on cash balances

E-banking speeds up how fast money moves. Because the bank updates the account instantly, but the business accountant might not record the transaction in the cash book until later, these systems heavily impact the cash balance records.

**Automated credit systems and Credit transfers**
A credit transfer is a direct payment from one bank account to another. For example, a customer transfers ₦50,000 directly into the business's account via a mobile app, or a company pays staff salaries automatically using the government's IPPIS system.
* **Impact on balance:** These *increase* the bank statement balance immediately. However, the cash book balance will remain understated (too low) until the business sees the credit alert or statement and debits its cash book. 

**Interbank transfers**
This is the electronic movement of funds from a bank account in one bank to an account in a different bank. 
* **Impact on balance:** An inward interbank transfer increases the balance. An outward transfer (e.g., a supplier payment) decreases it immediately, often before a receipt is physically processed.

**Direct Debit**
A direct debit is an arrangement where a business gives permission to a third party (like an electricity company or internet provider) to pull money from the business's bank account automatically when a bill is due. The amount can vary each month.
* **Impact on balance:** This *decreases* the true bank balance automatically. Because the business might not know the exact day or amount the money was taken, the cash book balance will be overstated (too high) until the payment is recorded as a credit in the cash book.

**Standing Order**
Similar to a direct debit, but a standing order is initiated by the *account holder*, not the payee. The business instructs the bank to pay a *fixed amount* at regular intervals (e.g., ₦100,000 on the 25th of every month for shop rent). It also decreases the cash balance automatically.

## Discrepancies between the cash book and bank statement

When you maintain a cash book, the bank column represents your bank account. A debit in your cash book means you received money; a credit means you paid money out. 

At the end of the month, the bank sends a **Bank Statement**. The bank statement looks at the relationship from the *bank's perspective*. To the bank, when you deposit money, they owe you (you are their creditor), so they **credit** your account. When you withdraw money, they **debit** your account.
* **Favourable balance:** Debit in the Cash Book = Credit on the Bank Statement.
* **Overdraft (Unfavourable):** Credit in the Cash Book = Debit on the Bank Statement.

Ideally, the balance in the cash book should equal the balance on the bank statement. In practice, they rarely match. 

### Identifying the factors that cause discrepancies

The differences between the two balances are caused by three main categories: timing differences, items generated by the bank, and errors.

**1. Timing Differences**
These occur because there is a delay between when a transaction is recorded in the cash book and when it clears the banking system.
* **Unpresented cheques:** These are cheques drawn (issued) by the business to suppliers and credited in the cash book, but the suppliers have not yet presented them to the bank for payment. This makes the bank statement balance *higher* than the cash book balance.
* **Uncredited cheques (Unlodged cheques):** These are cheques received from customers and debited in the cash book, but the bank has not yet cleared the funds and credited them to the account. This makes the bank statement balance *lower* than the cash book balance.

**2. Items initiated by the bank (not yet in the cash book)**
The bank makes automatic additions and deductions. The business usually doesn't know about these until it receives the bank statement.
* **Bank charges:** Deductions by the bank for services, account maintenance fees, SMS alerts, or Commission on Turnover (COT).
* **Interest charged / Interest earned:** The bank charges interest if the business is overdrawn, or pays interest on positive balances.
* **Direct credits / Credit transfers:** Money paid directly into the account by third parties (e.g., dividends).
* **Standing orders and Direct debits:** Automatic regular payments set up by the business.
* **Dishonoured cheques:** A cheque previously deposited and recorded as a receipt in the cash book, which the bank later rejects (e.g., due to insufficient funds, mismatched signature, or "Refer to Drawer"). The bank reverses the credit, but the cash book still shows the money as received.

**3. Errors**
Mistakes can be made by the business's bookkeeper or by the bank.
* **Cash book errors:** Overcasting (over-adding) or undercasting a column, writing a wrong amount (e.g., entering ₦4,500 instead of ₦5,400), or forgetting to record a transaction entirely.
* **Bank errors:** The bank might accidentally debit your account for another customer's cheque, or credit your account with someone else's money. 

## The adjusted cash book

Before preparing a bank reconciliation statement, the business must update its cash book with the transactions it didn't know about and fix any internal errors. This process results in the **Adjusted Cash Book**.

### Determining the adjusted cash book balance

**Rule of thumb:** You only adjust the cash book for items the bank knows about but you didn't (like bank charges), or for errors you made yourself. **Never** put unpresented cheques, uncredited cheques, or bank errors in the adjusted cash book. 

**Steps to determine the adjusted cash book balance:**
1. **Step 1:** Open a T-account starting with the unadjusted balance from the cash book. If it is favourable, put it on the debit side. If it is an overdraft, put it on the credit side.
2. **Step 2:** Add to the **Debit side** (Receipts):
   * Direct credits and credit transfers.
   * Interest and dividends received.
   * Correction of errors that understated receipts or overstated payments.
3. **Step 3:** Add to the **Credit side** (Payments):
   * Bank charges and commissions.
   * Interest on overdrafts.
   * Standing orders and direct debits.
   * Dishonoured cheques.
   * Correction of errors that overstated receipts or understated payments.
4. **Step 4:** Balance the account to find the new, adjusted balance carried down (c/d).

**Worked Example:**
Miyagi Stores has an unadjusted cash book debit balance of ₦145,000 on 31st May. The bank statement shows a credit (favourable) balance of ₦140,400. The discrepancies are:
* Unpresented cheques: ₦22,000
* Uncredited cheques: ₦18,500
* Bank charges: ₦1,200
* Standing order for insurance: ₦15,000
* Dividend received directly into bank: ₦9,000
* Error: A payment of ₦5,400 to a supplier was recorded in the cash book as ₦4,500 (meaning payments were understated by ₦900).

*Action:* Ignore the unpresented and uncredited cheques for now. Put the dividend on the debit side. Put the bank charges, standing order, and the ₦900 error on the credit side.

**Adjusted Cash Book for Miyagi Stores**

| Dr (Receipts) | ₦ | Cr (Payments) | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Balance b/d (Unadjusted) | 145,000 | Bank charges | 1,200 |
| Dividend received | 9,000 | Standing order | 15,000 |
| | | Error (Understated payment) | 900 |
| | | **Balance c/d (Adjusted)** | **136,900** |
| **Total** | **154,000** | **Total** | **154,000** |
| Balance b/d | 136,900 | | |

The determined adjusted cash book balance is **₦136,900** (Debit).

## The bank reconciliation statement

The Bank Reconciliation Statement is not part of the double-entry ledger. It is a separate statement prepared to prove that the difference between the adjusted cash book balance and the bank statement balance is due entirely to timing differences (and bank errors, if any).

An arrow points to an "Adjusted Cash Book" with items added (Direct credits, errors) and deducted (Bank charges, standing orders). From the Adjusted Cash Book, another arrow points to a document labeled "Bank Reconciliation Statement" where "Unpresented cheques" are added and "Uncredited cheques" are deducted to finally match a document on the far right labeled "Bank Statement". Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

### Reconciliation rules

The format changes slightly depending on whether you start with the Cash Book or Bank Statement, and whether the balance is favourable or an overdraft. 

| Starting Point | Add | Deduct | Ending Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Balance as per Cash Book** | Unpresented cheques | Uncredited cheques | Balance as per Bank Statement |
| **Overdraft as per Cash Book** | Uncredited cheques | Unpresented cheques | Overdraft as per Bank Statement |
| **Balance as per Bank Statement** | Uncredited cheques | Unpresented cheques | Balance as per Cash Book |
| **Overdraft as per Bank Statement** | Unpresented cheques | Uncredited cheques | Overdraft as per Cash Book |

### Worked Example 1: Favourable Balance

Continuing with the Miyagi Stores example, we take our newly calculated Adjusted Cash Book balance of ₦136,900, the unpresented cheques of ₦22,000, and uncredited cheques of ₦18,500.

**Bank Reconciliation Statement as at 31st May**

| Details | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Balance as per Adjusted Cash Book** | **136,900** |
| Add: Unpresented cheques | 22,000 |
| | **158,900** |
| Less: Uncredited cheques | (18,500) |
| **Balance as per Bank Statement** | **140,400** |

Notice that the final figure exactly matches the bank statement balance of ₦140,400 given in the question!

### Worked Example 2: Overdraft

Overdrafts reverse the normal logic. An overdraft is a negative balance (money owed to the bank). 
Let us assume a business has an **Adjusted Cash Book Overdraft of ₦41,300**.
It has **Unpresented cheques of ₦14,000** and **Uncredited cheques of ₦9,000**.
What is the Bank Statement balance?

Look at the table of rules above. For an overdraft starting from the cash book, we *add* uncredited cheques and *deduct* unpresented cheques.

**Bank Reconciliation Statement (Overdraft)**

| Details | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Overdraft as per Adjusted Cash Book** | **41,300** |
| Add: Uncredited cheques | 9,000 |
| | **50,300** |
| Less: Unpresented cheques | (14,000) |
| **Overdraft as per Bank Statement** | **36,300** |

If you were asked to start with the Bank Statement Overdraft of ₦36,300 and work backward to the Cash Book, you would add unpresented cheques (₦14,000) and deduct uncredited cheques (₦9,000) to arrive back at ₦41,300.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Cheque** | A written order to a bank to pay a specified sum of money to a named payee. |
| **Pay-in-slip** | A document used to deposit cash or cheques into a bank account. |
| **E-banking** | Carrying out banking transactions electronically using ATM, POS, internet, or mobile apps. |
| **Direct debit** | An automatic payment initiated by the payee, taking a variable amount from the payer's account. |
| **Standing order** | An instruction by an account holder to their bank to pay a fixed amount regularly. |
| **Unpresented cheques** | Cheques issued by the business but not yet presented to the bank for payment by the payee. |
| **Uncredited cheques** | Cheques received and lodged into the bank but not yet cleared and credited by the bank. |
| **Dishonoured cheque** | A cheque that the bank refuses to pay, often returned marked "Refer to Drawer" due to a lack of funds. |
| **Overdraft** | A negative bank balance, meaning the account holder has withdrawn more money than they have. |
| **Bank charges** | Fees deducted by the bank for rendering services to the account holder. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Favourable vs Unfavourable:** A favourable balance is a **Debit** in the Cash Book but a **Credit** on the Bank Statement. Do not mix them up!
* **Dishonoured cheques:** When a cheque is dishonoured, it goes on the **Credit side** of the Adjusted Cash Book because you are reversing the money you previously thought you had received.
* **Bank errors:** Never correct a bank error in the Adjusted Cash Book. Bank errors belong only in the Bank Reconciliation Statement.
* **Overdraft rules:** Many students fail overdraft questions because they apply favourable balance rules. Memorise the rule: to reconcile an Overdraft from Cash Book to Bank Statement, *Add uncredited, Less unpresented*.
* **Impact of e-banking:** JAMB often asks theory questions on how direct debits or automated credits affect balances. Remember that until the cash book is updated, direct debits make the cash book balance overstated (higher than reality), and credit transfers make the cash book balance understated (lower than reality).

## Quick summary

1. A cheque, pay-in-slip, debit card, and POS terminal are common instruments of bank transactions. 
2. E-banking systems make transactions instantaneous. Automated credit transfers increase the bank balance immediately, while direct debits and standing orders reduce it.
3. Discrepancies between the cash book and bank statement are caused by timing differences (unpresented/uncredited cheques), bank-initiated items (charges, interest), and accounting errors.
4. The Adjusted Cash Book is prepared to correct internal business errors and to record bank-initiated transactions like standing orders and bank charges.
5. Unpresented cheques, uncredited cheques, and bank errors are never included in the Adjusted Cash Book.
6. The Bank Reconciliation Statement uses the adjusted cash book balance and adjusts for unpresented and uncredited cheques to prove it matches the bank statement.
7. For a favourable balance, reconcile from Cash Book to Bank Statement by adding unpresented cheques and deducting uncredited cheques. For overdrafts, the rule is reversed.

## Practice questions

**1. A document used by a depositor to lodge cash or cheques into a bank account is called a**
- A. Cheque stub
- B. Bank statement
- C. Pay-in-slip
- D. Standing order

**Answer: C.** A pay-in-slip (or teller) is the official bank form used to deposit money. A stamped copy acts as a source document for receipts.

**2. What is the impact of an automatic direct debit on a business's cash balance if it has not yet been recorded in the cash book?**
- A. It makes the cash book balance lower than the bank statement balance
- B. It makes the cash book balance higher than the bank statement balance
- C. It increases the true cash balance
- D. It has no effect until it is recorded

**Answer: B.** The bank has already deducted the money, reducing the bank statement balance. Since the cash book has not yet been updated with this payment, the cash book balance remains higher than the bank statement (it is overstated).

**3. Which of the following is considered a timing difference in bank reconciliation?**
- A. Unpresented cheques
- B. Bank charges
- C. Dishonoured cheques
- D. An error in the cash book

**Answer: A.** Unpresented (and uncredited) cheques are timing differences because they have been recorded by the business but simply haven't had the time to clear through the banking system yet.

**4. A business has an unadjusted cash book debit balance of ₦50,000. It discovers that bank charges are ₦2,000 and a customer made a direct credit transfer of ₦8,000. What is the adjusted cash book balance?**
- A. ₦44,000
- B. ₦48,000
- C. ₦56,000
- D. ₦58,000

**Answer: C.** Step 1: Start with ₦50,000. Step 2: Add the direct credit (₦8,000). Step 3: Deduct the bank charges (₦2,000). Calculation: 50,000 + 8,000 - 2,000 = ₦56,000. Option A reversed the signs. Option D ignored the bank charges.

**5. The adjusted cash book balance of a firm is ₦60,000 (debit). Unpresented cheques amount to ₦12,000 and uncredited cheques are ₦15,000. Calculate the bank statement balance.**
- A. ₦57,000
- B. ₦63,000
- C. ₦87,000
- D. ₦33,000

**Answer: A.** Starting from a favourable cash book balance, the rule is: Add unpresented cheques, Less uncredited cheques. Calculation: 60,000 + 12,000 - 15,000 = ₦57,000. Option B incorrectly reversed the rule.

**6. The adjusted cash book balance is ₦25,000 (favourable) and the bank statement balance is ₦29,000 (favourable). If uncredited cheques amount to ₦6,000, what is the value of unpresented cheques?**
- A. ₦2,000
- B. ₦8,000
- C. ₦10,000
- D. ₦16,000

**Answer: C.** Using the formula: Adjusted CB + Unpresented - Uncredited = Bank Statement. This gives: 25,000 + Unpresented - 6,000 = 29,000. Therefore, Unpresented - 6,000 = 4,000. Unpresented cheques = ₦10,000.

**7. A business discovers that the bank accidentally credited its account with ₦20,000 belonging to another customer. How should this be treated?**
- A. Debit the adjusted cash book with ₦20,000
- B. Credit the adjusted cash book with ₦20,000
- C. Include it in the bank reconciliation statement
- D. Treat it as a direct credit in the profit or loss account

**Answer: C.** Bank errors are never corrected in the business's cash book because the business did not make the mistake. They are simply shown as an adjustment item in the bank reconciliation statement until the bank fixes the error.

**8. A firm’s unadjusted cash book shows a debit balance of ₦50,000. A cheque of ₦6,800 received from a debtor was incorrectly entered in the cash book as ₦8,600. The bank charged ₦1,500 for account maintenance. What is the adjusted cash book balance?**
- A. ₦46,700
- B. ₦49,700
- C. ₦50,300
- D. ₦41,700

**Answer: A.** The receipt was overstated by ₦1,800 (8,600 - 6,800). To correct this, you must deduct ₦1,800 from the cash book. You also deduct the ₦1,500 bank charges. Calculation: 50,000 - 1,800 - 1,500 = ₦46,700. Option D is a common mistake where a student deducts the entire ₦6,800 instead of just the difference.

**9. A cash book shows an overdraft of ₦24,000. Unpresented cheques amount to ₦8,000 and uncredited cheques amount to ₦5,000. What is the balance as per the bank statement?**
- A. ₦21,000 Favourable
- B. ₦27,000 Overdraft
- C. ₦21,000 Overdraft
- D. ₦37,000 Overdraft

**Answer: C.** To reconcile an overdraft starting from the cash book, the normal rules are reversed: Add uncredited cheques, Less unpresented cheques. Calculation: 24,000 + 5,000 (uncredited) = 29,000. Then 29,000 - 8,000 (unpresented) = 21,000. The result is an overdraft of ₦21,000.

**10. When a customer's cheque previously deposited into the bank is returned unpaid (dishonoured), the accounting entry in the adjusted cash book is to**
- A. Debit the bank column
- B. Credit the bank column
- C. Debit the cash column
- D. Ignore it because it is an error

**Answer: B.** When the cheque was initially received, it was debited. Since it has been dishonoured (rejected) by the bank, the business must reverse the entry by crediting the bank column in the adjusted cash book to reduce the balance.
