# JAMB Accounting Notes: Incomplete Records and Single Entry

## Meaning of incomplete records and single entry

**Content a: Meaning of incomplete records and single entry**

Many small businesses in Nigeria—such as a market woman selling provisions in Mushin, a local barber, or a tailor in Aba—do not employ professional accountants or keep complete double-entry records for every transaction. When an accountant is asked to prepare their Trading, Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet at the end of the year, they are faced with records that do not balance. 

**Single Entry**
Single entry is a bookkeeping system where only one aspect of a transaction is recorded, instead of the two aspects required by the double-entry principle. Usually, the business owner only keeps a **Cash Book** (to track money coming in and going out) and **Personal Accounts** (to know how much debtors owe them and how much they owe creditors). They do not keep nominal accounts for expenses or real accounts for fixed assets. 

**Incomplete Records**
Incomplete records is a broader term. It refers to any accounting system that falls short of strict double-entry rules. A business might try to keep double-entry books, but some records are lost, destroyed by fire, stolen, or simply forgotten due to poor bookkeeping. The accountant must use available documents (invoices, receipts, bank statements) to fill in the missing gaps before preparing the final accounts.

## Differentiate between incomplete records and single entry

**Content b and Objective i: understand and differentiate between incomplete records and single entry**

While textbooks and accountants often use the terms "single entry" and "incomplete records" interchangeably to mean "records that are not complete double entry," there is a technical difference you need to know for your exam.

| Feature | Single Entry | Incomplete Records |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | A deliberate, simplified system of bookkeeping where only one aspect (usually cash or personal accounts) is recorded. | A state of affairs where books were meant to be complete but are partially missing or poorly kept. |
| **Cause** | Intentional. The owner chooses this method because it is simple, cheap, and requires no accounting knowledge. | Accidental or due to negligence. Caused by fire, theft, loss of documents, or an inexperienced bookkeeper. |
| **What is kept** | Usually restricted to a Cash Book and personal ledger accounts for debtors and creditors. | May contain a mix of cash books, personal accounts, and some nominal or real accounts, but with gaps. |
| **Consistency** | Consistently ignores the two-fold effect of double entry. | Inconsistent. Some transactions might have double entry, while others do not. |

## Determination of proprietor's capital using statement of affairs

**Objective ii: determine proprietor’s capital using statement of affairs**

To prepare final accounts, you must know the **Opening Capital** (the capital at the start of the year). Because a single entry system does not maintain a Capital Account, the accountant must calculate it using the **Accounting Equation**:

$$ \text{Assets} = \text{Capital} + \text{Liabilities} $$

By rearranging this equation, we get:

$$ \text{Capital} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} $$

To find the capital at any given date, we prepare a **Statement of Affairs**. 

A Statement of Affairs looks exactly like a Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position). It lists all the assets a business owns on a specific date and subtracts all the liabilities it owes on that same date. The balancing figure is the Capital.
- To find **Opening Capital**, prepare a Statement of Affairs at the *beginning* of the year.
- To find **Closing Capital**, prepare a Statement of Affairs at the *end* of the year.

### Step-by-step example: Preparing a Statement of Affairs

Alhaji Musa, a tailor in Aba, does not keep proper books. On 1st January, his business had the following assets and liabilities:
- Cash in hand: ₦20,000
- Cash in bank: ₦80,000
- Stock of materials: ₦150,000
- Debtors (customers owing him): ₦60,000
- Creditors (suppliers he owes): ₦40,000
- Sewing Equipment: ₦300,000

**Step 1:** Identify the assets and liabilities.
- *Assets:* Equipment, Stock, Debtors, Bank, Cash.
- *Liabilities:* Creditors.

**Step 2:** Prepare the Statement of Affairs to find the opening capital.

**Statement of Affairs as at 1st January**

| Liabilities | ₦ | Assets | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Creditors | 40,000 | Sewing Equipment | 300,000 |
| **Capital (Balancing figure)** | **570,000** | Stock of materials | 150,000 |
| | | Debtors | 60,000 |
| | | Cash in bank | 80,000 |
| | | Cash in hand | 20,000 |
| **Total** | **610,000** | **Total** | **610,000** |

*Working:* Total Assets (₦610,000) - Total Liabilities (₦40,000) = Opening Capital (₦570,000).

### Finding Profit without Final Accounts (Net Worth Method)

Sometimes, JAMB will simply ask you to find the Net Profit for the year without preparing full Trading and Profit & Loss accounts. You can do this by comparing the Opening Capital and the Closing Capital.

If Closing Capital is greater than Opening Capital, the business made a profit (assuming no other adjustments). However, we must adjust for **Drawings** (cash or goods taken by the owner for personal use) and **Additional Capital** (new money brought into the business during the year).

The formula is:
$$ \text{Net Profit} = \text{Closing Capital} - \text{Opening Capital} + \text{Drawings} - \text{Additional Capital} $$

**Example:**
Using Alhaji Musa's business, suppose his Closing Capital at 31st December is ₦690,000. During the year, he took ₦40,000 cash for his daughter's school fees (Drawings) and did not bring in any new capital.
- Closing Capital = ₦690,000
- Opening Capital = ₦570,000
- Drawings = ₦40,000
- Additional Capital = ₦0

$$ \text{Net Profit} = 690,000 - 570,000 + 40,000 - 0 = \text{₦160,000} $$

## Conversion of single entry to double entry

**Content e and Objective iii: determine the amount of sales, purchases, cash balances, debtors, creditors and expenses by converting single entry to double entry**

To prepare full final accounts, we must convert the single entry records into strict double entry. This is done by opening Total Accounts (or Control Accounts) to find missing figures. 

The general rule for finding missing figures using T-accounts is:
1. Enter the opening balance (brought down).
2. Enter the closing balance (carried down).
3. Enter the cash movements (receipts or payments) during the year.
4. The remaining difference (the balancing figure) is the missing item (Sales, Purchases, Cash stolen, etc.).

### Determining Cash and Bank Balances (and missing cash)

A business might lose cash due to theft, or the owner might take cash without recording it (drawings), or there might be cash sales not recorded. To find missing cash figures, we construct a **Summary Cash Book**.

- **Debit side (Receipts):** Opening balance + Cash from Debtors + Cash Sales.
- **Credit side (Payments):** Payments to Creditors + Cash Purchases + Expenses Paid + Drawings + Cash Banked + Closing balance.

If both sides do not agree after entering all known items, the difference could represent unrecorded cash sales (if the debit side is short) or unrecorded drawings/stolen cash (if the credit side is short).

### Determining Sales using Debtors Account

Sales are made up of two parts: **Cash Sales** and **Credit Sales**. 
- Cash sales are found from the Cash Book.
- Credit sales must be derived by creating a **Total Debtors Account** (Sales Ledger Control Account).

**Step-by-step to find Credit Sales:**
1. Debit: Opening Debtors balance (balance b/f).
2. Credit: Cash/Cheques received from debtors, Discount Allowed, Bad Debts, Returns Inwards.
3. Credit: Closing Debtors balance (balance c/f).
4. **Balancing figure on the Debit side = Credit Sales.**

**Example:**
A trader provides the following: Opening debtors ₦40,000; Cash received from debtors ₦150,000; Discount allowed ₦5,000; Closing debtors ₦60,000.

**Total Debtors Account**

| Dr | ₦ | Cr | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Balance b/f | 40,000 | Cash/Bank | 150,000 |
| **Credit Sales (Missing figure)** | **175,000** | Discount allowed | 5,000 |
| | | Balance c/f | 60,000 |
| **Total** | **215,000** | **Total** | **215,000** |

*Working:* Total Credits (150,000 + 5,000 + 60,000) = 215,000. Total Debits known = 40,000. Missing Credit Sales = 215,000 - 40,000 = ₦175,000.

### Determining Purchases using Creditors Account

Purchases also consist of **Cash Purchases** and **Credit Purchases**.
- Cash purchases are taken from the Cash Book.
- Credit purchases are found by creating a **Total Creditors Account** (Purchases Ledger Control Account).

**Step-by-step to find Credit Purchases:**
1. Credit: Opening Creditors balance (balance b/f).
2. Debit: Cash/Cheques paid to creditors, Discount Received, Returns Outwards.
3. Debit: Closing Creditors balance (balance c/f).
4. **Balancing figure on the Credit side = Credit Purchases.**

### Determining Expenses for the Year

The amount paid for an expense in the Cash Book is not always the correct amount for the Profit and Loss Account. We must adjust for Accruals (amounts owing) and Prepayments (amounts paid in advance).

- **Expense for P&L** = Amount Paid + Closing Accrual - Opening Accrual + Opening Prepayment - Closing Prepayment.

## Determination of missing figures using mark-up and margin

**Content c and Objective iv: use accounting equations, gross and net profit percentages to determine cost of sales, gross and net profits**

Often, a business's records are so damaged (e.g. by fire) that you cannot find sales or purchases using control accounts. Instead, you must use the business's standard profit percentages to work backwards.

You must deeply understand the basic Trading Account equation:
$$ \text{Opening Stock} + \text{Purchases} - \text{Closing Stock} = \text{Cost of Sales} $$
$$ \text{Sales} - \text{Cost of Sales} = \text{Gross Profit} $$

JAMB frequently tests your ability to convert between **Mark-up** and **Margin**.

### 1. Mark-up
Mark-up is the Gross Profit expressed as a fraction or percentage of the **Cost of Sales** (Cost of goods sold).
$$ \text{Mark-up} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Cost of Sales}} $$

*If Cost of Sales is ₦100,000 and Mark-up is 25% (or 1/4), Gross Profit is ₦25,000. Therefore, Sales will be ₦125,000.*

### 2. Margin
Margin is the Gross Profit expressed as a fraction or percentage of the **Sales** (Selling price).
$$ \text{Margin} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Sales}} $$

*If Sales is ₦100,000 and Margin is 20% (or 1/5), Gross Profit is ₦20,000. Therefore, Cost of Sales will be ₦80,000.*

### Converting between Mark-up and Margin

This is a favourite JAMB trap. You might be given the *Sales* figure, but given the *Mark-up* on cost. You cannot apply a mark-up percentage directly to sales. You must convert it first!

**The Golden Rule for Conversion:**
- To change from **Mark-up to Margin**: Add the numerator to the denominator. (e.g., Mark-up $\frac{1}{4} \rightarrow$ Margin $\frac{1}{4+1} = \frac{1}{5}$)
- To change from **Margin to Mark-up**: Subtract the numerator from the denominator. (e.g., Margin $\frac{1}{5} \rightarrow$ Mark-up $\frac{1}{5-1} = \frac{1}{4}$)

**Common Conversions Table:**

| Mark-up (on Cost) | Margin (on Sales) |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{1}{2}$ (50%) | $\frac{1}{3}$ (33.3%) |
| $\frac{1}{3}$ (33.3%) | $\frac{1}{4}$ (25%) |
| $\frac{1}{4}$ (25%) | $\frac{1}{5}$ (20%) |
| $\frac{1}{5}$ (20%) | $\frac{1}{6}$ (16.6%) |

### Worked Example: Using Margin to find missing Purchases

A fire destroyed the stock of a trader. You are given:
- Sales for the year: ₦600,000
- Opening Stock: ₦50,000
- Closing Stock (salvaged from fire): ₦10,000
- Margin: 20% on sales.

What was the value of Purchases?

**Step 1: Find Gross Profit and Cost of Sales.**
- Since Margin is 20% (on sales), and we have the Sales figure, we can apply it directly.
- Gross Profit = 20% of ₦600,000 = ₦120,000.
- Cost of Sales = Sales - Gross Profit = 600,000 - 120,000 = ₦480,000.

**Step 2: Use the Cost of Sales equation to find Purchases.**
- $\text{Opening Stock} + \text{Purchases} - \text{Closing Stock} = \text{Cost of Sales}$
- $50,000 + \text{Purchases} - 10,000 = 480,000$
- $40,000 + \text{Purchases} = 480,000$
- $\text{Purchases} = 480,000 - 40,000 = \text{₦440,000}$

*Common Mistake Note:* If the question had said "Mark-up is 25%", you would first convert it to a Margin of 20% ($\frac{1}{5}$) before multiplying by Sales. Never multiply Mark-up directly by Sales!

## Preparation of final accounts from incomplete records

**Content d: Preparation of final accounts from incomplete records**

Once you have gathered all the missing figures (Opening Capital, Sales, Purchases, accurate Expenses), you can prepare the Trading Account, Profit & Loss Account, and Balance Sheet normally.

Let us return to our example of **Alhaji Musa, the tailor in Aba**. We already found his Opening Capital (₦570,000). 

Here is the summary of his Cash Book for the year ending 31st December:
- Cash Sales: ₦120,000
- Receipts from Debtors (banked): ₦450,000
- Cash banked from hand: ₦25,000
- Payments to Creditors by cheque: ₦280,000
- Rent paid by cheque: ₦30,000
- Wages paid in cash: ₦60,000
- Drawings in cash: ₦40,000

Balances as at 31st December:
- Stock of materials: ₦130,000
- Debtors: ₦85,000
- Creditors: ₦55,000
- Depreciation on Equipment: 10% on cost per annum.

**Step 1: Verify Cash and Bank Closing Balances**
We create a rough cash book to find the closing balances.
- *Cash Dr:* Opening Bal (20,000) + Cash Sales (120,000) = ₦140,000.
- *Cash Cr:* Wages (60,000) + Drawings (40,000) + Cash banked (25,000) = ₦125,000.
- *Closing Cash Balance* = 140,000 - 125,000 = **₦15,000**.
- *Bank Dr:* Opening Bal (80,000) + Debtors receipts (450,000) + Cash banked (25,000) = ₦555,000.
- *Bank Cr:* Creditors (280,000) + Rent (30,000) = ₦310,000.
- *Closing Bank Balance* = 555,000 - 310,000 = **₦245,000**.

**Step 2: Derive Total Sales and Total Purchases**
- *Credit Sales* = Cash from Debtors (450,000) + Closing Debtors (85,000) - Opening Debtors (60,000) = ₦475,000.
- *Total Sales* = Credit Sales (475,000) + Cash Sales (120,000) = **₦595,000**.
- *Credit Purchases* = Paid to Creditors (280,000) + Closing Creditors (55,000) - Opening Creditors (40,000) = **₦295,000**. (Total Purchases = 295,000 as there are no cash purchases).

**Step 3: Prepare the Trading and Profit & Loss Account**

**Alhaji Musa**
**Trading, Profit and Loss Account for the year ended 31st December**

| | ₦ | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sales** | | 595,000 |
| *Less Cost of Sales:* | | |
| Opening Stock | 150,000 | |
| Add Purchases | 295,000 | |
| Goods Available for Sale | 445,000 | |
| Less Closing Stock | (130,000) | |
| **Cost of Sales** | | (315,000) |
| **Gross Profit** | | **280,000** |
| *Less Expenses:* | | |
| Wages | 60,000 | |
| Rent | 30,000 | |
| Depreciation on Equipment (10% of 300,000) | 30,000 | |
| *Total Expenses* | | (120,000) |
| **Net Profit** | | **160,000** |

**Step 4: Prepare the Balance Sheet**

**Alhaji Musa**
**Balance Sheet as at 31st December**

| Assets | ₦ | Capital and Liabilities | ₦ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Non-Current Assets** | | **Capital Account** | |
| Equipment (Cost 300k - Depr 30k) | 270,000 | Opening Capital | 570,000 |
| | | Add Net Profit | 160,000 |
| **Current Assets** | | | 730,000 |
| Stock | 130,000 | Less Drawings | (40,000) |
| Debtors | 85,000 | **Closing Capital** | **690,000** |
| Bank | 245,000 | | |
| Cash | 15,000 | **Current Liabilities** | |
| *Total Current Assets* | *475,000* | Creditors | 55,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **745,000** | **Total Capital and Liabilities** | **745,000** |

*(Notice how perfectly both sides balance at ₦745,000. This confirms our derived figures were correct).*

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| Single Entry | A simplified bookkeeping system where only one aspect of a transaction is recorded, normally cash and personal accounts. |
| Incomplete Records | Accounting records that are partial or deficient because full double-entry procedures were not followed, or records were lost. |
| Statement of Affairs | A statement showing the assets and liabilities of a business at a specific date, prepared to determine the capital. |
| Net Worth Method | A method of calculating profit by comparing the opening and closing capital, adjusted for drawings and new capital. |
| Control Account | A total account (e.g., Total Debtors or Total Creditors) used to determine missing credit sales or credit purchases. |
| Mark-up | Gross Profit expressed as a fraction or percentage of Cost of Sales. |
| Margin | Gross Profit expressed as a fraction or percentage of Sales. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mistaking Mark-up for Margin:** This is the #1 mistake students make. Before calculating Gross Profit, check the basis. If given Mark-up (on cost) but the value given is Sales, you **must** convert to Margin first ($\frac{1}{4}$ Mark-up = $\frac{1}{5}$ Margin).
- **Forgetting to add Cash Sales/Purchases:** When a control account gives you a balancing figure, that is only the *Credit* Sales or Purchases. You must add any cash sales/purchases from the Cash Book to get the Total.
- **Statement of Affairs is NOT a Balance Sheet:** They look identical, but a Statement of Affairs is prepared specifically from incomplete records to find Capital, whereas a Balance Sheet is prepared from a balanced Trial Balance.
- **Drawings adjustment in Net Worth method:** Remember the formula: $\text{Closing Capital} - \text{Opening Capital} + \text{Drawings} - \text{Additional Capital}$. A common mistake is subtracting drawings instead of adding them back to find the true profit.

## Quick summary

1. Single entry is an intentional system tracking only cash and personal accounts; incomplete records are accidental gaps in double entry.
2. A Statement of Affairs is prepared to find the Opening Capital (Assets - Liabilities = Capital).
3. Profit can be calculated without full accounts using the Net Worth method: Closing Capital - Opening Capital + Drawings - Additional Capital.
4. Missing credit sales are found by opening a Total Debtors Account. Total Sales = Credit Sales + Cash Sales.
5. Missing credit purchases are found by opening a Total Creditors Account.
6. A Summary Cash Book helps determine missing cash figures, like unrecorded sales or drawings.
7. Mark-up is based on Cost of Sales. Margin is based on Sales. To convert Mark-up to Margin as a fraction, add the numerator to the denominator.
8. With all missing figures derived, final accounts (Trading, P&L, Balance Sheet) can be fully prepared.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following best describes the difference between single entry and incomplete records?**

- A. Single entry tracks only liabilities, while incomplete records track only assets.
- B. Single entry is an intentional simplified bookkeeping system, while incomplete records are accidental gaps in double entry.
- C. Single entry requires a trial balance, while incomplete records do not.
- D. Incomplete records only occur in large companies, while single entry is used by standard banks.

**Answer: B.** Single entry is deliberately chosen because it is simple, focusing on cash and personal accounts. Incomplete records happen due to poor recording, loss, or destruction of books.

**2. A trader has the following balances: Cash ₦20,000; Bank ₦50,000; Stock ₦80,000; Debtors ₦40,000; Creditors ₦30,000; Loan ₦10,000. What is the proprietor's capital?**

- A. ₦150,000
- B. ₦190,000
- C. ₦200,000
- D. ₦230,000

**Answer: A.** Capital = Total Assets - Total Liabilities. Assets = Cash (20k) + Bank (50k) + Stock (80k) + Debtors (40k) = ₦190,000. Liabilities = Creditors (30k) + Loan (10k) = ₦40,000. Capital = 190,000 - 40,000 = ₦150,000. Adding the loan to assets instead of liabilities leads to the incorrect option C.

**3. From the following information, determine the credit sales: Opening debtors ₦40,000; Cash received from debtors ₦150,000; Discount allowed ₦5,000; Closing debtors ₦60,000.**

- A. ₦150,000
- B. ₦170,000
- C. ₦175,000
- D. ₦255,000

**Answer: C.** Using a Debtors Control Account: Credit Sales = Cash received (150k) + Discount allowed (5k) + Closing debtors (60k) - Opening debtors (40k) = 215,000 - 40,000 = ₦175,000. Option B incorrectly ignores the discount allowed, and Option D incorrectly adds opening debtors instead of subtracting them.

**4. A business made sales of ₦600,000. If the margin is 20% on sales, what is the cost of sales?**

- A. ₦450,000
- B. ₦480,000
- C. ₦500,000
- D. ₦720,000

**Answer: B.** Margin is Gross Profit on Sales. Gross Profit = 20% of ₦600,000 = ₦120,000. Cost of Sales = Sales - Gross Profit = 600,000 - 120,000 = ₦480,000. Option A comes from wrongly treating the 20% margin as a 25% mark-up on sales instead of cost.

**5. A Statement of Affairs is prepared in single entry bookkeeping mainly to determine:**

- A. Net profit
- B. Cost of sales
- C. Total purchases
- D. Capital

**Answer: D.** A Statement of Affairs lists assets and liabilities on a given date to calculate the balancing figure, which is the Capital.

**6. If a trader's mark-up is 25% on cost, what is the equivalent margin on sales?**

- A. 20%
- B. 30%
- C. 33.3%
- D. 50%

**Answer: A.** Mark-up is $\frac{1}{4}$. To convert to margin, add the numerator to the denominator: $\frac{1}{4+1} = \frac{1}{5}$, which is 20%.

**7. To find the missing amount of credit purchases from incomplete records, which of the following accounts is prepared?**

- A. Trading account
- B. Cash book
- C. Total creditors account
- D. Total debtors account

**Answer: C.** The Total Creditors Account (Purchases Ledger Control) is prepared to determine the balancing figure for credit purchases.

**8. If a business pays ₦50,000 for rent during the year, but owes an additional ₦5,000 at the end of the year, what amount is posted to the Profit and Loss Account?**

- A. ₦45,000
- B. ₦50,000
- C. ₦55,000
- D. ₦60,000

**Answer: C.** In adjusting incomplete records for final accounts, accruals (amounts owing) must be added to the cash paid to reflect the true expense for the year. 50,000 + 5,000 = ₦55,000.

**9. When completing records, how are cash sales determined if they are not fully recorded?**

- A. By preparing a Statement of Affairs
- B. By preparing a Total Debtors account
- C. By finding the balancing figure on the debit side of the Cash Book summary
- D. By calculating the mark-up on purchases

**Answer: C.** Cash sales involve cash coming in, so they appear on the debit (receipts) side of the cash book. A summary cash book will reveal unrecorded cash sales as a missing debit figure.

**10. A trader's capital on 1st Jan was ₦150,000 and on 31st Dec was ₦210,000. During the year, he introduced additional capital of ₦10,000 and withdrew ₦20,000 for personal use. What is the net profit for the year?**

- A. ₦50,000
- B. ₦60,000
- C. ₦70,000
- D. ₦90,000

**Answer: C.** Using the Net Worth formula: Net Profit = Closing Capital - Opening Capital + Drawings - Additional Capital. Net Profit = 210,000 - 150,000 + 20,000 - 10,000 = ₦70,000. Option A uses incorrect signs (adding additional capital and subtracting drawings).
