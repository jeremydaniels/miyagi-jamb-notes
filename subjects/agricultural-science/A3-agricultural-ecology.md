# JAMB Agricultural Science Notes: Agricultural Ecology

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A, Topic 3 of the JAMB Agricultural Science syllabus: Agricultural Ecology. It covers the ecological zones of West Africa and the environmental factors that affect farming.

It covers the following content items:
- a. Ecological zones of West Africa
- b. Agricultural products of each ecological zone
- c. Environmental factors and their effects on crop and livestock production

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
1. differentiate between the features of the ecological zones in West Africa.
2. classify agricultural products according to each ecological zone.
3. differentiate abiotic from biotic factors affecting agricultural production.

**Note on the syllabus wording:** In the wider syllabus, you may see references to temperature, relative humidity, and light listed as examples of "climate change". Technically, these are **climate elements**. Climate change refers to long-term shifts in weather patterns. Both affect agriculture, but temperature, light, and humidity are the basic elements of the climate, and are treated as such in these notes.

## Ecological zones of West Africa

**Ecology** is the study of living organisms in relation to their environment. **Agricultural ecology** (or agroecology) is the study of how crop plants and farm animals interact with their environment, and how the environment influences their growth, distribution, and yield. 

In West Africa, the environment changes gradually as you move from the Atlantic coast in the south to the Sahara Desert in the north. This creates distinct bands called **ecological zones**.

An **ecological zone** is a large geographical area with a distinct climate (especially rainfall and temperature) and a characteristic type of natural vegetation. 

In West Africa, **rainfall** is the most important factor that determines these zones. The closer an area is to the Atlantic Ocean, the higher the rainfall and the taller the vegetation. As you move inland towards the north, rainfall decreases, the dry season becomes longer, and the vegetation changes from tall trees to grasses, and eventually to desert shrubs.

The major ecological zones in West Africa, from the south (coast) to the north, are:
1. Mangrove Swamp Forest
2. Tropical Rainforest
3. Derived Savanna
4. Guinea Savanna
5. Sudan Savanna
6. Sahel Savanna
7. Desert

*(Note: There is also the **Montane (Highland) zone**, found in high-altitude areas like the Jos Plateau and Obudu in Nigeria, which has a cool climate similar to temperate regions.)*

## Agricultural products of each ecological zone

Because crops and animals require different amounts of rainfall, sunlight, and temperature to survive and yield well, they are adapted to specific ecological zones. A crop planted in the wrong zone will either die or produce a very poor yield.

### Classifying agricultural products according to each ecological zone

We can classify the major agricultural products (crops and livestock) according to the ecological zone that best supports their production.

#### 1. Mangrove Swamp Forest
- **Crops:** Swamp rice (grown in the flooded areas), rubber, raffia palm, and coconut.
- **Livestock/Fishery:** Fishing is the most prominent agricultural activity here due to the abundance of rivers, creeks, and the ocean. Poultry and pig farming can be practiced on raised platforms or drier patches. Cattle rearing is virtually non-existent due to waterlogged soils and disease.

#### 2. Tropical Rainforest
- **Crops:** This zone is perfect for **tree crops** (due to deep, well-drained soils and high rainfall) and **root/tuber crops** (which need ample moisture). 
  - *Tree crops:* Cocoa, oil palm (*Elaeis guineensis*), rubber, kola nut, citrus, and timber trees (e.g., mahogany, iroko).
  - *Roots and tubers:* Cassava, yam, cocoyam, and sweet potato.
  - *Fruits and Vegetables:* Plantain, banana, pineapple, pepper, and tomato.
- **Livestock:** Livestock farming is severely limited by the **tse-tse fly**, which transmits trypanosomiasis (called nagana in cattle; sleeping sickness in humans) to cattle. Only **trypanotolerant** breeds (breeds naturally resistant to the disease) like the **Muturu** and **N'Dama** cattle, and West African Dwarf sheep and goats, thrive here. Poultry and piggery are heavily practiced because the animals are usually kept indoors.

#### 3. Derived Savanna
- **Crops:** Supports a mixture of rainforest and savanna crops. Yam, cassava, maize, cowpea, melon, citrus, and oil palm do well here.
- **Livestock:** Cattle rearing becomes easier here as tse-tse fly populations are lower than in the rainforest. Sheep, goats, and poultry are also common.

#### 4. Guinea Savanna
- **Crops:** Known for producing both **grains** and **roots**. 
  - *Cereals:* Maize, sorghum (guinea corn), and rice (in riverine or swampy areas like the Niger-Benue trough).
  - *Legumes:* Cowpea (beans), soybean, and groundnut.
  - *Roots and Tubers:* Yam (this zone is excellent for yam production) and cassava.
- **Livestock:** Very suitable for livestock production due to the abundance of tall grasses for pasture and a much lower incidence of tse-tse flies. Breeds include the White Fulani cattle, as well as sheep and goats.

#### 5. Sudan Savanna
- **Crops:** Focused heavily on **cereals** and **legumes** that can tolerate a shorter rainy season. 
  - *Cereals:* Sorghum and millet.
  - *Legumes:* Groundnut and cowpea.
  - *Others:* Cotton, onion, garlic, tomatoes, and carrots (often grown under irrigation).
- **Livestock:** This is a major hub for livestock production in West Africa. The vast grasslands, absence of tse-tse flies, and suitable climate support large herds of cattle (e.g., Sokoto Gudali, White Fulani), sheep (e.g., Balami, Uda), and goats (e.g., Red Sokoto).

#### 6. Sahel Savanna
- **Crops:** Only drought-resistant, fast-maturing crops can survive the short rainy season. Millet, acha (hungry rice), and some fast-maturing varieties of cowpea are grown. 
- **Livestock:** Dominated by **nomadic pastoralism** (moving with animals in search of pasture and water). Livestock includes cattle, sheep, goats, camels, and donkeys.

## Environmental factors and their effects on crop and livestock production

Agricultural production is completely dependent on the environment. Every stage of farming, from land preparation to harvesting and animal rearing, is influenced by surrounding factors.

### Differentiating abiotic from biotic factors

The environmental factors that affect farming are broadly divided into two main groups: **abiotic factors** (non-living) and **biotic factors** (living).

| Feature | Abiotic Factors | Biotic Factors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | The **non-living** chemical and physical parts of the environment. | The **living** organisms in the environment. |
| **Categories** | **Climatic** (weather/climate), **Edaphic** (soil-related), and **Topographic** (land shape) factors. | Pests, parasites, disease-causing pathogens, predators, weeds, and beneficial microbes. |
| **Examples** | Rainfall, temperature, sunlight, relative humidity, wind, soil pH, soil texture, slope of the land. | Insects, rodents, birds, ticks, tapeworms, bacteria, fungi, viruses, earthworms, bees. |
| **Mode of effect** | They determine which crops or animals can survive in an area and regulate their physiological processes (e.g., photosynthesis, transpiration). | They interact directly with the crop or animal (e.g., feeding on them, causing disease, competing for nutrients, or helping them). |

#### Effects of abiotic factors

**1. Climatic Factors**
Climatic factors are the elements of weather and climate. 
- **Rainfall (Water):**
  - *Effects on crops:* Water is essential for seed germination, dissolving soil nutrients for root absorption, and photosynthesis. Excess rainfall causes flooding, soil erosion, and leaching of nutrients. Lack of rainfall causes drought, wilting, and death of crops.
  - *Effects on livestock:* Provides drinking water. Determines the availability of pasture. High rainfall areas have high disease incidence (like foot rot in ruminants and trypanosomiasis).
- **Temperature:**
  - *Effects on crops:* Affects the rate of photosynthesis, respiration, and transpiration. Extreme heat causes rapid water loss (wilting) and abortion of flowers. Low temperatures can slow down growth. 
  - *Effects on livestock:* High temperatures cause heat stress, reducing feed intake and milk or egg production, while increasing water intake. Poultry birds may pant heavily to lose heat because they do not have sweat glands.
- **Light (Sunlight):**
  - *Effects on crops:* Sunlight is the source of energy for **photosynthesis**. It also affects **photoperiodism** (the response of plants to the length of daylight). Some plants only flower when days are short (short-day plants, e.g., cowpea and rice), while others need long days to flower (long-day plants, e.g., wheat).
  - *Effects on livestock:* Light stimulates the reproductive cycle in poultry. An increase in day length encourages hens to lay more eggs.
- **Wind:**
  - *Effects on crops:* Gentle wind helps in the pollination of flowers and dispersal of seeds. However, strong winds can cause **lodging** (uprooting or bending over) of tall crops like maize, rice, and sugarcane. Wind also causes wind erosion in the savanna areas and spreads diseases (e.g., fungal spores).
- **Relative Humidity:**
  - *Definition:* The amount of water vapour in the air.
  - *Effects:* High relative humidity reduces the rate of transpiration in plants and evaporative cooling (sweating or panting) in animals, leading to heat stress. It also creates a damp environment that strongly encourages the rapid spread of **fungal diseases** in crops (e.g., black pod disease of cocoa) and diseases in animals.

**2. Edaphic (Soil) Factors**
Edaphic factors relate to the physical and chemical properties of the soil.
- **Soil texture and structure:** Determines the water-holding capacity, aeration, and root penetration. Sandy soils dry out too quickly; clay soils hold water but can become waterlogged; loamy soil is the best for crops.
- **Soil pH:** The acidity or alkalinity of the soil affects nutrient availability. Most crops prefer slightly acidic to neutral soils (pH 6.0 to 7.0). Extreme acidity limits nutrients like phosphorus and causes toxic levels of aluminium.
- **Soil fertility:** The presence of essential nutrients (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium) directly determines crop yield and the quality of forage for animals.

**3. Topographic Factors**
Topography is the shape and physical features of the land surface.
- **Steep slopes** are prone to severe water erosion and are difficult to farm with machines (tractors).
- **Flat lands or gentle slopes** are easy to mechanize and retain soil fertility better.
- **Valleys** collect water, making them suitable for swamp rice but risky for crops that cannot tolerate waterlogging.

#### Effects of biotic factors

Biotic factors are the living organisms in the farming environment. Some are harmful, while others are beneficial.

**Harmful Biotic Factors**
1. **Pests:** Organisms (like insects, rodents, birds, and monkeys) that damage crops or livestock.
   - *Effects:* They reduce crop yield by eating leaves, stems, or roots; reduce the quality of farm produce; and increase the cost of production (buying pesticides). Some insects (like aphids and whiteflies) are **vectors** that transmit viral diseases to plants.
2. **Parasites:** Organisms (like ticks, lice, tse-tse flies, tapeworms, and roundworms) that live on or inside farm animals and feed on them.
   - *Effects:* They cause blood loss (anaemia), weight loss, irritation, and transmit diseases (e.g., tse-tse flies transmit trypanosomiasis; ticks transmit tick-borne fever).
3. **Diseases (Pathogens):** Microorganisms such as fungi, bacteria, and viruses.
   - *Effects:* They cause illnesses in crops and animals, leading to poor growth, low yields, high mortality (death) rates, and economic loss.
4. **Weeds:** Plants growing where they are not wanted.
   - *Effects:* They compete with crops for sunlight, space, water, and soil nutrients. They can also harbour pests and diseases, and reduce the overall yield of the crop.
5. **Predators:** Carnivorous animals (like hawks, snakes, foxes, and leopards) that kill and eat farm animals.
   - *Effects:* Cause direct loss of livestock, especially poultry and young ruminants.

**Beneficial Biotic Factors**
1. **Soil Organisms:** Earthworms, termites, and millipedes.
   - *Effects:* They burrow into the soil, improving aeration and water drainage. They also decompose organic matter to form humus, enriching soil fertility.
2. **Microorganisms:** Bacteria (e.g., *Rhizobium*) and fungi.
   - *Effects:* *Rhizobium* bacteria live in the root nodules of leguminous plants (like cowpeas and groundnuts) and fix nitrogen from the air into the soil. Other microbes decompose dead organic matter.
3. **Pollinators:** Bees, butterflies, and some birds.
   - *Effects:* They transfer pollen from one flower to another, ensuring fertilization and fruit/seed formation.
4. **Natural Enemies (Predators of Pests):** Ladybird beetles, praying mantises, and some wasps.
   - *Effects:* They feed on agricultural pests, providing **biological control**. For example, ladybirds eat destructive aphids.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Agricultural Ecology | The study of the interaction between agricultural crops/animals and their environment. |
| Ecological Zone | A broad geographical area with a distinct climate and vegetation. |
| Photoperiodism | The response of plants or animals to the length of daylight (e.g., flowering in plants or egg-laying in poultry). |
| Abiotic factors | The non-living components of an ecosystem (e.g., rainfall, temperature, soil). |
| Biotic factors | The living components of an ecosystem (e.g., pests, parasites, weeds, soil microbes). |
| Climatic factors | Environmental factors relating to weather and climate (e.g., wind, relative humidity, light). |
| Edaphic factors | Environmental factors relating to the soil (e.g., soil pH, texture, structure). |
| Trypanotolerant | The ability of certain livestock breeds (like N'Dama and Muturu cattle) to resist trypanosomiasis (nagana) caused by tse-tse flies. |
| Lodging | The bending over or uprooting of tall crops by strong winds. |
| Relative Humidity | The amount of water vapour present in the air; high humidity encourages fungal diseases. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Sequence of the Savanna:** Remember the order from South to North: **Derived → Guinea → Sudan → Sahel**. The rainfall decreases and the dry season lengthens as you move North.
- **Tse-tse flies and Cattle:** A very common JAMB question asks why cattle rearing is concentrated in the North (Savanna). The answer is that the Southern Rainforest is heavily infested with tse-tse flies, which cause trypanosomiasis (nagana in cattle).
- **Abiotic vs Biotic:** Do not confuse these. If it is alive (virus, weed, earthworm, insect), it is **biotic**. If it is not alive (sunlight, soil pH, rainfall, wind), it is **abiotic**.
- **Edaphic vs Climatic:** Know that "edaphic" strictly refers to soil factors (pH, texture, structure), while "climatic" refers to weather (rainfall, temperature, humidity).
- **Photoperiodism:** This is governed by **light** (specifically the duration of daylight), not temperature.
- **Rainforest crops:** Remember that tree crops (cocoa, rubber, oil palm) thrive in the rainforest because of high rainfall and deep soil. 
- **High Relative Humidity:** Know that it drastically increases the occurrence of **fungal diseases**.

## Quick summary

1. Agricultural ecology studies the relationship between crops, farm animals, and their environment.
2. The main ecological zones of West Africa from south to north are: Mangrove Swamp, Tropical Rainforest, Derived Savanna, Guinea Savanna, Sudan Savanna, and Sahel Savanna. 
3. Rainfall is the most important factor determining the ecological zones in West Africa; it is highest at the coast and lowest in the northern deserts.
4. Tree crops (cocoa, rubber) and root crops (yam, cassava) dominate the Rainforest, while grains (millet, sorghum), legumes (groundnut), and extensive livestock farming dominate the Savanna zones.
5. Trypanotolerant breeds like Muturu cattle are kept in the South, while breeds like White Fulani are raised in the North to avoid tse-tse flies.
6. Abiotic factors are the non-living climatic (rainfall, light, humidity) and edaphic (soil pH, texture) conditions affecting farming.
7. Biotic factors are the living organisms interacting with the farm, including harmful pests and diseases, and beneficial pollinators and soil microbes.

## Practice questions

**1. The most important factor that determines the distribution of ecological zones in West Africa is**

- A. soil type
- B. rainfall
- C. topography
- D. sunlight

**Answer: B.** Rainfall is the most critical factor; ecological zones in West Africa form horizontal bands determined largely by the decrease in annual rainfall from the coast towards the Sahara.

**2. Which of the following sequences correctly represents the ecological zones of Nigeria from the coast to the extreme north?**

- A. Mangrove swamp, Tropical rainforest, Derived savanna, Guinea savanna, Sudan savanna, Sahel savanna
- B. Tropical rainforest, Mangrove swamp, Guinea savanna, Derived savanna, Sahel savanna, Sudan savanna
- C. Mangrove swamp, Tropical rainforest, Guinea savanna, Sudan savanna, Derived savanna, Sahel savanna
- D. Tropical rainforest, Derived savanna, Guinea savanna, Sudan savanna, Sahel savanna, Mangrove swamp

**Answer: A.** The correct progression from the high-rainfall southern coast to the arid north is Mangrove, Tropical rainforest, Derived savanna, Guinea savanna, Sudan savanna, Sahel savanna.

**3. Large-scale cattle production in West Africa is predominantly found in the savanna zones mainly because of the**

- A. absence of tse-tse flies
- B. high rainfall for pasture growth
- C. presence of tall trees for shade
- D. absence of ticks and tapeworms

**Answer: A.** The rainforest zone is heavily infested with tse-tse flies, which transmit trypanosomiasis (nagana) to cattle. The savanna has far fewer tse-tse flies.

**4. Which of the following is an edaphic factor affecting agricultural production?**

- A. Relative humidity
- B. Soil pH
- C. Rainfall
- D. Weed competition

**Answer: B.** Edaphic factors are those related to the soil, such as soil pH, soil texture, and soil structure. Relative humidity and rainfall are climatic factors, while weed competition is a biotic factor.

**5. Which ecological zone in West Africa is most suitable for the cultivation of cocoa, rubber, and oil palm?**

- A. Sudan savanna
- B. Tropical rainforest
- C. Sahel savanna
- D. Mangrove swamp forest

**Answer: B.** The tropical rainforest provides the high rainfall and deep, well-drained soils required by these tree crops.

**6. The response of a crop to the length of daylight is termed**

- A. photosynthesis
- B. phototropism
- C. photoperiodism
- D. light intensity

**Answer: C.** Photoperiodism is the physiological reaction of organisms (like flowering in plants) to the length of day or night.

**7. High relative humidity in a crop farm is likely to encourage the rapid spread of**

- A. viral diseases
- B. fungal diseases
- C. bacterial diseases
- D. nutritional deficiencies

**Answer: B.** Fungi thrive and their spores germinate rapidly in very damp, highly humid environments.

**8. Which of the following is a beneficial biotic factor in agricultural production?**

- A. A locust swarm
- B. *Trypanosoma*
- C. Earthworms
- D. *Striga* plant

**Answer: C.** Earthworms are beneficial because their burrowing improves soil aeration, drainage, and their casts enrich the soil. Locusts are pests, *Trypanosoma* is a parasite, and *Striga* is a parasitic weed.

**9. Which of these livestock breeds is adapted to the tropical rainforest because of its trypanotolerance?**

- A. White Fulani cattle
- B. Sokoto Gudali cattle
- C. Muturu cattle
- D. Balami sheep

**Answer: C.** Muturu and N'Dama cattle are trypanotolerant, meaning they have a natural resistance to trypanosomiasis (nagana) caused by the tse-tse flies prevalent in the rainforest.

**10. Strong winds on a farm can result in all the following EXCEPT**

- A. wind erosion
- B. lodging of tall crops
- C. dispersal of seeds and spores
- D. increase in soil acidity

**Answer: D.** Wind does not affect soil acidity (soil pH). It does cause lodging (bending over) of crops, disperses seeds/spores, and causes wind erosion.
