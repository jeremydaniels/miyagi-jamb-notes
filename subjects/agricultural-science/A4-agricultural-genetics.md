## Terminologies e.g., locus, alleles, genotype, dominance

To understand genetics, you must first know the language. Genetics is the scientific study of heredity (how traits are passed from parents to offspring) and variation (differences among individuals of the same species). 

Here are the key genetic terms you must know for your JAMB exam:

- **Gene:** The basic unit of inheritance. It is a specific sequence of DNA that determines a particular trait (for example, plant height in maize or coat colour in cattle).
- **Chromosome:** Thread-like structures found in the nucleus of a cell, made of DNA and proteins. They carry the genes.
- **Locus (plural: loci):** The specific physical location or position of a gene on a chromosome.
- **Alleles:** Alternative forms of a gene occupying the same locus on homologous (matching) chromosomes. For example, the gene for plant height has an allele for "Tall" and an allele for "Short".
- **Genotype:** The genetic makeup or constitution of an organism. It represents the actual genes present and is written as letters, such as **TT**, **Tt**, or **tt**.
- **Phenotype:** The physical appearance or observable traits of an organism, which is determined by its genotype and the environment. For example, a maize plant's genotype might be **Tt**, but its phenotype is **Tall**.
- **Dominance (Dominant trait):** A condition where one allele completely masks the physical expression of another allele in a heterozygous pair. A dominant allele is always represented by a capital letter (e.g., **T** for Tall).
- **Recessive trait:** An allele whose trait is masked and only shows up if the individual has two copies of it. It is represented by a small letter (e.g., **t** for Short).
- **Homozygous:** When an individual has two identical alleles for a particular trait (e.g., **TT** for pure-breeding tall, or **tt** for pure-breeding short).
- **Heterozygous:** When an individual has two different alleles for a particular trait (e.g., **Tt**). The individual carries the hidden recessive gene but shows the dominant phenotype. This is often called a **hybrid**.
- **Gamete:** A mature sex cell (sperm in males, ovum/egg in females, or pollen in plants) which contains half the normal number of chromosomes (haploid).
- **Zygote:** The diploid cell formed when a male gamete fuses with a female gamete during fertilization.

## First and second laws of Mendel

Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk known as the "Father of Genetics", established the fundamental rules of inheritance in the 1860s through his experiments with the garden pea (*Pisum sativum*). His findings are summarized into two major laws.

### Mendel's First Law: Law of Segregation
Mendel's First Law states that an organism's characteristics are controlled by pairs of factors (now called alleles), and these **pairs separate (segregate) randomly into different gametes during cell division**.
Because of this segregation, every gamete carries only one allele for each trait. For instance, a plant with the heterozygous genotype **Tt** will produce some gametes carrying **T** and some carrying **t**.

### Mendel's Second Law: Law of Independent Assortment
Mendel's Second Law states that when two or more pairs of traits are considered, **each pair of alleles separates independently of the other pairs during gamete formation**. 
This means that the inheritance of one trait (like seed colour) does not affect the inheritance of another trait (like seed shape). 
*Note:* This law holds true as long as the genes are on different chromosomes and are not linked together.

## Apply the first and second laws of Mendel to genetics

In agriculture, we apply Mendel's laws to predict the physical traits (phenotypes) and genetic makeup (genotypes) of crops and livestock when they are bred. 

By knowing that traits segregate and assort independently, an animal breeder can predict the outcomes of mating two animals. For example, if a farmer crosses a fast-growing, disease-susceptible chicken with a slow-growing, disease-resistant chicken, they can use the principles of independent assortment to isolate and create a fast-growing, disease-resistant variety in the second generation. Breeders use a grid system called a **Punnett square** to apply Mendel's laws of probability and accurately map out these genetic crosses.

## Determine the outcome of genetic crossing involving homozygous and heterozygous traits

To determine the outcome of a genetic cross, we trace how alleles segregate (First Law) and re-combine during fertilization. We explain this step by step using a **monohybrid cross** (a cross involving only one trait, like plant height).

Let us cross a **homozygous dominant** tall maize plant (**TT**) with a **homozygous recessive** short maize plant (**tt**).

**Step 1: Identify the parents (P₁ generation)**
- Parent 1 phenotype: Tall 
- Parent 1 genotype: **TT**
- Parent 2 phenotype: Short
- Parent 2 genotype: **tt**

**Step 2: Segregate the gametes**
- Parent 1 (**TT**) produces only **T** gametes.
- Parent 2 (**tt**) produces only **t** gametes.

**Step 3: First Filial (F₁) Generation cross**
All gametes from Parent 1 (**T**) fuse with gametes from Parent 2 (**t**).
- Resulting F₁ genotype: **100% Tt** (heterozygous).
- Resulting F₁ phenotype: **100% Tall** (because T is dominant over t).

**Step 4: Cross two F₁ heterozygous individuals (Tt × Tt)**
If we cross two F₁ offspring to produce the Second Filial (F₂) generation:
- Both parents are **Tt**.
- Gametes produced by each parent: half are **T** and half are **t**.

**Step 5: Use a Punnett square to find the F₂ outcome**

| Gametes | **T** | **t** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **T** | TT | Tt |
| **t** | Tt | tt |

**Outcome of the F₂ cross (4 total outcomes):**
- 1 **TT** (Homozygous tall)
- 2 **Tt** (Heterozygous tall)
- 1 **tt** (Homozygous short)

## Compute simple probability ratios

Based on the Punnett square above, we must compute the exact probability ratios expected by JAMB. Every square in a 2×2 Punnett square represents a 1 in 4 chance, or 25%.

**Step-by-step computation of the Genotypic Ratio (F₂ generation):**
The formula for probability is:
$$\text{Probability} = \frac{\text{Number of specific genotypes}}{\text{Total number of outcomes}}$$

1. **Step 1:** Count the number of pure dominant (**TT**) boxes: 1 box out of 4 total. Probability = $\frac{1}{4} \times 100\% = 25\%$.
2. **Step 2:** Count the number of heterozygous (**Tt**) boxes: 2 boxes out of 4 total. Probability = $\frac{2}{4} \times 100\% = 50\%$.
3. **Step 3:** Count the number of pure recessive (**tt**) boxes: 1 box out of 4 total. Probability = $\frac{1}{4} \times 100\% = 25\%$.
4. **Step 4:** Write the Genotypic ratio by combining the counts: **1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt** (or simply **1:2:1**).

**Step-by-step computation of the Phenotypic Ratio (F₂ generation):**
The formula for probability is:
$$\text{Probability} = \frac{\text{Number of specific phenotypes}}{\text{Total number of outcomes}}$$

1. **Step 1:** Count the number of tall plants. Since T is dominant, both **TT** and **Tt** genotypes produce tall plants. 
2. **Step 2:** Total tall boxes = 1 (from TT) + 2 (from Tt) = 3 boxes. Probability = $\frac{3}{4} \times 100\% = 75\%$.
3. **Step 3:** Count the number of short plants. This only occurs when both alleles are recessive (**tt**).
4. **Step 4:** Total short boxes = 1 box. Probability = $\frac{1}{4} \times 100\% = 25\%$.
5. **Step 5:** Write the Phenotypic ratio by combining the counts: **3 Tall : 1 Short** (or simply **3:1**).

## Cell division

Cell division is the process by which a parent cell divides into two or more daughter cells. It is how organisms grow, heal damaged tissues, and reproduce. In agriculture, understanding cell division is critical because it forms the biological basis for plant propagation and animal breeding. There are two main types of cell division: **mitosis** and **meiosis**.

### Mitosis
Mitosis is a type of cell division that results in two identical daughter cells, each having the exact same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus (diploid, 2n). 
- **Where it occurs:** In **somatic** (body or vegetative) cells. In plants, it occurs rapidly at the growing tips (meristems) of roots and shoots.
- **Process summary:** The cell copies its chromosomes, the chromosomes line up in the middle of the cell, get pulled apart to opposite ends, and the cell splits into two identical halves.
- **Importance in agriculture:** It is responsible for physical growth, repair of damaged tissues, and asexual/vegetative propagation (like taking cassava stem cuttings or budding orange trees).

### Meiosis
Meiosis is a reduction division that results in four daughter cells, each having half the number of chromosomes of the parent cell (haploid, n).
- **Where it occurs:** In the reproductive organs (**gonads**) — the testes and ovaries in animals, and the anthers and ovaries in plants.
- **Process summary:** The cell copies its chromosomes, but then undergoes *two* consecutive divisions. During the first division, a process called **crossing over** occurs, where chromosomes swap bits of genetic material, causing genetic variation. The final result is four genetically distinct haploid cells.
- **Importance in agriculture:** It produces gametes (sperm, eggs, pollen) required for sexual reproduction. It is responsible for the genetic variation upon which selection and crop improvement rely.

## Differentiate between the types of cell division

To clearly distinguish between the two types of cell division, use the comparison table below:

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Site of occurrence** | Somatic (body) or vegetative cells | Reproductive cells (gonads: testes, ovaries, anthers) |
| **Number of divisions** | One nuclear division | Two successive nuclear divisions |
| **Number of daughter cells** | Two daughter cells produced | Four daughter cells produced |
| **Chromosome number** | Same as parent cell (Diploid, 2n) | Half of the parent cell (Haploid, n) |
| **Genetic composition** | Daughter cells are genetically identical to the parent | Daughter cells are genetically different due to crossing over |
| **Agricultural function** | Growth, tissue repair, and asexual (vegetative) propagation | Gamete formation and sexual reproduction (creates variation) |

## Methods of crop improvement e.g., introduction, selection, crossing, quarantine etc.

Crop improvement (also known as plant breeding) is the science of altering the genetic makeup of plants to increase their usefulness to humans. Agricultural scientists use four primary tools or methods to achieve this: introduction, selection, crossing (hybridisation), and quarantine. 

### 1. Introduction
This involves bringing a new crop variety or species from its native place (often a foreign country) into a new environment where it was not previously grown. Crops brought in from outside are called **exotic** varieties, while native crops are **indigenous**. For example, cocoa was introduced to West Africa from South America.

### 2. Selection
This is the process of closely observing plants in a population and deliberately choosing the ones with desirable characteristics to become the parents of the next generation. 
- **Mass selection:** Choosing a large number of plants based on physical traits and mixing their seeds for the next planting.
- **Pure-line selection:** Selecting a single superior plant that self-pollinates and breeding it until its traits are homozygous and completely uniform.

### 3. Crossing (Hybridisation)
This involves taking pollen from one plant (the male parent) and using it to fertilize the stigma of a different plant (the female parent). The goal is to combine the good traits of both parents into the offspring (the hybrid). For example, crossing a high-yielding but disease-susceptible plant with a low-yielding but disease-resistant plant. This often results in **heterosis** or hybrid vigour.

### 4. Quarantine
While quarantine is primarily a protective regulatory measure, JAMB lists it under crop improvement methods because it is the crucial step that safely facilitates plant introduction. Quarantine involves keeping newly introduced seeds or plant materials in strict isolation and observation for a period of time (often at the airport or border). This ensures they are free of foreign pests and diseases before they are released to local farmers. In Nigeria, this is managed by the **Nigeria Agricultural Quarantine Service (NAQS)**.

## Give reasons for crop improvement

Farmers and breeders invest time and money into improving crops to achieve the following specific objectives:
1. **Higher yields:** To produce more food or cash crop per hectare (e.g., breeding improved maize varieties that produce larger cobs).
2. **Early maturity:** To develop crops that complete their life cycle faster, allowing farmers to harvest before drought sets in or to plant multiple cycles a year.
3. **Disease and pest resistance:** To create plants that can withstand attacks from pests and pathogens naturally (e.g., cowpea resistant to root-knot nematodes).
4. **Better adaptability:** To breed crops that can survive harsh environmental conditions, like drought, high soil salinity, or poor soils.
5. **Improved quality:** To enhance the taste, colour, shelf-life, and nutritional value of produce (e.g., higher protein content in soybeans, or sweeter pineapples).
6. **Uniformity:** To ensure plants grow to the same height and mature at the exact same time, making mechanized harvesting much easier.

## Distinguish between various methods of crop improvement, and their respective advantages and disadvantages

The four main methods of crop improvement each operate differently. Here is how they are distinguished alongside their pros and cons:

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Introduction** | Bringing a new exotic crop variety to a local area. | 1. Fast and requires little scientific skill.<br>2. Can bring entirely new, highly productive crops to a country. | 1. May accidentally introduce new, devastating pests.<br>2. The exotic crop may fail to adapt to the local climate. |
| **Selection** | Choosing the best-performing plants to provide seed. | 1. Preserves locally adapted, hardy traits.<br>2. Relatively easy and cheap for local farmers. | 1. Very slow process; takes many years to fix a trait.<br>2. Cannot create entirely new traits. |
| **Crossing** | Mating two genetically different plants (hybridisation). | 1. Combines diverse, highly desirable traits.<br>2. Results in hybrid vigour (heterosis). | 1. Expensive, time-consuming, and requires expert skill.<br>2. Farmers must buy new hybrid seeds yearly. |
| **Quarantine** | Isolating imported crops to check for diseases. | 1. Prevents devastating foreign diseases from destroying local agriculture. | 1. Can cause long delays in getting improved varieties to farmers.<br>2. Requires expensive isolation facilities. |

## Methods of animal improvement e.g., introduction, breeding, quarantine and selection: Breeding systems – inbreeding, line-breeding, cross-breeding, artificial insemination

Just like crops, farm animals are genetically improved to maximize profit and farming efficiency. The methods used are introduction, selection, breeding, and quarantine, with a heavy focus on specialized breeding systems.

### 1. Introduction
Importing high-performing exotic animal breeds from other countries to a local farm. An example is importing Holstein Friesian cattle from Europe to Nigeria for milk production.

### 2. Selection
Choosing the best animals to be parents of the next generation and culling (removing) the poor performers. Selection often relies on **progeny testing**, which means evaluating a breeding animal based on the performance of its offspring.

### 3. Quarantine
Holding newly purchased or imported animals in an isolation unit for a specific period (usually 14 to 30 days) to monitor them for diseases. If they are sick, they are treated or culled; if healthy, they join the main herd.

### 4. Breeding (and Breeding Systems)
Breeding is the intentional mating of selected males and females. The specific genetic relationship between the animals being mated defines the **breeding system**. 

**A. Inbreeding:**
The mating of closely related animals, such as a brother and a sister, or a sire (father) to his daughter.

**B. Line-breeding:**
A milder form of inbreeding involving the mating of distant relatives (e.g., cousins, or a grandson to a grand-dam).

**C. Cross-breeding:**
The mating of animals from entirely different breeds (e.g., crossing a local White Fulani cow with an exotic Holstein Friesian bull).

**D. Artificial Insemination (AI):**
AI is not a mating system itself, but an advanced reproductive *technique* used to deposit male semen into the reproductive tract of a female without natural mating. 
**Step-by-step process of AI:**
1. **Step 1 (Collection):** Semen is collected from a superior male (e.g., a bull) using an artificial vagina.
2. **Step 2 (Examination & Dilution):** The semen is checked under a microscope to ensure sperm are alive and motile. It is diluted (extended) with protective liquids like egg yolk.
3. **Step 3 (Storage):** The extended semen is frozen in liquid nitrogen at exactly **-196 °C**. At this extreme temperature, it can stay alive for years.
4. **Step 4 (Insemination):** When a female is observed to be "on heat" (estrus), a trained technician thaws a semen straw and injects it safely into her cervix using a syringe.

## Give reasons for animal improvement

The primary objectives for improving livestock include:
1. **Higher yields:** To increase the output of meat, milk, and eggs (e.g., breeding poultry chickens that lay over 250 eggs per year).
2. **Fast growth rate:** To reach market weight faster, significantly reducing the cost of feeding and housing.
3. **High feed conversion efficiency:** To breed animals that effectively convert a small amount of feed into a large amount of body mass.
4. **Disease resistance:** To develop animals naturally immune or tolerant to common local diseases. For example, the indigenous N'Dama and Muturu cattle are tolerant to trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness transmitted by the tsetse fly).
5. **Adaptability:** To breed animals that can tolerate extreme heat, cold, or poor pasture conditions.
6. **Better product quality:** To produce meat with less fat (lean meat), or milk with a higher butterfat content for cheese-making.

## Differentiate between the various methods of animal improvement, and their respective advantages and disadvantages

To successfully differentiate the core methods and breeding systems used to improve farm animals, review this comparison table:

| Method / System | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Introduction** | Importing exotic breeds into a new local area. | Quick way to acquire superior, high-yielding traits. | Exotic animals may die from local diseases or heat stress. |
| **Selection** | Choosing the best-performing individuals as parents. | Maintains local hardiness while gradually improving yield. | Very slow process, especially for cattle (long gestation). |
| **Quarantine** | Isolating imported animals to observe for diseases. | Prevents foreign contagious diseases from infecting local herds. | Expensive and requires dedicated isolation facilities and feeding. |
| **Inbreeding** | Mating closely related animals (e.g., sire to daughter). | Concentrates good genes rapidly to create completely pure breeds. | Causes **inbreeding depression** (weakness, uncovers hidden lethal recessive traits). |
| **Cross-breeding** | Mating completely different breeds. | Produces **hybrid vigour (heterosis)** with highly superior traits. | Offspring are not purebred; farmers must maintain separate parent herds. |
| **Artificial Insemination (AI)** | Injecting frozen semen (-196 °C) into a female without mating. | Prevents venereal diseases; one superior bull can fertilize thousands of cows. | Requires highly trained staff; farmers must accurately detect when females are on heat. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Genotype vs Phenotype:** A common mistake is confusing the *genotypic* ratio with the *phenotypic* ratio. In a standard heterozygous cross (Tt × Tt), the genotypic ratio is **1:2:1**, but the phenotypic ratio is **3:1**. Read the question carefully to see which one JAMB is asking for.
- **Meiosis vs Mitosis:** Remember that **Meiosis** = **M**aking **E**ggs (and sperm). It happens in the gonads and halves the chromosome number. Mitosis happens in body cells for physical growth.
- **Mendel's Plant:** Mendel used the **garden pea** (*Pisum sativum*). Do not pick "sweet pea" if it is in the options.
- **Artificial Insemination temperature:** The standard temperature for storing semen in liquid nitrogen is **-196 °C**.
- **Inbreeding vs Cross-breeding:** Remember that inbreeding *fixes* pure traits but causes *depression* (weakness). Cross-breeding mixes traits and creates *hybrid vigour* (strength).
- **Quarantine:** It is mostly an indirect method of improvement that strictly stops the spread of diseases when bringing in exotic breeds.

## Quick summary

1. Mendel's First Law (Segregation) states that alleles separate into different gametes; the Second Law (Independent Assortment) states different traits sort independently.
2. The genotype is an organism's genetic makeup (like TT, Tt, tt), while the phenotype is its physical appearance (like tall or short).
3. A monohybrid cross between two heterozygous individuals (Tt × Tt) gives a genotypic ratio of 1:2:1 and a phenotypic ratio of 3 Tall to 1 Short.
4. Mitosis produces two identical diploid cells for growth and repair; meiosis produces four non-identical haploid gametes for sexual reproduction.
5. Crops and animals are genetically improved to increase yields, improve disease resistance, ensure early maturity, and achieve faster growth rates.
6. Crop improvement methods include introduction (bringing exotic crops), selection (choosing the best), crossing (mixing traits), and quarantine (disease control).
7. Animal breeding systems include inbreeding (mating close relatives), line-breeding, and cross-breeding (mating different breeds to get hybrid vigour/heterosis).
8. Artificial Insemination (AI) allows farmers to safely and cheaply use frozen semen (-196 °C) from highly superior males to impregnate many females, preventing venereal diseases.

## Practice questions

**1. The specific physical position occupied by a gene on a chromosome is called its**
- A. allele
- B. locus
- C. chromatid
- D. genotype

**Answer: B.** locus.
The locus (plural: loci) is the exact physical location of a gene on a chromosome. An allele is an alternative form of that gene.

**2. A pure-breeding tall maize plant (TT) is crossed with a pure-breeding short maize plant (tt). What is the probability of producing a short plant in the first filial (F₁) generation?**
- A. 100%
- B. 75%
- C. 25%
- D. 0%

**Answer: D.** 0%.
Formula: $\text{Probability} = \frac{\text{Number of desired outcomes}}{\text{Total number of outcomes}} \times 100\%$
Step 1: Identify parent genotypes: pure-breeding tall is **TT** and pure-breeding short is **tt**.
Step 2: Determine gametes: **TT** produces only **T** gametes; **tt** produces only **t** gametes.
Step 3: Combine gametes in a cross: all offspring receive one **T** and one **t**, making their genotype **Tt**.
Step 4: Determine phenotype: **T** is dominant, so all **Tt** offspring are tall.
Step 5: Calculate probability of a short plant (tt): number of **tt** outcomes is 0 out of a total of 4 possible combinations.
Step 6: $\text{Probability} = \frac{0}{4} \times 100\% = 0\%$.
The most tempting wrong option is C (25%), which is the mistake of assuming the parents are heterozygous (Tt × Tt) rather than pure-breeding homozygous parents.

**3. If two heterozygous tall maize plants (Tt) are crossed, what is the expected phenotypic ratio of their offspring?**
- A. 1 Tall : 2 Medium : 1 Short
- B. 3 Tall : 1 Short
- C. 1 Tall : 3 Short
- D. 2 Tall : 2 Short

**Answer: B.** 3 Tall : 1 Short.
Formula: $\text{Ratio} = \text{Count of phenotype A} : \text{Count of phenotype B}$
Step 1: Identify parent genotypes: **Tt** × **Tt**.
Step 2: Identify gametes: Both parents produce **T** and **t** gametes.
Step 3: Complete a Punnett square: offspring genotypes are 1 **TT**, 2 **Tt**, and 1 **tt** (Total = 4 outcomes).
Step 4: Count the tall phenotypes: **TT** and **Tt** both produce tall plants because T is dominant. Number of tall = $1 + 2 = 3$.
Step 5: Count the short phenotypes: only **tt** produces short plants. Number of short = $1$.
Step 6: Write the ratio: 3 Tall : 1 Short.
The most tempting wrong option is A (1:2:1), which is the mistake of confusing the *genotypic* ratio (1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt) with the *phenotypic* ratio (observable physical appearance).

**4. Which of the following statements correctly differentiates meiosis from mitosis?**
- A. Mitosis produces four daughter cells while meiosis produces two.
- B. Mitosis occurs in reproductive cells while meiosis occurs in vegetative cells.
- C. Meiosis halves the chromosome number while mitosis maintains the exact chromosome number.
- D. Meiosis is essential for vegetative propagation while mitosis is essential for sexual reproduction.

**Answer: C.** Meiosis halves the chromosome number while mitosis maintains the exact chromosome number.
Meiosis is a reduction division that produces haploid (n) cells, while mitosis produces identical diploid (2n) cells. The most tempting wrong option is B, which reverses the actual locations where the two processes occur.

**5. The main reason for placing imported crop seeds in quarantine before distribution is to**
- A. adapt them to the new environment
- B. prevent the introduction of foreign pests and diseases
- C. improve their genetic characteristics
- D. break seed dormancy

**Answer: B.** prevent the introduction of foreign pests and diseases.
Quarantine isolates incoming plant or animal materials so experts can observe them for hidden pathogens before release. It does not alter their genetics or help them adapt.

**6. A farmer crossed a local, disease-resistant N'Dama cow with an exotic, high-yielding Holstein bull. The offspring grew faster and produced more milk than both parents. This outcome is an example of**
- A. inbreeding depression
- B. line-breeding
- C. hybrid vigour (heterosis)
- D. mass selection

**Answer: C.** hybrid vigour (heterosis).
Heterosis is the phenomenon where the cross-bred offspring outperforms the average of both its parents due to the combination of diverse, superior dominant alleles. Option A, inbreeding depression, is the exact opposite and results from mating closely related animals.

**7. Which of the following is a major disadvantage of inbreeding in farm animals?**
- A. It produces hybrid vigour
- B. It brings out hidden lethal recessive traits
- C. It makes animals grow too large to handle
- D. It introduces new exotic diseases

**Answer: B.** It brings out hidden lethal recessive traits.
Because inbreeding mates close relatives (who share similar genes), there is a high chance that hidden, harmful recessive alleles will pair up (become homozygous) in the offspring, leading to deformities and loss of vigour.

**8. In artificial insemination, collected semen is preserved for a long time by freezing it in liquid nitrogen at a temperature of**
- A. 0 °C
- B. -196 °C
- C. -4 °C
- D. 100 °C

**Answer: B.** -196 °C.
Liquid nitrogen has a boiling point of roughly -196 °C, and this extreme cold safely suspends the life processes of the sperm cells so they can be stored for years without dying.

**10. A significant advantage of Artificial Insemination (AI) over natural mating is that it**
- A. guarantees that 100% of offspring will be female
- B. eliminates the need to observe when the female is on heat
