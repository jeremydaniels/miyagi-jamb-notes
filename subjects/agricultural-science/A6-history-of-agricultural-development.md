## Agricultural systems

An agricultural system is a method or practice used by farmers to produce crops and raise livestock. These systems are shaped by the climate, soil type, land availability, and the level of technology in the area. The major systems practiced in West Africa include:

- **Shifting cultivation:** A traditional system where a farmer cultivates a piece of land until its fertility drops, then completely abandons the land and relocates their family and settlement to a new, virgin forest area. The abandoned land may never be returned to. This system requires abundant land and a very low population density.
- **Bush fallowing (Land rotation):** The farmer cultivates a piece of land for a few years and then leaves it to rest (fallow) so it can regain its fertility naturally. Unlike shifting cultivation, the farmer **does not relocate their settlement**; they simply move to another plot nearby and will return to the original plot after the fallow period (usually 3 to 7 years).
- **Crop rotation:** Growing different types of crops on the same piece of land in a definite, planned sequence over several seasons. Deep-rooted crops (like yam) are often rotated with shallow-rooted ones (like maize), and legumes (like cowpea or groundnut, which add nitrogen to the soil) are included to maintain soil fertility.
- **Mixed cropping:** Growing two or more different types of crops on the same piece of land at the same time (e.g., planting maize, yam, and melon together). This is very common among traditional smallholder farmers because it maximizes land use and provides insurance against the failure of one crop.
- **Monoculture (Monocropping):** Growing only one type of crop on a piece of land at a time. It is common in commercial, mechanized farming (e.g., a large oil palm plantation or a massive rice farm). While it makes mechanization easy, it encourages the rapid spread of pests and depletes specific soil nutrients.
- **Mixed farming:** The practice of cultivating crops and rearing animals simultaneously on the same farm. This is a highly sustainable system because of the mutual benefits: the animals provide manure for the crops and can be used for draught power (ploughing), while the crop residues serve as feed for the animals.
- **Pastoral farming:** The rearing of grazing animals (like cattle, sheep, and goats). It can be **nomadic** (herdsmen moving constantly in search of pasture and water, typical of the Fulani in West Africa) or **settled** (ranching, where animals are kept in a fenced area and provided with forage).
- **Taungya system:** An agroforestry system where farmers are allowed to grow short-term arable crops (like maize, cassava, or vegetables) alongside newly planted forest tree seedlings. Once the tree canopy closes and blocks the sunlight, the farmers move out to a new clearing. This helps the government establish forests cheaply while providing fertile land for farmers.

### Compare various agricultural systems

JAMB often tests your ability to distinguish between systems that sound similar. Use the comparison tables below to understand their differences clearly.

**1. Shifting Cultivation vs. Bush Fallowing**

| Feature | Shifting Cultivation | Bush Fallowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Settlement** | The farmer completely relocates their settlement to a new area. | The farmer remains living in their permanent settlement (village). |
| **Return to land** | The abandoned land is often never cultivated again. | The farmer returns to the resting land after the fallow period. |
| **Land availability** | Requires a very sparse population and vast virgin forests. | Practiced where population is growing and land is somewhat limited. |

**2. Monoculture vs. Mixed Cropping**

| Feature | Monoculture (Monocropping) | Mixed Cropping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Number of crops** | Only one type of crop is grown at a time. | Two or more crops are grown together on the same land. |

| **Integration** | High integration (manure for crops, crop residue for animals). | No crop integration; relies solely on natural or planted pasture. |
| **Knowledge required** | Requires broad skills in both agronomy and animal husbandry. | Requires specialized knowledge of animal health and management. |

## Problems of agricultural development

Despite its vast potential, agricultural development in West Africa is held back by several serious bottlenecks. 

### Identify the problems of agricultural development

The core constraints to agricultural development in the West African region include:

1. **Land tenure systems:** Land tenure refers to the way land is owned, acquired, and held in a community. In traditional West African societies, land is typically communal (owned by the whole community or extended family) or acquired strictly through inheritance. 
2. **Inadequate infrastructures:** This refers to the severe lack of basic physical and organizational structures in rural farming communities. Examples include poor roads, lack of electricity, absence of pipe-borne water, and non-existent modern storage facilities.
3. **Inadequate finance for agriculture:** Capital is scarce for smallholder farmers. Commercial banks consider agriculture a highly risky venture, leaving most farmers trapped in a cycle of subsistence farming and poverty.
4. **Environmental degradation:** This is the deterioration of the natural environment through destructive human activities. It includes deforestation, bush burning, overgrazing, poor tillage methods, and industrial pollution (such as oil spills).
5. **Poor agricultural extension services:** There is a severe shortage of trained agricultural extension workers whose job is to take research findings and modern farming techniques from laboratories directly to the farmers.
6. **Inadequate basic tools and farm machinery:** Farming is still heavily dependent on crude, manually operated tools like the cutlass and the hoe, rather than mechanized implements like tractors and combine harvesters.

### Effects of problems and their solutions

To improve agricultural production, it is important to match each problem with its specific effect on the sector and the appropriate solution.

**1. Land tenure systems**
- **Effects:** Inheritance leads to **fragmentation** (land is continuously divided among children into tiny, scattered plots that are economically unviable). Furthermore, communal land cannot be permanently sold to individuals or used as collateral to secure bank loans. This makes large-scale mechanized commercial farming nearly impossible.
- **Solutions:** Reforming land laws (such as the Land Use Act of 1978 in Nigeria, which vests all land in the state governor to make allocation to serious farmers easier); encouraging farmers to form cooperatives to acquire large tracts of land; and land consolidation (merging small fragmented plots).

**2. Inadequate infrastructures**
- **Effects:** A high rate of **post-harvest losses** occurs because perishable produce (like tomatoes, vegetables, and yams) cannot be stored or transported to urban markets quickly. The lack of rural amenities also drives the youth to migrate to urban areas (rural-urban migration), leaving only the elderly to farm.
- **Solutions:** Governments must actively provide rural infrastructure: tarring feeder roads, implementing rural electrification projects, building solar-powered cold rooms, constructing functional silos, and providing irrigation dams.

**3. Inadequate finance for agriculture**
- **Effects:** Poor farmers lack the collateral (like land titles) demanded by commercial banks. Without finance, they cannot afford modern inputs such as tractors, high-yielding seeds, agrochemicals, and fertilizers. Consequently, farm yields and incomes remain miserably low.
- **Solutions:** Establishing specialized agricultural banks (like the Bank of Agriculture in Nigeria) to offer soft loans at single-digit interest rates; providing farm inputs at heavily subsidized rates; and encouraging farmers to save and borrow through cooperative societies.

**4. Environmental degradation**
- **Effects:** Deforestation and overgrazing expose the soil to severe erosion. Bush burning destroys soil organic matter and beneficial soil microbes. In the Niger Delta region, oil spills poison aquatic habitats, drastically reducing fish catches and rendering farmland toxic for years.
- **Solutions:** Enacting and enforcing strict environmental protection laws; practicing afforestation (planting trees); adopting soil conservation practices like cover cropping and zero tillage; and carrying out bioremediation (using microbes to clean up oil spills).

**5. Poor agricultural extension services**
- **Effects:** Rural farmers remain completely ignorant of modern farming techniques, improved seed varieties, and safe chemical handling (e.g., proper pesticide dosages), relying instead on outdated traditional methods.
- **Solutions:** The government should employ, train, and properly equip more extension workers, ensure they have vehicles (like motorcycles) to reach remote villages, and heavily utilize mass media and ICT (radio broadcasts, mobile apps) to reach farmers.

**6. Inadequate basic tools and farm machinery**
- **Effects:** Relying on the cutlass and hoe makes farming exceptionally tedious, painful, and slow (drudgery). It severely limits the acreage a farmer can cultivate, stunting total food production.
- **Solutions:** Establishing government or private tractor-hiring services at affordable rates; subsidizing farm machinery; and encouraging local engineers to fabricate cheap, simple machinery adapted to West African soils.

## Establishment of national and international research institutes

Research institutes are specialized scientific organizations set up to study agricultural challenges, breed better crops and animals, and develop modern farming techniques. 

### Trace the history of research institutes from past to present

1. **The Pre-colonial and Early Colonial Era:** Agricultural research in West Africa began in the late 19th century. The first notable milestone was the establishment of a botanical garden in Lagos in the late 19th century (sources give different dates, so do not memorise one). In **1899**, the Moor Plantation was established in Ibadan to test and multiply cash crops like rubber and cocoa.
2. **Establishment of Government Departments:** Recognizing the economic value of agriculture, the colonial government formally established the Department of Agriculture for Southern Nigeria in **1910**, followed by a similar department for Northern Nigeria in **1912**.
3. **Regional and Pan-West African Stations:** During the colonial period, regional research stations were created (e.g., at Samaru in the North and Umudike in the East) to cater to the specific crops of those ecological zones. The British also set up pan-West African institutes to serve all their colonies in the region, such as the West African Cocoa Research Institute (WACRI) and the West African Institute for Oil Palm Research (WAIFOR).
4. **Post-Independence Nationalization:** After Nigeria gained independence in 1960, these pan-West African institutes were nationalized. WAIFOR became the Nigerian Institute for Oil Palm Research (NIFOR), and WACRI became the Cocoa Research Institute of Nigeria (CRIN).
5. **Modern Era:** Today, Nigeria has numerous highly specialized national agricultural research institutes. Their activities are coordinated at the federal level by the **Agricultural Research Council of Nigeria (ARCN)**. Furthermore, heavily funded international institutes operate alongside national bodies to tackle regional and global food security.

### Assess the role of research institutes in agricultural development

The establishment of these institutes led directly to the **increased application of science** in agriculture. To understand their role, you must know the full names, locations, and primary mandates (focus crops) of these institutes, as JAMB frequently tests them.

**Overall Contributions to Agricultural Development:**
Through the work of these institutes, agricultural development is transformed in several ways:
1. **Breeding improved varieties:** Scientists use genetics to breed crop varieties and animal breeds that are high-yielding, mature early, and are resistant to harsh weather, pests, and diseases.
2. **Developing better farming systems:** They conduct soil mapping, recommend accurate fertilizer application rates (e.g., NPK ratios for different soils), and establish the best planting spacing and seed rates for different ecological zones.
3. **Pest and disease control:** Formulating effective chemical and biological control methods. For example, IITA successfully used a biological control agent (a parasitic wasp) to wipe out the cassava mealybug across Africa.
4. **Food processing and storage:** Inventing better ways to process raw farm produce into shelf-stable foods to reduce post-harvest losses.
5. **Training and extension:** Providing vital training for agricultural extension officers who then take this scientific knowledge directly to the rural farmers.

## Agricultural Development Projects (ADPs)

The Agricultural Development Projects (ADPs) were launched in Nigeria starting in **1975** (beginning with pilot projects in Gusau, Funtua, and Gombe) as a collaborative effort between the Federal Government, State Governments, and the World Bank. Today, every state in Nigeria has an ADP. They represent one of the most widespread and successful rural development strategies in West Africa.

Specific offshoots and examples of ADP initiatives include:
- **RTEP (Root and Tuber Expansion Programme):** Designed specifically to increase the production, processing, and marketing of root and tuber crops (cassava, yam, potato) to enhance food security and elevate the incomes of smallholder farmers.
- **FADAMA (National Fadama Development Project):** "Fadama" is a Hausa word for low-lying flood plains. This project provides infrastructure (like irrigation pumps and tube wells) to help farmers exploit these wet areas for **dry-season irrigated farming**, ensuring that crops (especially rice and vegetables) can be grown all year round, not just in the rainy season.

### Give reasons for the establishment of ADPs

The ADPs were set up to break the cycle of rural poverty and low productivity among smallholder farmers. Their primary reasons for establishment include:
1. **To provide effective extension services:** ADPs are the backbone of agricultural extension in Nigeria. They utilize the "Training and Visit" (T&V) system to teach farmers modern techniques directly on their farms.
2. **To improve rural infrastructure:** ADPs construct essential facilities that benefit the whole community, particularly **feeder roads** to transport produce, earth dams, and boreholes for irrigation and drinking water.
3. **To supply farm inputs:** Ensuring farmers have access to improved farm inputs (such as fertilizers, hybrid seeds, and safe agrochemicals) at the right time and at subsidized rates.
4. **To raise the standard of living:** By increasing crop yields and reducing transport bottlenecks, farmers earn higher incomes, which improves their overall quality of life and discourages rural-urban migration.

## National agricultural programmes

Over the decades, Nigerian governments have launched various ambitious, nationwide programmes aimed at solving food shortages. While many suffered from poor funding and inconsistency when governments changed, they are a vital part of the nation's agricultural history. Examples include:
- **NAFPP:** National Accelerated Food Production Project
- **OFN:** Operation Feed the Nation
- **Green Revolution**
- **NALDA:** National Agricultural Land Development Authority
- **NCRPs:** Nationally Coordinated Research Programmes
- **NARP:** National Agricultural Research Project
- **PCU:** Project Coordinating Unit

### Evaluate the contributions of national agricultural programmes

Each of these programmes made specific contributions to the development of agriculture in the region:

- **NAFPP (National Accelerated Food Production Project):**
  - *Launched:* 1972/1973 by Gen. Yakubu Gowon.
  - *Contributions:* It introduced a "package approach," combining agricultural research, extension, and the rapid distribution of high-yielding seeds and fertilizers to farmers. It significantly boosted the yields of staples like maize, rice, and cassava before it phased out.

- **OFN (Operation Feed the Nation):**
  - *Launched:* 1976 by Gen. Olusegun Obasanjo's military regime.
  - *Contributions:* It successfully created massive public awareness about the looming food crisis. It mobilized everyone—students, soldiers, civil servants, and ordinary citizens—to embrace farming and grow food in any available space, distributing highly subsidized inputs.

- **Green Revolution:**
  - *Launched:* 1980 by President Shehu Shagari's civilian government.
  - *Contributions:* Aiming to wipe out hunger and achieve complete self-sufficiency in staple foods, it promoted large-scale, commercial mechanized farming. It provided tractors, land-clearing equipment, and heavily subsidized fertilizers to farmers.

- **NALDA (National Agricultural Land Development Authority):**
  - *Launched:* 1992 by Gen. Ibrahim Babangida.
  - *Contributions:* NALDA directly tackled the problem of strict communal land tenure systems. The authority acquired vast tracts of idle land, cleared and prepared it, and allocated it in workable plots to interested farmers, drastically reducing the bottlenecks of land acquisition.

- **NCRPs (Nationally Coordinated Research Programmes):**
  - *Contributions:* Before any new crop variety or farming technique is recommended to the public, the NCRPs organize multi-locational field trials across Nigeria's diverse ecological zones (from the rainforest to the Sahel savanna). This ensures that a crop will actually perform well in a specific region before farmers adopt it.

- **NARP (National Agricultural Research Project):**
  - *Contributions:* It injected critical funding into the national agricultural research system. It rehabilitated decaying infrastructure in research institutes and universities and provided advanced training for scientists, ensuring that research remained robust.

- **PCU (Project Coordinating Unit):**
  - *Contributions:* Established under the Federal Ministry of Agriculture, the PCU acts as the central administrative body. It effectively coordinates, monitors, and evaluates all World Bank and Federal Government-assisted projects (including all the ADPs) across the states, ensuring that funds are utilized properly and goals are met.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse shifting cultivation with bush fallowing.** In shifting cultivation, the farmer relocates their settlement permanently. In bush fallowing, the farmer stays in their village but rotates the farm plots.
- **Know your acronyms perfectly.** JAMB frequently tests the exact meaning of institute initials. Remember that **CRIN** is for Cocoa (and other tree crops), **IAR** is for Institute for Agricultural Research (handling sorghum, groundnut, and cotton), and **NCRI** is for Cereals (handling rice and sugarcane).
- **FADAMA equals dry-season farming.** If a question mentions fadama, look for options relating to irrigation, flood plains, or dry-season agriculture.
- **Differentiate historical programmes by regime and year.** NAFPP (1972/73), OFN (1976 - Military), Green Revolution (1980 - Civilian), NALDA (1992). Do not mix up their core objectives.
- **Understand land tenure problems.** The biggest problem with communal land is that a farmer cannot sell it or use it as collateral for a bank loan, which severely limits access to agricultural finance.

## Quick summary

1. Agricultural systems range from traditional methods like bush fallowing and mixed cropping to modern commercial monoculture and mixed farming.
2. Major bottlenecks to agricultural development include restrictive communal land tenure, lack of rural infrastructure, poor access to finance, and environmental degradation.
3. Solutions to these problems require government intervention: providing feeder roads, credit facilities through agricultural banks, and enacting the Land Use Act.
4. Agricultural research in West Africa evolved from colonial botanical gardens to highly specialized national and international institutes that apply science to breed better crops and improve farming systems.
5. Key institutes include NCRI (cereals), CRIN (tree crops), NIFOR (oil palm), IITA (tropical crops), and AfricaRice/WARDA (rice).
6. ADPs were established with World Bank help to provide essential rural infrastructure, farm inputs, and extension services. Examples include FADAMA (dry season farming) and RTEP (root crops).
7. National programmes like OFN, NAFPP, the Green Revolution, and NALDA were launched historically to mobilize citizens, provide land, distribute inputs, and aim for national food security.

## Practice questions

**1. The agricultural system in which a farmer cultivates a piece of land for a period and then completely abandons it, relocating their settlement to a new area is called:**

- A. bush fallowing
- B. crop rotation
- C. shifting cultivation
- D. mixed farming

**Answer: C.** In shifting cultivation, the farmer abandons the depleted land and completely relocates their settlement. In bush fallowing (Option A), the farmer leaves the land to rest but stays in the same settlement.

**2. A major problem of the communal land tenure system in agricultural development is that it:**

- A. encourages large scale commercial farming
- B. prevents the use of land as collateral for bank loans
- C. reduces the cost of agricultural inputs
- D. leads to soil erosion and desertification

**Answer: B.** Because communal land is owned by the entire community, an individual cannot claim permanent ownership to secure loans from commercial banks. 

**3. The research institute mandated to improve the production of cocoa, kola, cashew, coffee, and tea in Nigeria is:**

- A. NIFOR
- B. CRIN
- C. NRCRI
- D. NIHORT

**Answer: B.** The Cocoa Research Institute of Nigeria (CRIN) handles these specific tree crops. NIFOR handles oil palm and date palm.

**4. WARDA, an international agricultural research institute, focuses primarily on the improvement of:**

- A. wheat
- B. maize
- C. rice
- D. sorghum

**Answer: C.** WARDA (West Africa Rice Development Association, now AfricaRice) focuses entirely on rice improvement in Africa. Wheat is handled by LCRI.

**5. The agricultural programme introduced in 1976 by the Nigerian military government to massively mobilize the public for food production was:**

- A. Green Revolution
- B. Operation Feed the Nation (OFN)
- C. NALDA
- D. NAFPP

**Answer: B.** Operation Feed the Nation (OFN) was launched in 1976 by the military regime to create awareness and mobilize citizens to farm. The Green Revolution was launched later in 1980.

**6. Which of the following is a primary reason for the establishment of Agricultural Development Projects (ADPs)?**

- A. To export raw materials to foreign countries
- B. To provide rural infrastructure and effective extension services
- C. To import heavily mechanized farm tools for the urban elite
- D. To take over communal lands from local farmers

**Answer: B.** ADPs were established to provide essential rural infrastructure (like feeder roads) and deliver agricultural extension services to boost rural farmers' productivity.

**7. The practice of growing arable agricultural crops on the same piece of land alongside forestry trees in their early stages of growth is known as:**

- A. taungya system
- B. crop rotation
- C. pastoral farming
- D. bush fallowing

**Answer: A.** The taungya system is an agroforestry method that combines the early stages of tree growth with arable crop farming.

**8. FADAMA projects were established in Nigeria mainly to encourage:**

- A. intensive poultry production
- B. dry season irrigated farming on flood plains
- C. the mechanization of tree crop harvesting
- D. deep-sea and inland fishing

**Answer: B.** Fadamas are low-lying flood plains rich in groundwater, utilized through this project to ensure year-round, dry-season irrigated crop production.
