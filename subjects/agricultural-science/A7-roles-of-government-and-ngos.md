## Fiscal policies favourable to agricultural production

A **fiscal policy** is a government's strategy for using revenue collection (taxation) and expenditure (spending) to influence the economy. In the agricultural sector, the government uses fiscal policies to protect local farmers, lower the cost of farming, and encourage the export of agricultural products.

Key fiscal policies applied to agriculture include:

- **Import duties (Tariffs):** These are taxes imposed on agricultural goods imported from other countries. By placing a high tariff on items like imported rice, poultry, or frozen fish, the government makes foreign products more expensive than locally grown ones.
- **Ban on importation:** This is an extreme fiscal measure where the government completely prohibits the entry of certain foreign agricultural products. For example, Nigeria has at various times banned the importation of frozen chicken and certain grains to force the country to rely on local farmers.
- **Export duties reduction or elimination:** To encourage farmers to produce for the international market and earn foreign exchange, the government may reduce or completely remove taxes on agricultural exports (like cocoa, rubber, and sesame seeds).
- **Tax holidays and exemptions:** A tax holiday is a temporary period during which a new business is excused from paying taxes. The government often grants tax holidays to new agro-allied industries to help them survive their first few years. Furthermore, agricultural machinery (like tractors) and agrochemicals are often exempted from import duties to make them cheaper for farmers to acquire.

## Evaluate the effects of government policies

Government fiscal policies have significant impacts—both positive and negative—on agricultural development.

**Positive effects:**
1. **Stimulation of local production:** High tariffs or import bans protect farmers from foreign competition, guaranteeing a ready market. This encourages them to expand their farms and produce more food.
2. **Increase in farmers' income:** Protected from cheaper foreign goods, local farmers can sell their produce at profitable prices, improving their standard of living.
3. **Conservation of foreign exchange:** By growing food locally instead of spending dollars to import it, the country saves its foreign currency reserves, which stabilizes the economy.
4. **Employment generation:** When agriculture becomes profitable, it attracts young people and investors, reducing the unemployment rate and creating jobs in processing, packaging, and marketing.
5. **Cheaper farm inputs:** Tax exemptions on imported machinery and chemicals reduce the overall cost of production for farmers.

**Negative effects:**
1. **Smuggling:** When a high-demand product is banned or heavily taxed (e.g., foreign rice), it often leads to illegal smuggling across borders.
2. **Inflation and food scarcity:** If local farmers are not adequately supported to meet the huge demand left by an import ban, food becomes scarce, and prices skyrocket, causing hardship for consumers.
3. **Retaliation by trading partners:** If a country places heavy taxes on imports, other countries may retaliate by placing high taxes on that country's agricultural exports, which hurts local farmers trying to sell abroad.

## Agricultural laws and reforms

Agricultural laws are rules enacted by the government to regulate how farming is practiced, manage land ownership, protect the environment, and ensure food safety.

Important types of agricultural laws include:
- **Land tenure laws:** Laws that regulate how land is owned and used. The most important in Nigeria is the **Land Use Act of 1978**.
- **Quarantine laws:** These laws regulate the movement of plants and animals across international borders. Any imported agricultural material must undergo a period of strict isolation and testing (quarantine) before being released. In Nigeria, the **Nigerian Agricultural Quarantine Service (NAQS)** enforces these laws.
- **Forestry and Wildlife laws:** These regulations aim to protect forests and endangered animal species. They prohibit indiscriminate logging (tree felling), mandate the planting of new trees (afforestation), and ban poaching in game reserves and national parks.
- **Environmental protection laws:** These laws control the use of dangerous agrochemicals (like certain pesticides) to prevent the pollution of water bodies and the destruction of the soil ecosystem.

## Identify agricultural laws and their effect

When evaluating the effect of agricultural laws on production, the **Land Use Act of 1978** is the most significant and frequently tested. It was promulgated by the military government of General Olusegun Obasanjo to solve the severe problems associated with customary land ownership, where families would refuse to sell land to investors.

**Key features of the Land Use Act:**
1. **Vesting of land in the Governor:** The Act states that all land within a state's territory is vested in the Governor of that state, who holds it in trust and administers it for the use and common benefit of all Nigerians.
2. **Certificate of Occupancy (C of O):** Individuals and corporate bodies do not "own" land permanently. Instead, they are granted a statutory right of occupancy (usually for a maximum of 99 years), backed by a legal document called a Certificate of Occupancy.
3. **Maximum land limits for agriculture:** To prevent a few wealthy individuals from hoarding all available land, the Act sets strict maximum limits for agricultural land acquisition:
   - For **crop farming**: a maximum of **500 hectares**.
   - For **grazing (livestock farming)**: a maximum of **5,000 hectares**.
4. **Age restriction:** A person must be at least **21 years old** to be granted a statutory right of occupancy.

**Step-by-step process of acquiring land under the Act for agriculture:**
1. **Identification:** The farmer identifies a suitable piece of rural land and negotiates with the local community or customary holders.
2. **Survey:** A registered surveyor is hired to prepare a survey plan of the land.
3. **Application:** An application form and the survey plan are submitted to the state's Ministry of Lands.
4. **Vetting:** The application is checked to ensure the land requested does not exceed the legal limits (500 ha for crops, 5,000 ha for grazing).
5. **Payment:** The farmer pays the required official fees and taxes.
6. **Approval:** The State Governor approves and signs the Certificate of Occupancy, officially granting the right to use the land.

**Effects of the Land Use Act on agricultural production:**
- **Positive effects:** It makes it easier for genuine investors to acquire large tracts of land for commercial farming. It reduces severe land disputes and litigations. It overrides discriminatory local customs, allowing men and women from any state to acquire land anywhere in the country. It prevents the fragmentation of land into tiny, unproductive plots.
- **Negative effects:** The process of acquiring a Certificate of Occupancy is often slow, heavily bureaucratic, and expensive, which discourages poor rural farmers. Local farming communities can be displaced from their ancestral lands.

**Effects of Quarantine laws:**
- **Positive effect:** They prevent the introduction and spread of foreign, devastating pests and diseases (like Avian Influenza in poultry) which could wipe out local agricultural production.

## Government programmes aimed at agricultural development

Since independence, the Nigerian government has launched several agricultural programmes and agencies to boost food production, provide inputs, and develop rural areas. You must know their full names, acronyms, and historical functions.

1. **National Accelerated Food Production Project (NAFPP):** Launched in 1972, this was one of the earliest programmes aimed at increasing the production of grains (maize, rice, millet) and root crops (cassava, yam) by introducing high-yielding varieties and fertilizers to farmers.
2. **Operation Feed the Nation (OFN):** Launched in 1976 by the military regime, its primary goal was to popularize agriculture and achieve national self-sufficiency in food. It encouraged everyone—including schools, the military, and civil servants—to grow their own food.
3. **Green Revolution:** Launched in 1980 by the civilian government to replace OFN. It focused on achieving self-sufficiency through massive investment in modern farming technology, large-scale irrigation, and the provision of improved seeds.
4. **Agricultural Development Programmes (ADPs):** Established in collaboration with the World Bank, ADPs operate in every state in Nigeria. Their main function is to provide **agricultural extension services**—sending extension workers to rural areas to teach farmers modern techniques and distribute inputs.
5. **River Basin Development Authorities (RBDAs):** Established to harness water resources. Their primary aim is to construct dams and boreholes to provide water for **irrigation** (dry-season farming), livestock, and domestic use.
6. **Directorate of Food, Roads and Rural Infrastructure (DFRRI):** Established in 1986 to open up rural areas by constructing feeder roads, providing rural electrification, and sinking boreholes to improve the quality of life for rural farmers.
7. **National Agricultural Land Development Authority (NALDA):** Established specifically to execute large-scale **land clearing and land preparation**. Its goal was to open up new lands for farmers who could not afford heavy land-clearing machinery.
8. **National Fadama Development Project (FADAMA):** "Fadama" is a Hausa word for low-lying, irrigable floodplains. This project focuses on utilizing these wetlands for **dry-season farming** through small-scale irrigation, increasing the year-round income of rural farmers.
9. **Root and Tuber Expansion Programme (RTEP):** Aimed to increase the production and processing of root and tuber crops (like cassava, yam, sweet potato) to ensure food security.

## Identify agricultural incentives provided by the government

In addition to launching broad programmes, the government provides specific agricultural incentives. These are financial or material encouragements designed to make farming less risky, cheaper, and more profitable.

1. **Subsidies:** The government pays a portion of the cost of expensive farm inputs (such as fertilizers, improved seeds, and agrochemicals) so that farmers can buy them at a significantly reduced price. A subsidy is financial aid; it is not a loan and does not need to be repaid.
2. **Credit facilities:** These are loans provided to farmers at very low interest rates. The government establishes specialized agricultural banks, such as the **Bank of Agriculture (BOA)**, and schemes like the **Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund (ACGSF)** to encourage commercial banks to lend to farmers.
3. **Guaranteed Minimum Price (GMP):** This is a policy where the government promises to buy agricultural produce from farmers at a fixed baseline price. 
4. **Input supply:** The government directly procures and distributes essential inputs like high-yielding seeds, day-old chicks, fingerlings, and fertilizers to rural farmers, often through the ADPs.
5. **Tractor hiring services:** Since heavy machinery is too expensive for most farmers to buy, the government provides tractors and implements at local centers that farmers can rent for a few days at a subsidized rate.

**Step-by-step process of the Guaranteed Minimum Price (GMP):**
1. The government sets a baseline price for a crop (e.g., maize) before the harvest season begins.
2. Farmers harvest their crops. If there is a massive bumper harvest, the open market price drops drastically due to oversupply.
3. If the market price falls below the GMP, farmers can take their produce to a designated government buyer (e.g., a commodity board).
4. The government buys the produce at the GMP, ensuring the farmer covers their production costs and avoids a financial loss.
5. The government stores this produce in silos and releases it to the market later during the dry season to stabilize consumer food prices.

## Assess the effects of agricultural incentives

The provision of these agricultural incentives has profound effects on the development of the agricultural sector:

- **Reduces the cost of production:** Subsidies on inputs and low-interest credit facilities drastically lower the amount of money farmers need to spend to run their farms.
- **Increases yield and productivity:** Access to subsidized improved seeds, fertilizers, and mechanized equipment ensures a higher output per hectare compared to traditional farming methods.
- **Reduces financial risk:** The Guaranteed Minimum Price protects farmers from the devastating effects of price fluctuations, making agriculture a safer and more stable business.
- **Attracts investment and youth participation:** When farming is made less tedious (through tractor hiring) and more profitable (through credit), younger generations and corporate investors are encouraged to participate.
- **Ensures food security:** By providing these incentives, the government ensures that farmers produce enough food year-round to feed the growing population.

## Provision of infrastructures

Infrastructures are the basic physical systems, structures, and facilities required for a society, community, or business to function efficiently. In agriculture, even the best policies, seeds, and fertilizers will fail if the necessary infrastructure is not in place to support the farmers.

## Compare the various infrastructural facilities

The table below compares the various infrastructural facilities provided by the government, contrasting their specific uses in agricultural development.

| Type of Infrastructure | Examples | Primary Uses in Agriculture |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Transport systems** | Feeder (rural) roads, railways, waterways, airports | Used for moving farm inputs (fertilizers, seeds, machinery) to the farm, and transporting harvested produce from rural areas to urban markets. Good roads reduce the cost of transport and prevent the spoilage of perishable goods during transit. |
| **Communication systems** | Radio, television, mobile phone networks, the internet | Crucial for **agricultural extension services**. Communication systems are used to rapidly broadcast weather forecasts, current market prices, disease outbreak warnings, and new farming techniques to remote farmers. |
| **Storage and processing facilities** | Silos, warehouses, cold rooms, abattoirs, rice mills, cassava graters | Used to preserve harvested produce, thereby preventing post-harvest losses. Schools provide basic literacy, which is necessary for farmers to read instructions on agrochemical labels and keep accurate farm records. |

## Contribution of NGOs to agricultural development

A **Non-Governmental Organization (NGO)** is a private, non-profit organization that operates independently of the government. NGOs are established to address specific social, environmental, or economic issues. While the government creates laws and national programmes, NGOs often work directly at the grassroots level to fill the gaps left by the government, especially in remote rural communities.

*(Note: International research institutes like the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) and the International Livestock Research Institute (ILRI) are officially intergovernmental research centers, but in the context of the JAMB syllabus, they operate similarly to NGOs by working independently to provide research, funding, and extension services to African farmers).*

## Examine the roles of NGOs

NGOs play a vital and direct role in agricultural development. Their main contributions include:

1. **Funding and Micro-credit:** NGOs provide financial assistance, grants, and micro-loans to poor rural farmers who lack the collateral required to borrow money from traditional commercial banks.
2. **Capacity Building and Training (Extension):** They employ extension workers to train farmers on modern, sustainable, and climate-smart agricultural practices. NGOs frequently set up **demonstration farms** to physically show farmers how new techniques and improved seeds perform compared to local varieties.
3. **Provision of Inputs:** NGOs often distribute essential agricultural inputs—such as improved seeds, fertilizers, agrochemicals, farm tools, and livestock (e.g., day-old chicks or pregnant goats)—to vulnerable or crisis-affected communities.
4. **Rural Development:** They assist in building small-scale rural infrastructures. An NGO might fund the sinking of a borehole for clean water, construct a small community storage facility, or help repair a vital feeder road.
5. **Research and Information Dissemination:** Many NGOs fund or conduct agricultural research to develop disease-resistant and high-yielding crop varieties. They then disseminate this information directly to the farmers.
6. **Advocacy and Human Rights:** NGOs serve as the voice of the farmers. They advocate for better government policies, campaign for environmental protection against pollution (such as advocating for the clean-up of oil spills in the Niger Delta), and fight for gender equality, ensuring that women have the right to own agricultural land.

**Examples of NGOs operating in West African agriculture:**
- **Leventis Foundation Nigeria:** Operates several agricultural training schools across Nigeria, offering free, intensive, year-long training programmes to youths to equip them to become self-employed modern farmers.
- **Heifer International:** Combats poverty and hunger by providing livestock (such as goats, cows, and poultry) and intensive training to rural families. The families are required to pass on the first female offspring of their livestock to another needy family.
- **ActionAid:** Works extensively to alleviate poverty, advocates for the land rights of smallholder farmers, and strongly empowers women in agriculture.
- **The Hunger Project:** Focuses on rural community empowerment, organizing farmers into cooperatives, and achieving long-term food security.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Memorize the Land Use Act limits:** JAMB frequently asks for the maximum land allocations under the Land Use Act. It is **500 hectares** for crop production and **5,000 hectares** for grazing (livestock). Also, the applicant must be at least **21 years old**.
- **Know the year of the Land Use Act:** The Act was promulgated in **1978**.
- **Distinguish between subsidies and credit:** A **subsidy** is money the government pays so that you can buy items cheaper (you do not pay a subsidy back). A **credit facility** is a loan (you must pay it back, usually with low interest).
- **Match programmes to their core functions:** 
  - FADAMA = dry-season / irrigation farming.
  - NALDA = land clearing and preparation.
  - ADPs = agricultural extension and input distribution.
  - RBDAs = water resources and large-scale irrigation (dams).
- **NGOs do not make laws:** A common trick question asks which of the following is a role of an NGO. Remember that NGOs are private entities; they cannot enact national laws, issue Certificates of Occupancy, impose taxes, or arrest smugglers.
- **Infrastructure uses:** If a question asks which infrastructure is primarily used for the rapid dissemination of agricultural extension information, the answer is **communication systems** (radio, TV), not transport. Transport is for moving physical goods.

## Quick summary

1. The government uses fiscal policies like import duties, import bans, and tax holidays to protect local farmers, stimulate production, and conserve foreign exchange.
2. Agricultural laws like quarantine laws prevent the spread of foreign diseases, while forestry laws protect wildlife and the environment.
3. The Land Use Act of 1978 vested all land in the state Governor and set maximum limits for agricultural land acquisition (500 ha for crops, 5,000 ha for grazing) to make land easier for genuine farmers to acquire.
4. Government agricultural incentives include subsidies, low-interest credit facilities, and guaranteed minimum prices, which lower the cost of production and make farming a safer business.
5. Historic government programmes aimed at boosting agriculture include OFN (popularizing agriculture), Green Revolution, ADPs (extension services), NALDA (land preparation), and FADAMA (dry-season farming).
6. Infrastructures like transport (roads for moving produce), communication (radio for extension), and water supply (dams for irrigation) are absolutely essential for a functioning agricultural sector.
7. NGOs assist in agricultural development independently of the government by providing funding, training farmers, distributing inputs, building rural infrastructure, and advocating for farmers' rights.

## Practice questions

**1. Under the Nigerian Land Use Act of 1978, what is the maximum area of land that can be granted to an individual for grazing purposes?**
- A. 500 hectares
- B. 1,000 hectares
- C. 2,000 hectares
- D. 5,000 hectares

**Answer: D.** The Land Use Act limits land acquisition to a maximum of 5,000 hectares for grazing (livestock) and 500 hectares for crop production.

**2. Which of the following government policies is most likely to encourage local farmers to produce more rice?**
- A. Removing taxes on imported rice
- B. Placing a ban on the importation of rice
- C. Increasing the export duty on locally produced rice
- D. Reducing the subsidies given to local farmers

**Answer: B.** A ban on importation stops foreign rice from entering the country, forcing consumers to buy locally, which stimulates local production. Removing taxes on imports would flood the market with cheap foreign rice, hurting local farmers.

**3. The policy where the government promises to buy leftover farm produce from farmers at a fixed price to prevent them from running at a loss is called?**
- A. Subsidy
- B. Tax holiday
- C. Guaranteed minimum price
- D. Agricultural credit

**Answer: C.** A guaranteed minimum price ensures farmers receive a baseline price for their goods, protecting them during times of bumper harvest when open market prices drop. 

**4. The primary objective of agricultural plant and animal quarantine laws is to?**
- A. Stop farmers from travelling out of the country
- B. Prevent the introduction and spread of foreign pests and diseases
- C. Confiscate farm produce from local markets
- D. Force farmers to pay heavy taxes on their livestock

**Answer: B.** Quarantine laws mandate the strict isolation and testing of imported agricultural goods to ensure they do not bring dangerous foreign diseases, pests, or weeds into the country.

**5. Which of the following infrastructural facilities is primarily responsible for the rapid dissemination of agricultural extension information to rural farmers?**
- A. Transport systems
- B. Storage facilities
- C. Communication systems
- D. Health centers

**Answer: C.** Communication systems, such as radio, television, and mobile networks, allow agricultural extension workers to rapidly broadcast weather forecasts, market prices, and new techniques to farmers far away.

**6. The government agency established primarily to execute large-scale land clearing and preparation for farmers is the?**
- A. National Agricultural Land Development Authority (NALDA)
- B. Bank of Agriculture (BOA)
- C. Operation Feed the Nation (OFN)
- D. Nigerian Agricultural Quarantine Service (NAQS)

**Answer: A.** NALDA was established specifically to develop land (clearing and preparation) to make it ready for agricultural use, as heavy land-clearing machinery is often too expensive for individual farmers.

**7. Which of the following is NOT a role of Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) in agricultural development?**
- A. Providing micro-credit to rural farmers
- B. Formulating and enacting national agricultural laws
- C. Building the capacity of farmers through training
- D. Providing agricultural inputs like seeds and tools

**Answer: B.** NGOs are private, non-profit organizations. They do not have the legal power or authority to formulate or enact national laws; that is strictly the role of the government.

**8. The government provides agricultural subsidies mainly to?**
- A. Increase the cost of farming
- B. Reduce the cost of agricultural inputs for farmers
- C. Force farmers to pay back loans with high interest
- D. Discourage youths from taking up agriculture

**Answer: B.** A subsidy is financial aid from the government that covers part of the cost of inputs (like fertilizers and seeds), making them cheaper and more accessible for farmers. It does not need to be paid back.

**9. The agricultural programme established in Nigeria specifically to boost dry-season farming through small-scale irrigation is the?**
- A. Green Revolution
- B. Directorate of Food, Roads and Rural Infrastructure (DFRRI)
- C. National Fadama Development Project (FADAMA)
- D. Root and Tuber Expansion Programme (RTEP)

**Answer: C.** "Fadama" refers to irrigable, low-lying floodplains. The FADAMA project focuses on utilizing these wetlands for dry-season farming through small-scale irrigation to ensure year-round income.

**10. Under the Land Use Act of 1978, what is the minimum age required for a person to be granted a statutory right of occupancy?**
- A. 18 years
- B. 21 years
- C. 25 years
- D. 30 years

**Answer: B.** The Act specifies that a person must be at least 21 years old to be legally granted a statutory right of occupancy (and issued a Certificate of Occupancy) for land.
