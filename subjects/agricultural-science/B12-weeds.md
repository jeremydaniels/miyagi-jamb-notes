# JAMB Agricultural Science Notes: Weeds and Weed Control

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B, Topic 12 of the JAMB Agricultural Science syllabus: Weeds. It covers the identification, biology, and management of agricultural weeds.

## What is a weed?

A **weed** is simply a plant growing where it is not wanted. 

In agriculture, any plant that interferes with the objectives of farming is a weed. This means a plant is not naturally a weed; its status depends entirely on the situation. For example, if a maize seed accidentally germinates in a cassava farm, that maize plant is considered a weed because it was not planted there and will compete with the cassava for nutrients. However, there are many wild plants (such as spear grass and Siam weed) that have no economic value on a farm and are universally regarded as weeds.

## Characteristic features of weeds

**Objective iii: Identify the characteristic features of weeds.**

Weeds are highly successful competitors that can easily outgrow cultivated crops. They possess special biological and physical characteristic features (adaptations) that allow them to survive harsh conditions and resist eradication:

1. **Prolific and high reproductive capacity:** A single weed plant (like *Amaranthus* or pigweed) can produce tens of thousands or even millions of seeds in a single growing season. This ensures that even if many seeds die, thousands will survive.
2. **Long seed dormancy:** Weed seeds can remain alive (viable) but inactive in the soil for many years. This hidden supply of seeds is called the "seed bank". The seeds only break dormancy and germinate when conditions (like rainfall, sunlight, and soil tillage) become perfectly favourable.
3. **Aggressive and rapid growth rate:** Weeds usually germinate and grow much faster than crop plants. This allows them to quickly form a canopy over the soil, blocking sunlight from reaching the slower-growing crop seedlings.
4. **Deep and extensive root systems:** Weeds often have taproots that can reach deep underground water sources during severe dry periods, while their widespread fibrous roots can quickly absorb surface moisture and fertilizer before the crop can.
5. **Efficient vegetative propagation:** Many troublesome perennial weeds can regenerate from tiny, broken plant parts (like stems, rhizomes, or tubers) left in the soil after a farmer ploughs or hoes the land.
6. **Efficient dispersal mechanisms:** Weeds possess structures like wings, parachutes, hooks, or buoyant waterproof tissues that allow them to spread rapidly across large distances via wind, animals, or water.
7. **Mimicry:** Some weeds look exactly like the crop at the seedling stage (e.g., wild rice growing in a cultivated rice field). This visual similarity makes it very difficult for farmers to identify and uproot the weeds manually without destroying the crop.
8. **Hardiness:** Weeds can survive extreme adverse environmental conditions such as severe drought, poor soil fertility, high temperatures, and waterlogging.

## Gross morphology, reproduction, dispersal and effects of weeds

**Content a: Gross morphology, methods of reproduction, dispersal and effect of weeds**

### Gross morphology of weeds

"Gross morphology" refers to the general physical structure, shape, and appearance of the plant. Based on their morphology, agricultural weeds are broadly grouped into three major categories:

1. **Grasses (Monocots):**
   - **Leaves:** They have long, narrow leaves with parallel veins. 
   - **Stems:** Their stems are usually hollow and round (called culms) with distinct solid joints called nodes.
   - **Roots:** They possess a fibrous root system that spreads widely near the soil surface.
   - **Examples:** *Imperata cylindrica* (Spear grass), *Panicum maximum* (Guinea grass).
2. **Sedges (Monocots):**
   - **Leaves:** They look very similar to grasses with narrow leaves, but the leaves are often arranged in groups of three.
   - **Stems:** They usually have solid, triangular stems (a helpful memory trick is: "sedges have edges"). They do not have swollen nodes.
   - **Roots:** They often grow from underground modified stems (tubers) and are commonly found in wet, poorly drained, or swampy soils.
   - **Example:** *Cyperus rotundus* (Nut grass).
3. **Broadleaves (Dicots):**
   - **Leaves:** They have wide (broad) leaves with a net-like (reticulate) arrangement of veins.
   - **Stems:** They usually possess a solid, highly branching stem that can become woody as the plant ages.
   - **Roots:** They typically have a strong, deep taproot system.
   - **Examples:** *Tridax procumbens*, *Chromolaena odorata* (Siam weed).

### Methods of reproduction in weeds

Weeds reproduce through two main methods to ensure their survival and rapid multiplication across the farm: sexually and asexually (vegetatively).

1. **Sexual reproduction (by seeds):** Most weeds reproduce through seeds formed after flowering, pollination, and fertilization. Seeds are highly resilient and can withstand severe drought and heat. Annual weeds (weeds that complete their life cycle in one year) like *Ageratum conyzoides* (goat weed) and *Tridax* rely entirely on seeds for reproduction.
2. **Asexual or vegetative reproduction:** Many troublesome perennial weeds (weeds that live for more than two years) reproduce using vegetative parts instead of seeds. If a farmer uses a hoe to chop the weed, the hidden underground parts simply sprout into multiple new plants.
   * **Rhizomes:** These are underground horizontal stems that store food. When chopped into pieces by a plough, each piece grows into a new plant. Example: *Imperata cylindrica* (Spear grass).
   * **Stolons:** These are above-ground horizontal stems (runners) that creep along the soil surface, taking root at the nodes. Example: *Cynodon dactylon* (Bahama grass).
   * **Tubers and Bulbs:** Swollen underground storage organs. Example: *Cyperus rotundus* (Nut grass) propagates rapidly using underground tubers.

### Dispersal of weeds

Dispersal is the process of scattering weed seeds, fruits, or vegetative parts away from the parent plant to new environments. Dispersal ensures that weeds colonise new farmland and avoid overcrowding. Weeds rely on various natural agents (wind, water, animals) and physical mechanisms to achieve this. The specific classification of weeds based on these agents is highly important and is detailed in the next section.

### Effects of weeds on agriculture

Weeds cause massive economic losses in West African agriculture. While they have a few minor positive uses (such as preventing soil erosion or acting as forage for livestock), their negative effects far outweigh any benefits.

**Negative effects include:**
1. **Severe Competition:** Weeds compete vigorously with crops for essential resources: sunlight, space, soil moisture, and soil nutrients. Because weeds are aggressive, this competition causes stunted crop growth and severe reductions in crop yield.
2. **Alternate hosts for pests and diseases:** Weeds harbour insects and disease-causing pathogens (like viruses, bacteria, and fungi) when the main crop is not on the farm. When the farmer plants the crop, these pests migrate from the weed to attack the crop. For example, some weeds act as hosts for the root-knot nematodes that attack yams and tomatoes, or the whiteflies that carry the cassava mosaic virus.
3. **Reduction in crop quality:** Weed seeds can get mixed with harvested crops (e.g., weed seeds accidentally bagged with harvested rice paddy or cowpea). This lowers the market value and grading of the farm produce.
4. **Increased cost of production:** Farmers spend a large amount of money, time, and labour on weeding (buying expensive herbicides, hiring labourers, fueling tractors for tillage). This reduces the farmer's net profit.
5. **Toxicity to livestock:** Some weeds contain poisonous chemicals that can kill farm animals if eaten in pasture, or they can give a bad taint (taste and smell) to a dairy cow's milk.
6. **Hindrance to farm operations:** Thick, thorny, or creeping weeds make it difficult for farmers and machines to move through the farm during field maintenance, fertilizer application, or harvesting.

## Classification of weeds by their mode of dispersal

**Objective ii: classify weeds according to their mode of dispersal.**

JAMB expects you to classify weeds based on the structural adaptations of their seeds and the agents that transport them. The four main modes of dispersal are:

| Mode of Dispersal | Mechanism and Adaptation | Common Weed Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wind Dispersal (Anemochory)** | Seeds are very light and often have hair-like parachutes (called **pappus**) or thin papery wings. These adaptations allow them to be caught by the wind and blown over very long distances. | *Tridax procumbens* (Tridax), *Chromolaena odorata* (Siam weed), *Emilia sonchifolia*. |
| **Animal and Human Dispersal (Zoochory)** | Seeds have tiny hooks, spines, or sticky hairs that cling tightly to an animal's fur or a farmer's clothing. Alternatively, animals eat succulent weed fruits and pass the tough, indigestible seeds out undamaged in their faeces. | *Desmodium* species, *Boerhavia diffusa* (Pigweed), *Acanthospermum hispidum* (Starburr), *Amaranthus*. |
| **Water Dispersal (Hydrochory)** | Seeds and fruits are buoyant (can float) and have waterproof coats or spongy tissues filled with air. This allows them to be carried by rain runoff, rivers, or irrigation canals without rotting. | *Commelina* species (Water dayflower), Water hyacinth (*Eichhornia crassipes*). |
| **Explosive Mechanism (Autochory)** | The dry seed pods split open violently with a twisting action when mature and dried by the sun, physically throwing seeds several metres away from the parent plant without any external agent. | *Calopogonium mucunoides*, *Centrosema pubescens* (when acting as weeds), *Mucuna* species. |

## The different weed control methods

**Content b: Weed control methods – weeding, mulching, cover cropping, tillage, herbicides and trap cropping**

To ensure high crop yields, farmers must control weeds. The syllabus outlines several specific methods ranging from manual labour to advanced chemical controls.

### 1. Physical / Mechanical Weeding

Physical weeding involves the direct physical removal of the weed from the soil.
* **Hand pulling:** Physically uprooting weeds by hand. This is only useful for very small gardens, nursery beds, or when weed populations are very low.
* **Hoeing and slashing:** Using simple farm tools like the cutlass (machete) to cut down tall weeds and the hoe to dig out weed roots. This is the most common and highly labour-intensive method used in traditional Nigerian peasant farming.
* **Mechanical weeding (Machine tillage):** Using tractor-drawn implements like rotary mowers, slashers, or inter-row cultivators to cut or bury weeds on large commercial farms.

### 2. Cultural Control

Cultural control involves modifying normal farming practices to make the farm environment unfavourable for weeds while favouring the growth of the crop.
* **Mulching:** Covering the soil surface around the crop with dry organic matter (like dry grass, wood shavings, or crop residues) or large black plastic sheets. **How it works:** The mulch creates a physical barrier that smothers weed seeds and blocks the sunlight they require to germinate and photosynthesise. It also conserves soil moisture and regulates temperature.
* **Cover cropping:** Planting fast-growing, dense, and creeping crops—usually leguminous plants like *Centrosema pubescens*, *Calopogonium mucunoides*, or *Mucuna pruriens*—to cover the soil rapidly. **How it works:** They aggressively compete for space and block sunlight from reaching weed seeds underneath, effectively choking the weeds out. As a bonus, being legumes, they add valuable nitrogen to the soil.
* **Tillage:** Ploughing and harrowing the soil deeply before planting. **How it works:** Tillage uproots existing weeds, exposes their roots to the intense drying heat of the sun (desiccation), and buries topsoil weed seeds deep into the ground where there is no light for them to germinate. 
* **Crop rotation and appropriate spacing (Density):** Rotating different crops prevents the build-up of weeds associated with one specific crop. Using the correct, close planting spacing allows the crop canopy to close quickly, casting shade and stopping weeds from growing beneath them.

### 3. Trap Cropping

**Trap cropping** is a highly specialised cultural method used specifically to control parasitic weeds, especially **Witchweed (*Striga*)** in savanna regions.
* **How it works:** The farmer plants a "decoy" or "trap" crop (like a specific variety of cotton, groundnut, or soybean). The roots of this decoy crop release chemical stimulants into the soil that trick the dormant *Striga* seeds into germinating. 
* However, the trap crop is not a suitable host for the parasite to attach to. As a result, the *Striga* seedlings starve and die shortly after germinating. 
* Alternatively, the farmer ploughs the entire field (destroying the trap crop and the young weeds) before the *Striga* can flower and produce new seeds. 
* This effectively clears the "seed bank" in the soil before planting the real, susceptible target crop (like maize or sorghum) the following season.

### 4. Chemical Control (Herbicides)

**Herbicides** are toxic agrochemicals specifically formulated to kill or inhibit the growth of weeds. They are highly efficient for large-scale farming and are classified in three main ways:

**A. Classification by time of application:**
* **Pre-plant herbicides:** Applied to the farm to clear all vegetation before any crop is planted.
* **Pre-emergence herbicides:** Applied to the soil *after* planting the crop seeds, but *before* the crop or the weed seeds germinate (emerge from the soil). They kill weed seeds exactly as they begin to sprout. Example: **Atrazine** (commonly used for maize farms).
* **Post-emergence herbicides:** Applied *after* both the crop and the weeds have germinated and are visibly growing above ground. Example: **Propanil** (used in rice fields).

**B. Classification by mode of action:**
* **Contact herbicides:** Kill only the green parts of the plant they directly touch. They do not travel down to the roots, meaning strong perennial weeds (like spear grass) with deep rhizomes will likely sprout again. Example: **Paraquat**.
* **Systemic (Translocated) herbicides:** Absorbed by the leaves or roots and transported (translocated) through the plant's vascular system to kill the entire plant, including the hidden underground rhizomes and tubers. This is the only chemical way to permanently destroy tough perennial weeds. Example: **Glyphosate**, **2,4-D**.

**C. Classification by target (selectivity):**
* **Selective herbicides:** Formulated to kill specific types of weeds without harming the main crop. For example, they might kill only broadleaf weeds while leaving grassy crops (like maize or rice) unharmed. Example: **2,4-D**, **Atrazine**.
* **Non-selective herbicides:** Kill any green plant they touch, whether it is a weed or a crop. They must be used with extreme care, usually for land clearing before crops are planted or carefully sprayed between rows. Example: **Glyphosate**, **Paraquat**.

**Safety Precautions:** Agrochemicals are poisonous. Farmers must wear Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) such as coveralls, rubber gloves, boots, and face masks. Spraying must be done on calm, windless days to prevent the wind from blowing the toxic chemicals onto the farmer or neighbouring crops (a dangerous effect known as spray drift).

## How to apply various weed control methods

**Objective: apply various weed control methods.**

To successfully manage weeds on a farm, a farmer must apply the correct method at the correct stage of crop production. Relying on just one method is rarely effective long-term. Farmers use a combination of physical, cultural, and chemical methods, a practice known as **Integrated Weed Management (IWM)**.

Here is how a farmer practically applies these methods across the farming season:

1. **Pre-planting application (Land clearing phase):** 
   Before planting any seeds, the farmer applies **tillage** (ploughing and harrowing) to uproot and bury existing weeds. If the weed infestation is very heavy, or if the farmer practices zero-tillage, a **non-selective, systemic herbicide** (like glyphosate) is applied to completely clear the field and kill all perennial rhizomes.
2. **At planting application (Sowing phase):** 
   Immediately after planting the seeds, but before they sprout, the farmer sprays a **pre-emergence herbicide** to stop weed seeds in the soil from germinating alongside the crop. The farmer also ensures the correct planting spacing (density) so the crops can form a canopy quickly and shade out the soil.
3. **Post-planting application (Crop growth phase):** 
   Once the crops and weeds have started growing, the farmer can use **hand pulling or hoeing** to physically remove weeds growing between the rows. If the farm is large, **selective post-emergence herbicides** can be sprayed to kill weeds without harming the crop. The farmer might also apply **mulch** around the base of the growing crops to suppress further weed germination and conserve moisture.
4. **Off-season / Long-term application:** 
   Between farming seasons, when the land is left fallow, the farmer might plant a dense **cover crop** (like *Mucuna*) to prevent weeds from taking over the land. If parasitic weeds like *Striga* were an issue in previous years, the farmer will apply the **trap cropping** method before planting the next cycle of cereal crops.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Scientific names are heavily tested:** Memorise that Spear grass is *Imperata cylindrica*, Siam weed is *Chromolaena odorata*, and Tridax is *Tridax procumbens*. 
* **Sedge vs. Grass:** Students often confuse them. Remember the phrase "Sedges have edges"—sedges have solid, triangular stems, while grasses have round, hollow stems.
* **Pre-emergence vs. Post-emergence:** "Pre" means before, "Post" means after. Do not confuse when they are applied. Pre-emergence is applied *before* the weeds or crops shoot out of the soil (emerge), not before planting.
* **Dispersal confusion:** If an exam question asks about a weed with hooks, spines, or sticky hairs (like *Boerhavia* or *Desmodium*), the dispersal agent is an animal or human, not wind.
* **Trap cropping misconception:** Trap cropping for weeds (like *Striga*) is about stimulating weed seed germination to deplete the soil seed bank. Do not confuse this with trap cropping for insect pests, which is meant to attract bugs away from the main crop.
* **Vegetative propagation:** If a question states that a farmer complains that hoeing a weed only makes it multiply, the weed reproduces vegetatively through underground rhizomes or tubers (e.g., spear grass or nut grass). Contact herbicides or shallow hoeing will not kill them; they require systemic herbicides or deep tillage.

## Quick summary

1. A weed is any plant growing out of place that interferes with agricultural activities and crop yields.
2. Weeds have aggressive adaptations such as massive seed production, long seed dormancy, deep root systems, mimicry, and the ability to reproduce vegetatively using rhizomes or tubers.
3. Morphologically, weeds are divided into narrow-leaf grasses (round hollow stems), sedges (triangular solid stems), and broadleaves (net-veined leaves and taproots).
4. Weeds compete with crops for nutrients, light, and water, reduce crop quality, harbour pests (acting as alternate hosts), and increase the overall cost of production.
5. Weeds disperse their seeds using wind (light parachute seeds), water (buoyant seeds), animals (seeds with hooks/sticky hairs), or explosive pod mechanisms.
6. Cultural weed control includes mulching, cover cropping, and tillage, which smother, shade out, or bury weeds.
7. Trap cropping is a specific cultural method used to force parasitic weed seeds (like *Striga*) to germinate so they can be starved and destroyed before the main crop is planted.
8. Chemical control involves herbicides, which can be selective or non-selective, systemic or contact, and applied either pre-emergence or post-emergence.

## Practice questions

**1. A plant is strictly defined as a weed in agriculture because it**
- A. has poisonous leaves and stems.
- B. produces large quantities of seeds.
- C. is growing where it is not wanted.
- D. cannot be used as animal feed.

**Answer: C.** The standard definition of a weed in agriculture is a plant growing where it is not wanted or interfering with farming objectives, regardless of its species or potential uses elsewhere.

**2. Which of the following is the scientific name for spear grass?**
- A. *Tridax procumbens*
- B. *Panicum maximum*
- C. *Imperata cylindrica*
- D. *Cynodon dactylon*

**Answer: C.** *Imperata cylindrica* is the scientific name for spear grass. *Panicum maximum* is Guinea grass, *Cynodon dactylon* is Bahama grass, and *Tridax procumbens* is Tridax.

**3. The most effective method of controlling a parasitic weed like *Striga* in a maize farm is**
- A. application of post-emergence contact herbicides.
- B. planting a trap crop.
- C. manual weeding at harvest.
- D. continuous irrigation.

**Answer: B.** Trap cropping stimulates *Striga* seeds to germinate using a decoy plant. They then die off without a suitable host before the susceptible maize crop is planted. Manual weeding at harvest is too late, as the weed would have already damaged the maize.

**4. Weeds with hair-like structures (pappus) on their seeds are usually dispersed by**
- A. water.
- B. explosive mechanism.
- C. animals.
- D. wind.

**Answer: D.** The pappus acts like a tiny parachute, making the seeds very light and easily carried by wind currents over long distances. *Tridax* is a common example of a weed dispersed this way.

**5. A major reason why *Imperata cylindrica* is very difficult to eradicate using just a cutlass or hoe is because it**
- A. produces poisonous saps that hurt the farmer.
- B. grows taller than most trees.
- C. reproduces vegetatively through deep underground rhizomes.
- D. has seeds that explode violently when touched.

**Answer: C.** Spear grass (*Imperata cylindrica*) has deep underground horizontal stems called rhizomes. Slashing or hoeing the top simply leaves the rhizome alive in the soil, which will quickly sprout new shoots.

**6. Which of the following describes the primary mechanism of mulching in weed control?**
- A. It poisons the weed roots chemically.
- B. It introduces insects that eat the weed seeds.
- C. It blocks sunlight, thereby preventing weed seeds from germinating and photosynthesizing.
- D. It stimulates the weed seeds to germinate rapidly so they die of exhaustion.

**Answer: C.** Mulching involves covering the soil with materials (like dry grass or plastic sheets). This physical barrier smothers weeds by cutting off the sunlight required for their germination and growth.

**7. A farmer applies an herbicide immediately after planting maize seeds but before the maize shoots appear above the soil. This type of herbicide is called**
- A. a pre-emergence herbicide.
- B. a post-emergence herbicide.
- C. a selective insecticide.
- D. a contact herbicide.

**Answer: A.** Herbicides applied to the soil after planting but before the crop or weed seeds emerge (shoot out) above the ground are called pre-emergence herbicides. 

**8. Which of these pairs of plants are commonly used as cover crops to suppress weeds in West Africa?**
- A. *Striga hermonthica* and *Ageratum conyzoides*
- B. *Centrosema pubescens* and *Calopogonium mucunoides*
- C. *Imperata cylindrica* and *Panicum maximum*
- D. Maize and Sorghum

**Answer: B.** *Centrosema* and *Calopogonium* are fast-growing, creeping leguminous cover crops used to aggressively smother weeds. The plants in options A and C are weeds themselves.

**9. Weeds with solid, triangular stems and fibrous roots are morphologically classified as**
- A. grasses.
- B. broadleaves.
- C. sedges.
- D. dicotyledons.

**Answer: C.** Sedges look similar to grasses but are distinguished by their solid, triangular stems (remember the rule: "sedges have edges"). Grasses generally have round, hollow stems with swollen nodes.

**10. One of the negative economic effects of weeds is that they serve as alternate hosts. This means that weeds**
- A. can be processed into highly nutritious organic manure.
- B. attract beneficial pollinating insects to the farm.
- C. harbour pests and diseases that can later transfer to the main crops.
- D. can take over the role of the main crop in providing food during drought.

**Answer: C.** Serving as an "alternate host" means the weed provides a home or food source for pests and pathogens (like insects, nematodes, and viruses) when the crop is absent, and these pests then migrate to attack the crop when it is planted.
