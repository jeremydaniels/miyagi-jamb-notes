# JAMB Agricultural Science Notes: Crop Diseases

It covers the following content items:

* **b.** A simple account of diseases caused by fungi, bacteria, nematodes and viruses; symptoms, the nature of the damage, methods of transmission and common methods of control.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

2. (i) relate various disease-causing organisms to the damage caused, symptoms and their mode of spread.
3. (ii) apply appropriate control methods.
4. relate each control method to its side effect.

**Note on the syllabus wording:** In content item "c", the syllabus mentions the "distribution of ecosystem". This is a typographical error in the official document; it means the **disruption** of the ecosystem (upsetting the natural balance of living things). We will cover it using both terms so you are fully prepared.

## Identification of disease–causing organisms both in store and in the field

A **crop disease** is any harmful deviation from the normal health, growth, or functioning of a plant, caused by a continuous irritation from a disease-causing organism (a **pathogen**) or environmental factor. 

In agriculture, pathogens are primarily grouped into four categories: **fungi**, **bacteria**, **viruses**, and **nematodes**. These organisms can attack crops while they are actively growing on the farm (field diseases) or after they have been harvested and packed away (store diseases). 

Identifying the organism responsible for a disease is the first step to saving the crop. You can identify the organism by looking at the **symptoms** (the visible reactions of the plant, such as wilting or colour changes) and the **signs** (the visible physical presence of the pathogen itself, such as fuzzy mould or bacterial ooze).

### Distinguish between common store and field disease–causing organisms

While some organisms can survive in both environments, most are specialized to attack crops either in the field or in the store. 

**Field disease-causing organisms** attack living, growing plants. The environment in the field usually has fluctuating temperatures, rain, wind, and sunlight. Field pathogens include fungi (causing rusts and blights), bacteria (causing wilts), viruses (causing mosaics), and soil-dwelling nematodes (causing root knots). 

**Store disease-causing organisms** attack harvested produce (such as dried grains, yam tubers, and beans) in barns, silos, and cribs. The storage environment is usually enclosed, with lower moisture but poor ventilation. Viruses and nematodes generally **do not** cause new infections in stored, dry grains because they require living host cells or soil to thrive. Instead, the most common storage pathogens are **fungi (moulds)** and some **bacteria** (causing soft rots in fleshy tubers like yams and cassava). 

| Feature | Field Disease-Causing Organisms | Store Disease-Causing Organisms |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stage of crop attacked** | Growing, living plants (roots, stems, leaves, flowers, developing fruits). | Harvested produce (grains, tubers, seeds, harvested fruits). |
| **Types of pathogens** | Fungi, bacteria, viruses, and nematodes. | Mostly fungi (moulds) and bacteria (soft rot organisms). |
| **Environmental requirements** | Need natural weather elements (rain splash for dispersal, soil moisture, sunlight for vectors). | Thrive in poor ventilation, warm temperatures, and slight moisture build-up in silos/barns. |
| **Mode of entry** | Enter through natural openings (stomata), roots, or wounds made by field pests and vectors. | Enter through bruises, cuts, or mechanical damage caused during harvesting and transportation. |
| **Common examples** | *Puccinia* (Rust), *Phytophthora* (Black pod), Cassava mosaic virus, Root-knot nematodes. | *Aspergillus flavus* (produces aflatoxins in groundnut), *Rhizopus* (soft rot of yam). |
| **Nature of damage** | Stunting, yellowing, leaf drop, reduced crop yield, failure to produce fruit. | Rotting, discolouration, bad odours, loss of viability (seeds won't germinate), production of dangerous toxins. |

## Account of diseases caused by fungi, bacteria, nematodes and viruses

To manage crop diseases effectively, we must understand the nature of the four main types of pathogens. 

1. **Fungi:** These are microscopic and macroscopic organisms (placed in their own kingdom, Fungi; older textbooks call them non-green plants). Because they lack chlorophyll, they cannot make their own food; they feed as parasites on living crops or as saprophytes on dead matter. They are made of thread-like structures called **hyphae** (collectively called a mycelium) and reproduce by producing millions of tiny **spores**. Fungi cause over 70% of all crop diseases.
2. **Bacteria:** These are microscopic, single-celled organisms. They reproduce very rapidly by simply dividing in two (binary fission). They often enter plants through natural openings or wounds and multiply inside the plant's water-conducting vessels (xylem), blocking water flow.
3. **Viruses:** These are ultramicroscopic infectious agents—much smaller than bacteria. They are not true cells; they consist merely of genetic material (DNA or RNA) wrapped in a protein coat. Viruses cannot survive or reproduce outside a living host cell. They must be injected into the plant by a **vector** (usually a piercing and sucking insect like a whitefly or aphid).
4. **Nematodes:** These are microscopic, unsegmented roundworms (eelworms) that live in the soil and water. Plant-parasitic nematodes possess a needle-like mouthpart called a **stylet**, which they use to pierce plant roots, inject enzymes, and suck out plant juices.

### Relate various disease-causing organisms to damage, symptoms and spread

Below is a detailed account of how each group of organisms affects crops, including specific Nigerian crop examples. 

#### 1. Fungal Diseases

**Common Symptoms:** 
- **Necrosis:** Death of plant tissue, leaving dark brown or black spots.
- **Chlorosis:** Yellowing of leaves.
- **Lesions:** Localized areas of diseased tissue on leaves or stems.
- **Visible growths:** Powdery mildew (white powder on leaves), rusts (orange/brown powdery spots), or fuzzy moulds.

**Nature of Damage:** 
Fungi destroy leaf area, reducing photosynthesis and ultimately yield. They rot roots and fruits, making them unsellable.

**Methods of Transmission (Mode of Spread):** 
- Wind (blows lightweight spores over long distances).
- Rain splash (water bounces spores from soil onto lower leaves).
- Infected farm tools (cutlasses and hoes).
- Infected seeds or planting materials.

**Major Examples:**
*   **Black Pod Disease of Cocoa:** Caused by the fungus *Phytophthora palmivora*. It is a massive problem in southwestern Nigeria. 
    *   *Symptoms:* Small brown spots on cocoa pods that quickly spread, turning the entire pod black and rotten. The beans inside are destroyed. 
    *   *Spread:* Rain splash and wind blow spores from the soil to the pods. High humidity makes it worse.
*   **Maize Rust:** Caused by *Puccinia polysora*. 
    *   *Symptoms:* Raised, reddish-brown powdery spots (pustules) on both sides of the maize leaves. 
    *   *Damage:* Severe leaf damage leading to stunted growth and poor grain filling.
*   **Rice Blast:** Caused by *Pyricularia oryzae*. 
    *   *Symptoms:* Spindle-shaped (diamond-shaped) spots on leaves with grey centres and brown borders. It causes the neck of the rice panicle to rot and break.

#### 2. Bacterial Diseases

**Common Symptoms:**
- **Wilting:** The plant droops and collapses entirely, even when the soil has plenty of water (because bacteria block the xylem).
- **Water-soaked spots:** Lesions on leaves that look wet or greasy.
- **Bacterial Ooze:** Sticky, foul-smelling liquid (exudate) weeping from stems or tubers.
- **Soft Rots:** Tubers turn into a mushy, foul-smelling liquid.

**Nature of Damage:**
Rapid death of the entire plant (wilt) or total destruction of harvested tubers (rot), leading to 100% loss in severe cases.

**Methods of Transmission (Mode of Spread):**
- Contaminated irrigation water or rain runoff.
- Infected vegetative planting materials (e.g., using infected cassava stems for the next season).
- Mechanical transmission via farm tools or insect bites.

**Major Examples:**
*   **Cassava Bacterial Blight (CBB):** Caused by *Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. manihotis*.
    *   *Symptoms:* Angular, water-soaked spots on leaves, wilting, leaf fall, and die-back of the stem. In severe cases, a sticky yellowish ooze appears on stems.
*   **Bacterial Wilt of Tomato:** Caused by *Ralstonia solanacearum*.
    *   *Symptoms:* Sudden wilting of the whole tomato plant while the leaves are still green. If you cut the stem and put it in a glass of clear water, a milky white thread of bacterial ooze will stream out.
*   **Soft Rot of Yam:** Often caused by *Erwinia* species (now *Pectobacterium*) in storage. 
    *   *Symptoms:* The yam tuber turns soft, dark, and emits a highly offensive odour. 

#### 3. Viral Diseases

**Common Symptoms:**
- **Mosaic patterns:** Irregular patches of light green, dark green, and yellow on leaves.
- **Stunting:** The plant is drastically shorter than normal.
- **Leaf distortion:** Leaves become curled, wrinkled, or malformed.
- **Vein clearing:** The veins of the leaf become transparent or yellow.

**Nature of Damage:**
Viruses take over the plant's cells, stunting growth so severely that the plant produces little to no yield.

**Methods of Transmission (Mode of Spread):**
Because viruses cannot survive freely in the environment, they are exclusively transmitted by:
- **Vectors:** Insects with piercing and sucking mouthparts (e.g., whiteflies, aphids, leafhoppers, mealybugs) feed on an infected plant, acquire the virus, and inject it into a healthy plant.
- **Infected planting materials:** Using cuttings, suckers, or tubers from a virus-infected plant guarantees the new plant will have the disease.

**Major Examples:**
*   **Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD):** A devastating disease across West Africa.
    *   *Symptoms:* Leaves show a pale yellow and green mottled (mosaic) pattern and are often twisted and much smaller than normal.
    *   *Spread:* By the **whitefly** (*Bemisia tabaci*) and through infected cassava stem cuttings.
*   **Groundnut Rosette Disease:** 
    *   *Symptoms:* Extreme stunting of the groundnut plant; leaves turn yellow and bunch together in a "rosette" shape.
    *   *Spread:* Transmitted by the **aphid** (*Aphis craccivora*).
*   **Cocoa Swollen Shoot Virus (CSSV):**
    *   *Symptoms:* Swelling of the stems and roots, leaf mottling, and die-back.
    *   *Spread:* Transmitted by the **mealybug**.

#### 4. Nematode Diseases

**Common Symptoms:**
- **Root galls (knots):** Swellings or lumps on the roots.
- **Nutrient deficiency symptoms:** Because the roots are damaged, the above-ground plant shows yellowing and stunting, looking as though the soil lacks fertilizer.
- **Wilting:** Occurs during the hottest part of the day due to poor water uptake.

**Nature of Damage:**
Nematodes destroy the root system. This starves the plant of water and minerals, severely reducing yields, especially in root and tuber crops where the economic part is in the soil.

**Methods of Transmission (Mode of Spread):**
- Movement of infested soil on farm machinery or boots.
- Planting infested "seed" tubers (e.g., seed yams with microscopic nematodes inside).
- Flowing surface water washing soil down a slope.

**Major Examples:**
*   **Root-knot Nematode:** Caused by *Meloidogyne* species affecting tomatoes, cowpeas, and yams.
    *   *Symptoms:* When the plant is uprooted, the roots are covered in round galls (knots). In yams, it causes "crazy roots" (excessive hairy roots) and bumpy, warty tubers that lose their market value.

### Apply appropriate control methods

To manage and control crop diseases, farmers use various strategies. These are grouped into cultural, chemical, biological, and physical controls. 

**Note on Viral Control:** You **cannot** cure a plant of a virus using chemicals (there are no agricultural "virucides"). To control viral diseases, you must control the insect vector or use resistant varieties.

**1. Cultural Control Methods (Farm Practices):**
*   **Crop Rotation:** Planting different crops on the same piece of land in successive seasons. By planting a crop that the pathogen cannot eat (a non-host), you break the disease cycle. (Very effective for nematodes and soil-borne bacteria).
*   **Rogueing:** This is the act of identifying, completely uprooting, and burning infected plants to prevent the disease from spreading to healthy neighbours. (Essential for viral diseases).
*   **Use of Disease-Resistant Varieties:** Planting crop breeds scientifically developed to withstand diseases. (e.g., planting CMD-resistant cassava varieties developed by IITA).
*   **Farm Hygiene (Sanitation):** Cleaning and disinfecting farm tools (cutlasses) before moving from one plant to another to stop sap-transmitted diseases.
*   **Early Planting:** Planting early in the season so the crop matures before pathogen populations (or their insect vectors) peak.
*   **Proper Spacing:** Prevents overcrowding, allowing sunlight and air to circulate, which dries leaves and reduces fungal infections.

**2. Chemical Control Methods:**
Using agrochemicals to kill or inhibit pathogens.
*   **Fungicides:** Chemicals that kill fungi. For example, **Bordeaux mixture** (a mixture of copper sulphate and slaked lime) is widely sprayed on cocoa to prevent Black Pod disease.
*   **Nematicides:** Chemicals applied to the soil (e.g., Carbofuran/Furadan) to kill nematodes.
*   **Bactericides:** Chemicals (often copper-based) used against bacteria. (Note: These are rare and expensive; cultural control is usually preferred for bacteria).
*   **Insecticides:** Used to kill the *vectors* (like aphids and whiteflies) that spread viral diseases, thereby indirectly controlling the virus.

**3. Biological Control Methods:**
Using living organisms (natural enemies) to control pathogens. Though more common for pests, some beneficial soil fungi (like *Trichoderma*) are introduced to attack harmful disease-causing fungi.

**4. Physical Control Methods:**
*   **Heat Treatment:** Treating seeds or vegetative cuttings with hot water to kill internal bacteria, fungi, or nematodes before planting.
*   **Soil Solarization:** Covering the soil with clear plastic sheets during the hot season. The trapped heat raises soil temperatures high enough to kill nematodes and fungal spores.

## Side effects of application of preventive and control methods

While cultural methods are generally safe, **chemical control methods** (fungicides, nematicides, insecticides) come with severe side effects if misused. The syllabus expects you to relate each control method to its specific side effect.

### Relate each control method to its side effect

| Control Method Applied | Resulting Side Effect | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Heavy use of Fungicides (e.g., Copper sprays on Cocoa)** | **Soil and Water Pollution** | Copper is a heavy metal. If sprayed repeatedly for years, it accumulates in the soil, becoming toxic to the crop itself. Rain washes the chemicals into nearby rivers, polluting drinking water and killing fish. |
| **Application of toxic Nematicides or Insecticides** | **Poisoning of farmers and consumers** | Farmers who spray chemicals without Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) inhale toxic fumes or absorb poisons through their skin. If crops are harvested and eaten before the chemical breaks down (ignoring the withdrawal period), consumers get food poisoning. |
| **Spraying Broad-Spectrum Insecticides (to control viral vectors)** | **Disruption of the Ecosystem** (Syllabus typo: "Distribution of ecosystem") | Broad-spectrum means the chemical kills *everything*, not just the bad pests. This kills beneficial pollinators (like bees, reducing crop yield) and natural predators (like ladybirds). Without predators, a minor pest might suddenly multiply and become a severe problem (pest resurgence). |
| **Continuous application of the same chemical** | **Pathogen Resistance** | Pathogens and vectors evolve rapidly. Using the same fungicide repeatedly will result in the fungi surviving and becoming resistant, making the chemical useless in the future. |
| **Burning infected crop residues (Rogueing and burning)** | **Air Pollution and loss of soil organic matter** | While burning destroys the disease spores, it releases smoke and carbon (IV) oxide into the air. It also means those plant materials cannot be used to make compost, reducing soil organic matter. |

**Ecosystem Disruption ("Distribution") in detail**
An agricultural ecosystem has a natural balance. Pests eat crops, but predators (like spiders and birds) eat the pests. Soil fungi decompose matter to feed plants. When a farmer dumps heavy chemicals to control a disease or vector, this balance is broken:
1. **Biomagnification:** Harmful chemicals enter the food chain. A caterpillar eats a sprayed leaf, a bird eats many caterpillars, and a hawk eats the birds. The poison concentrates at each step, often killing the top predators (hawks, owls) that normally keep rodent populations down.
2. **Loss of Soil Health:** Heavy fungicides can kill beneficial mycorrhizal fungi (which help roots absorb phosphorus) and earthworms (which aerate the soil).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pathogen** | Any organism capable of causing a disease (e.g., fungi, bacteria, viruses, nematodes). |
| **Vector** | An organism, usually an insect, that carries and transmits a disease-causing pathogen from an infected plant to a healthy one. |
| **Necrosis** | The death of plant cells or tissues, often appearing as dark, rotting spots. |
| **Chlorosis** | The yellowing of green plant tissues due to a lack of chlorophyll, often a symptom of disease. |
| **Rogueing** | The physical removal and destruction (usually by burning) of diseased plants from a farm to prevent spread. |
| **Mosaic** | A viral symptom where leaves show an irregular pattern of light green, dark green, and yellow patches. |
| **Bordeaux Mixture** | A chemical fungicide made of copper sulphate and lime, commonly used to control fungal diseases like black pod. |
| **Galls** | Abnormal swellings or knots on plant roots caused by nematode infections. |
| **Disruption of ecosystem** | The upsetting of the natural balance of life on a farm, often caused by indiscriminate chemical use (listed as "distribution" in the syllabus). |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Viruses CANNOT be killed with chemicals:** A very common JAMB trap is asking how to chemically control a viral disease like Cassava Mosaic. Options might include "apply fungicides" or "apply virucides". The correct answer is always related to **controlling the vector** (e.g., spraying insecticides to kill whiteflies) or **uprooting the plant (rogueing)**.
* **Know your vectors:** 
    * Cassava mosaic disease $\rightarrow$ Whitefly.
    * Groundnut rosette $\rightarrow$ Aphid.
    * Cocoa swollen shoot $\rightarrow$ Mealybug.
* **Store vs. Field:** Remember that storage diseases are almost exclusively caused by Fungi (moulds) and bacteria. If a question asks which pathogen rarely initiates infection in dry storage, the answer is viruses or nematodes.
* **Nematode symptoms:** If the question mentions "knots on roots" or "galls", the answer is immediately Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne*).
* **"Distribution of ecosystem":** If you see this strange phrase in an exam option regarding the side effect of chemical control, recognize it as the syllabus's typo for ecosystem disruption, and select it if it fits the context of harming beneficial organisms.
* **Bacterial Blight vs. Fungal Blight:** Cassava bacterial blight causes wilting and gummy ooze. Fungal blights usually cause dry necrotic patches and powdery spores.

## Quick summary

1. Crop diseases are caused by four main groups of pathogens: fungi (spores/moulds), bacteria (ooze/wilts), viruses (mosaics/stunting), and nematodes (root galls).
2. Field pathogens attack growing crops, while store pathogens (mostly fungi and some bacteria) attack harvested produce in barns and silos.
3. Fungal diseases (e.g., Cocoa black pod, Maize rust) spread via wind and rain and are controlled using spacing, crop rotation, and fungicides.
4. Bacterial diseases (e.g., Cassava bacterial blight, Tomato wilt) spread via water and tools, blocking plant xylem and causing sudden wilting or soft rots.
5. Viral diseases (e.g., Cassava mosaic, Groundnut rosette) are spread entirely by insect vectors (whiteflies, aphids) and infected cuttings; they are controlled by killing vectors and rogueing.
6. Nematodes are microscopic worms that attack roots, causing galls (knots) that starve the plant of nutrients; controlled by crop rotation and nematicides.
7. Chemical control methods have serious side effects, including soil and water pollution, poisoning of humans and livestock, and the disruption of the natural ecosystem (killing beneficial insects and natural predators).

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following groups of disease-causing organisms is most commonly responsible for the spoilage of harvested, dry grains in a storage silo?**
- A. Viruses
- B. Fungi
- C. Nematodes
- D. Insect vectors

**Answer: B.** Fungi (specifically moulds like *Aspergillus*) thrive in storage conditions if there is slight moisture, causing rots and spoilage. Viruses and nematodes require living tissue or soil to thrive.

**2. A farmer notices that her growing tomato plants are wilting suddenly, and a milky sticky liquid oozes from the stem when cut. The likely causative organism is a**
- A. fungus
- B. virus
- C. bacterium
- D. nematode

**Answer: C.** Sudden wilting combined with a sticky exudate (bacterial ooze) is the classic symptom of a bacterial infection (Bacterial wilt) blocking the plant's xylem.

**3. Cassava mosaic disease is highly destructive. What is the most effective way a farmer can stop its spread on the farm?**
- A. Spraying the leaves with copper-based fungicides
- B. Applying nematicides to the soil
- C. Uprooting and burning infected plants and controlling whiteflies
- D. Applying heavy doses of nitrogen fertilizer

**Answer: C.** Cassava mosaic is a viral disease. Viruses cannot be killed with fungicides or nematicides. The disease is managed by rogueing (uprooting/burning) and controlling the whitefly vector.

**4. The tiny roundworms in the soil that cause abnormal swellings or knots on the roots of crops are called**
- A. earthworms
- B. nematodes
- C. caterpillars
- D. millipedes

**Answer: B.** Root-knot nematodes are microscopic unsegmented roundworms that pierce roots, causing characteristic galls (knots) and nutrient deficiency.

**5. One major side effect of the continuous application of broad-spectrum chemicals to control crop diseases is the**
- A. increase in the organic matter content of the soil
- B. disruption of the ecosystem by killing beneficial organisms
- C. improvement of the soil's water-holding capacity
- D. permanent eradication of all farm weeds

**Answer: B.** Broad-spectrum chemicals kill both harmful pests and beneficial organisms (like bees and natural predators), disrupting the natural balance of the ecosystem.

**6. Which of the following sets correctly matches a crop disease with its method of transmission?**
- A. Groundnut rosette — Rain splash
- B. Cocoa black pod — Aphids
- C. Maize rust — Whiteflies
- D. Cassava mosaic — Whiteflies

**Answer: D.** Cassava mosaic virus is transmitted by the whitefly. Groundnut rosette is transmitted by aphids, and cocoa black pod/maize rust are fungal diseases spread by rain splash and wind, not vectors.

**7. Rogueing as a cultural method of disease control involves**
- A. rotating crops on a piece of land to break the disease cycle
- B. covering the soil with plastic to heat it up
- C. identifying, uprooting, and destroying infected plants
- D. planting disease-resistant crop varieties

**Answer: C.** Rogueing strictly means the physical removal and destruction (often burning) of diseased plants to prevent the pathogen from spreading to healthy crops.

**8. Black pod disease of cocoa is caused by a**
- A. fungus
- B. bacterium
- C. virus
- D. nematode

**Answer: A.** Black pod disease is caused by the fungus *Phytophthora palmivora*, which rots the pods and destroys the beans. 

**9. When agricultural chemicals are washed by rain into a nearby river, causing the death of fish, this side effect is best described as**
- A. biomagnification
- B. water pollution
- C. pest resurgence
- D. atmospheric poisoning

**Answer: B.** The runoff of toxic chemicals into water bodies contaminates the water, which is the definition of water pollution, leading to the death of aquatic life.

**10. A farmer pulls up a stunted, yellowing yam plant and finds the tubers are warty and covered in tiny lumps. Which control method should the farmer apply for the next planting season?**
- A. Spray Bordeaux mixture on the leaves
- B. Practice crop rotation and apply a nematicide to the soil
- C. Treat the seed yams with a bactericide
- D. Introduce ladybird beetles to eat the pests

**Answer: B.** The symptoms (warty lumps/galls on roots and tubers) indicate a nematode infection. Nematodes are soil-borne, so crop rotation with non-hosts and applying nematicides are the appropriate control methods.
