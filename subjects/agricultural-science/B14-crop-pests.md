## Crop Pests: Field and Storage Overview

**Content a: General account of pests of agricultural plants both in the field and in the store, their types, importance, principles and methods of prevention and control**

A **pest** is any organism (animal, plant, or microbe) that damages crops, livestock, or agricultural products, leading to economic losses for the farmer. In crop production, the most common pests are insects, birds, rodents, monkeys, nematodes, and snails. Pests reduce the yield and quality of farm produce, making pest management one of the most critical aspects of crop husbandry.

Pests can be broadly grouped based on *where* they attack the crop:
1.  **Field pests**: These attack crops while they are still growing in the field.
2.  **Storage pests**: These attack harvested crops in silos, barns, cribs, or warehouses.

---

### Identifying Various Field and Store Pests

**Objective i: identify the various field and store pests.**

To properly manage pests, a farmer must be able to identify them and know which stage of the crop they attack. 

#### 1. Field Pests
These pests are active out in the open farmland and attack various parts of the growing plant, from the roots to the fruits.
*   **Insects:** 
    *   **Stem borers:** Caterpillars of certain moths that bore into the stems of maize, rice, and sugarcane, causing the plant to wither and die (a condition called "dead heart").
    *   **Armyworms:** Caterpillars that march in large numbers, eating the leaves of cereals (like maize and guinea corn) down to the midrib.
    *   **Grasshoppers and Locusts:** Feed greedily on the foliage of almost all crops. Locusts can form massive swarms that destroy entire farms in hours.
    *   **Cotton stainers:** Suck sap from cotton bolls and introduce fungi that stain the white cotton lint.
    *   **Aphids:** Tiny insects that suck sap from plants (e.g., groundnut, cowpea) and often transmit dangerous plant viruses.
*   **Vertebrates (Animals with backbones):**
    *   **Birds:** Weaver birds and bush fowls peck at sown seeds, dig up germinating seedlings, and eat maturing grains (especially rice and millet).
    *   **Rodents:** Grasscutters (cane rats), bush rats, and squirrels eat the roots, tubers (like cassava and yam), and stems of growing plants.
    *   **Primates:** Monkeys and baboons are notorious for raiding maize and fruit plantations, often destroying more than they actually eat.
*   **Nematodes:** Microscopic, worm-like organisms in the soil. Root-knot nematodes attack the roots of tomatoes, yams, and cowpeas, causing swellings (galls) that block water and nutrient uptake.

#### 2. Storage Pests
These pests attack agricultural produce after harvest, during storage. They are a major cause of food insecurity in West Africa.
*   **Insects:**
    *   **Weevils:** The **maize weevil** (*Sitophilus zeamais*) and **rice weevil** (*Sitophilus oryzae*) have long snouts and bore into cereal grains, eating the starchy inside and leaving hollow shells.
    *   **Bean beetles (Cowpea weevils):** *Callosobruchus maculatus* attacks stored cowpeas and other legumes, riddling the seeds with holes.
    *   **Flour beetles:** Attack milled products like maize flour and cassava flour.
*   **Vertebrates:**
    *   **Rats and Mice:** The most destructive non-insect storage pests. They eat stored grains and tubers, and heavily contaminate the remaining produce with their urine, faeces, and hairs.

### Assessing the Economic Importance of Crop Pests

**Objective ii: assess their economic importance.**

Pests cause massive disruptions in agriculture. The economic importance (the effects of pests on farming and the economy) includes:

1.  **Reduction in Crop Yield (Quantity):** Pests eat leaves, destroying the plant's ability to photosynthesise, or they eat the fruits and seeds directly. This reduces the total volume of food harvested.
2.  **Reduction in Crop Quality:** Pests leave holes, stains, and webbing in produce. For example, yams bored by beetles or cotton stained by the cotton stainer command very low prices in the market.
3.  **Transmission of Diseases (Vectors):** Many pests, especially sucking insects, act as vectors. When an aphid sucks sap from a diseased groundnut plant and moves to a healthy one, it transmits the **Rosette virus**. Mealybugs transmit the **Cassava Swollen Shoot Virus**.
4.  **Increase in Cost of Production:** Farmers must spend large sums of money on pesticides, spraying equipment, and hired labour to control pests, which reduces their overall profit (net margin).
5.  **Loss of Income:** Reduced yield and poor quality lead directly to a loss of income for the farmer and can lead to national food scarcity.
6.  **Contamination of Produce:** Storage pests like rats and cockroaches leave droppings and urine on food, making it unfit for human consumption and leading to food waste.

### Relating Prevention and Control Methods to Different Pests

**Objective iii: relate various prevention and control methods to different pests.**

Pest control involves using specific strategies to keep pest populations below a level where they cause economic damage. Different pests require different control methods.

| Control Method | How it Works | Pests it Targets |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cultural Control** | Using good farming practices to make the environment unsuitable for pests. Includes crop rotation, early planting, regular weeding, burning crop residues, and proper tillage. | **Stem borers** (burning old maize stalks destroys their hiding places); **Nematodes** (crop rotation starves them); **Rodents** (weeding removes their hiding spots). |
| **Physical/Mechanical Control** | Physically removing the pest or placing barriers to stop them. | **Birds** (scarecrows, noisemakers, nets over nurseries); **Rodents** (fences, wire meshes, traps); **Giant grasshoppers/Beetles** (handpicking). |
| **Biological Control** | Using the pest's natural enemies (predators, parasites, or pathogens) to control it. | **Aphids** (ladybird beetles are introduced to eat them); **Cassava mealybug** (controlled by parasitic wasps introduced into the field). |
| **Chemical Control** | Using poisonous chemicals (pesticides) to kill the pests. | **Armyworms** (insecticide sprays); **Weevils in store** (fumigants in silos); **Rats** (rodenticide baits). |
| **Legislative Control** | Government laws preventing the movement of infested plants across borders (Quarantine). | Prevents the introduction of **foreign pests** (e.g., stopping the entry of alien fruit flies into Nigeria). |

## Insect Pests: Biting, Boring, and Sucking

**Content b: Life cycles of: biting insects e.g. grasshopper; boring insects e.g. weevils; sucking insects e.g. aphids and cotton strainer.**

Insects are the most numerous and destructive crop pests. They are classified according to their **mouthparts** and **feeding habits**.

1.  **Biting and Chewing Insects:** They have strong mandibles (jaws) to bite off, chew, and swallow plant parts (leaves, stems, roots). Examples: Grasshoppers, locusts, armyworms, termites, and beetles.
2.  **Piercing and Sucking Insects:** They have a needle-like mouthpart (proboscis or stylet) used to pierce plant tissues and suck out the sugary sap. Examples: Aphids, cotton stainers, mealybugs, and whiteflies.
3.  **Boring Insects:** Their larvae (and sometimes adults) bore into plant tissues, stems, or stored seeds to feed from the inside. Examples: Maize weevils, bean beetles, and stem borers.

To control insects, we must understand their **metamorphosis** (the changes they undergo as they develop from an egg to an adult). 
*   **Complete Metamorphosis (Holometabolous):** Four stages: Egg $\rightarrow$ Larva (caterpillar, grub, or maggot) $\rightarrow$ Pupa (resting stage) $\rightarrow$ Adult.
*   **Incomplete Metamorphosis (Hemimetabolous):** Three stages: Egg $\rightarrow$ Nymph (looks like a mini adult but lacks wings and reproductive organs) $\rightarrow$ Adult.

---

### Describing the Life Cycles of Various Insects

**Objective i: describe the life cycles of various insects.**

#### 1. Biting Insect: The Grasshopper
The grasshopper undergoes **incomplete metamorphosis** (Egg $\rightarrow$ Nymph $\rightarrow$ Adult).

1.  **Egg:** The adult female grasshopper digs a hole in moist, loose soil and lays her eggs in clusters (egg pods), covered in a frothy material that hardens to protect them.
2.  **Nymph:** After a few weeks (or months, depending on the dry season), the eggs hatch into nymphs. Nymphs look like small adult grasshoppers but do not have wings. They immediately begin to bite and chew on low-growing vegetation.
3.  **Adult:** The nymph feeds heavily and moults (sheds its exoskeleton) about 5 to 6 times. With each moult (called an instar), it grows larger and develops wing pads. After the final moult, it becomes a fully winged, sexually mature adult capable of flying, mating, and causing severe damage to crop foliage.

#### 2. Boring Insect: The Maize Weevil
The maize weevil (*Sitophilus zeamais*) undergoes **complete metamorphosis** (Egg $\rightarrow$ Larva $\rightarrow$ Pupa $\rightarrow$ Adult).

1.  **Egg:** The female weevil uses her long snout to bore a tiny hole into a maize grain (either in the field before harvest or in the store). She lays a single egg inside the hole and seals it with a waxy secretion.
2.  **Larva (Grub):** The egg hatches into a tiny, white, legless grub (larva). The larva remains completely inside the grain, boring through and feeding on the starchy endosperm. This stage causes the most damage.
3.  **Pupa:** Once fully grown, the larva pupates inside the hollowed-out grain. The pupa does not feed; it is undergoing internal transformation.
4.  **Adult:** The adult weevil emerges from the pupa, chews an exit hole through the seed coat, and crawls out. It will mate, and the females will begin laying eggs in fresh grains.

#### 3. Sucking Insects: Aphid and Cotton Stainer

**A. The Aphid**
Aphids have a unique, highly complex life cycle that can bypass the egg stage entirely to multiply rapidly.
1.  In the tropics, most aphids reproduce by **parthenogenesis** (females produce living young without mating with a male). 
2.  An adult female gives birth directly to live **nymphs** (viviparity).
3.  The nymphs begin sucking sap immediately and moult a few times to become adults in just about a week. 
4.  Because they do not lay eggs and mature so quickly, aphid populations can explode in a very short time, heavily infesting cowpea and groundnut fields.

**B. The Cotton Stainer (*Dysdercus* spp.)**
The cotton stainer undergoes **incomplete metamorphosis** (Egg $\rightarrow$ Nymph $\rightarrow$ Adult).
1.  **Egg:** The female lays spherical, yellow eggs in batches in moist soil, in cracks in the ground, or under crop debris near the cotton plant.
2.  **Nymph:** The eggs hatch into red nymphs. The nymphs crawl up the cotton plant and use their piercing mouthparts to suck sap from the developing cotton bolls and seeds.
3.  **Adult:** After several moults, they become winged adults. Both adults and nymphs feed on the bolls, introducing a fungus (*Nematospora*) that stains the white lint yellow or brown, making it commercially useless.

### Applying Knowledge of Life Cycles to Prevention and Control

**Objective ii: apply the knowledge of the life cycles of insect pests to their prevention and control.**

Knowing the life cycle of a pest reveals its **weakest link**—the stage at which it is most vulnerable to control measures.

1.  **Targeting the Egg Stage:** 
    *   *Grasshoppers and Cotton Stainers* lay their eggs in the soil. **Deep ploughing** (tillage) exposes these eggs to the hot sun, drying them out, or to predators like birds. 
    *   *Stem borers* lay eggs on crop residues. **Burning crop residues** after harvest destroys the eggs before the next planting season.
2.  **Targeting the Larval/Nymph Stage:** 
    *   Larvae (caterpillars) and nymphs are voracious eaters but usually have soft bodies. This makes them highly susceptible to **contact and stomach insecticides**. Spraying when the pest is in the early larval stage is much more effective than waiting until they are adults.
3.  **Targeting the Pupal Stage:** 
    *   Many insects pupate in the soil. Ploughing the field after harvest exposes the dormant pupae to harsh weather and predators.
4.  **Managing Boring Insects (Weevils):**
    *   Because the egg, larva, and pupa of the weevil are hidden safely *inside* the grain, ordinary contact insecticide sprays will not reach them. Therefore, to control weevils in a store, farmers must use **fumigants** (poisonous gases that penetrate the grain) or ensure the grain is dried to a very low moisture content (below 12%) so the larvae cannot survive inside.

## Understanding Common Pesticides and Side Effects

**Content c: Common pesticides and their side effects**

A **pesticide** is any chemical preparation used to kill, repel, or control pests. While they are highly effective, their misuse poses serious threats to human health and the environment.

### Differentiating Between Common Pesticides

**Objective i: differentiate between common pesticides.**

Pesticides are grouped based on the specific type of pest they are designed to kill:

| Type of Pesticide | Target Pest | Examples / Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Insecticides** | Insects (e.g., aphids, weevils, armyworms) | *Gammalin 20*, *DDT* (banned in many places), *Pyrethroids*. |
| **Rodenticides** | Rodents (e.g., rats, mice, grasscutters) | *Warfarin*, *Zinc phosphide*. Usually formulated as poisoned baits. |
| **Nematicides** | Nematodes (e.g., root-knot nematodes) | Applied to the soil. E.g., *Furadan*, *Nemagon*. |
| **Avicides** | Birds (e.g., weaver birds, Quelea birds) | Used as treated baits or chemical sprays in severe infestations. |
| **Molluscicides** | Molluscs (e.g., snails, slugs) | Snail baits used in vegetable farming. |
| **Fungicides** | Fungi (causing plant diseases) | *Bordeaux mixture*, *Copper sulphate*. (Note: Fungi are pathogens, but fungicides are part of the broader pesticide family). |
| **Herbicides** | Weeds | *Glyphosate*, *Atrazine*. (Used against plant pests/weeds). |

---

### Examining the Mode of Action of Pesticides on Pests

**Objective ii: examine their mode of action on pests.**

"Mode of action" means *how* the chemical enters the pest's body and kills it. Insecticides have four main modes of action:

1.  **Stomach Poisons:** 
    *   *How they work:* The chemical is sprayed onto the leaves of the plant. The insect must bite, chew, and swallow the treated plant tissue. The poison enters the insect's digestive tract and kills it.
    *   *Best for:* **Biting and chewing insects** like grasshoppers, armyworms, and leaf beetles.
    *   *Useless against:* Piercing and sucking insects, because they push their stylets deep into the plant to suck clean sap, bypassing the poison on the surface.
2.  **Contact Poisons:** 
    *   *How they work:* The chemical is sprayed directly onto the insect or surfaces where the insect walks. It penetrates the insect's exoskeleton (skin) or blocks its breathing pores (spiracles), causing death (often by nerve paralysis).
    *   *Best for:* Many insects, including soft-bodied **piercing and sucking insects** (like aphids) and crawling insects. 
3.  **Systemic Poisons:** 
    *   *How they work:* The chemical is applied to the soil or sprayed on the leaves, and the plant absorbs it. The poison travels through the plant's sap system. When an insect pierces the plant and sucks the sap, it ingests the poison and dies.
    *   *Best for:* **Piercing and sucking insects** (aphids, mealybugs, cotton stainers) and some stem borers.
4.  **Fumigants (Respiratory Poisons):** 
    *   *How they work:* These are chemicals applied in a gaseous form. The toxic gas is inhaled by the pest through its respiratory system, killing it. 
    *   *Best for:* **Storage pests** (weevils and beetles in airtight silos) and **soil pests** (nematodes). E.g., Phostoxin tablets.

### Side Effects of Pesticides

While chemical control is fast, it comes with severe side effects:
1.  **Environmental Pollution:** Chemicals can wash off farms into rivers and lakes, poisoning aquatic life (fish). They can also accumulate in the soil.
2.  **Killing Non-Target Organisms:** Broad-spectrum insecticides kill harmful pests but also kill beneficial insects like **honeybees** (which pollinate crops) and **ladybird beetles** (which eat aphids).
3.  **Toxicity to Humans (Toxic Residues):** If crops are harvested and eaten too soon after spraying, toxic residues can cause acute poisoning or long-term health issues (like cancer) in humans and livestock.
4.  **Pest Resistance:** If the same pesticide is used repeatedly, a few pests may survive due to genetic variation. They breed and create a new generation of pests that are immune (resistant) to that chemical.
5.  **Disruption of Ecosystems:** Removing one insect population completely can cause a secondary pest to explode in numbers because its natural competition is gone.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pest** | Any organism that damages agricultural produce, leading to economic losses. |
| **Vector** | An organism (usually an insect) that transmits a disease from a sick plant to a healthy one. |
| **Metamorphosis** | The physical changes in form that an insect undergoes as it develops from egg to adult. |
| **Instar** | A developmental stage of a nymph or larva between moults. |
| **Systemic poison** | A pesticide absorbed into the plant's sap, killing insects that suck the sap. |
| **Fumigant** | A poisonous gas used to kill pests in enclosed spaces (like silos) or in the soil. |
| **Parthenogenesis** | A form of reproduction where an unfertilised egg develops into a new individual (common in aphids). |
| **Nymph** | The immature stage of an insect that undergoes incomplete metamorphosis; it resembles the adult but lacks wings. |
| **Biological control** | Using a pest's natural predators or parasites to control its population. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Confusing insect feeding habits with life cycles:** Know which insects are biting (grasshopper), boring (weevil), and sucking (aphid, cotton stainer). This is a very common UTME question.
*   **Cotton Stainer spelling:** Even though the syllabus says "strainer", know that it is the cotton *stainer*. It introduces a fungus that stains cotton bolls.
*   **Best pesticide for sucking insects:** Never choose "stomach poison" for aphids or cotton stainers. Because they suck sap from *inside* the plant, they bypass surface poisons. The correct answer is always **systemic poison** (or contact poison if sprayed directly on them).
*   **Best pesticide for storage pests/weevils:** The answer is **fumigation**. Since weevils live *inside* the grain, contact sprays won't reach them. Gases penetrate the grain.
*   **Metamorphosis types:** Grasshoppers and cotton stainers = Incomplete. Weevils and stem borers = Complete.
*   **Aphid uniqueness:** They reproduce rapidly because they often skip the egg-laying stage in the tropics (parthenogenesis/viviparity).
*   **Vectors:** Aphids are famous vectors for groundnut rosette virus.

## Quick summary

1.  Pests cause economic losses by reducing crop yield, lowering produce quality, increasing production costs, and transmitting diseases.
2.  Field pests attack growing crops (e.g., grasshoppers, stem borers, monkeys), while storage pests attack harvested produce (e.g., weevils, rats).
3.  Insects are classified by feeding habits: biting and chewing (grasshoppers), piercing and sucking (aphids, cotton stainers), and boring (weevils).
4.  Knowing an insect's life cycle allows farmers to target the pest at its most vulnerable stage (e.g., ploughing to expose eggs or pupae).
5.  Pesticides include insecticides, rodenticides, nematicides, and avicides, each targeting specific pest groups.
6.  Pesticides work through different modes of action: stomach poisons, contact poisons, systemic poisons, and fumigants.
7.  The side effects of pesticides include environmental pollution, destruction of beneficial insects (like bees), toxic residues on food, and the development of pest resistance.

## Practice questions

**1. Any organism that causes damage to crop plants and livestock, thereby reducing their economic value, is referred to as a**
- A. parasite
- B. pest
- C. vector
- D. pathogen

**Answer: B.** A pest is defined economically as an organism that damages agricultural produce and causes losses to the farmer. A vector transmits diseases, and a pathogen causes diseases.

**2. Which of the following is primarily a storage pest?**
- A. Armyworm
- B. Cotton stainer
- C. Maize weevil
- D. Stem borer

**Answer: C.** The maize weevil attacks harvested grains in storage (silos or cribs), while armyworms, cotton stainers, and stem borers are field pests.

**3. The most effective mode of chemical control against piercing and sucking insects like aphids is the use of**
- A. stomach poisons
- B. systemic poisons
- C. avicides
- D. rodenticides

**Answer: B.** Systemic poisons are absorbed into the plant's sap. When the aphid pierces the plant to suck the sap, it ingests the poison. Stomach poisons are ineffective because the aphid does not chew the surface tissue where the poison lies.

**4. Which of the following insect pests undergoes incomplete metamorphosis?**
- A. Maize weevil
- B. Bean beetle
- C. Grasshopper
- D. Stem borer moth

**Answer: C.** The grasshopper has three life stages (Egg $\rightarrow$ Nymph $\rightarrow$ Adult), which is incomplete metamorphosis. Weevils, beetles, and moths undergo complete metamorphosis (four stages).

**5. The insect that transmits the rosette disease of groundnut is the**
- A. aphid
- B. mealybug
- C. grasshopper
- D. whitefly

**Answer: A.** Aphids are the primary vectors of the groundnut rosette virus, which they transmit while sucking sap from the plant. 

**6. A farmer noticing holes in the leaves of his maize plants is most likely dealing with a**
- A. biting and chewing insect
- B. piercing and sucking insect
- C. nematode
- D. boring insect

**Answer: A.** Biting and chewing insects (like grasshoppers and armyworms) use their mandibles to chew and eat the plant tissues, leaving visible holes or stripping the leaves entirely.

**7. Ploughing a field shortly after harvest is an effective cultural control method because it**
- A. adds toxic chemicals to the soil
- B. exposes insect eggs and pupae to sunlight and predators
- C. introduces biological predators into the soil
- D. increases the moisture content for the next planting season

**Answer: B.** Many pests (like grasshoppers) lay eggs in the soil, and others pupate there. Ploughing turns the soil, exposing these vulnerable stages to harsh weather (drying out) and predators (like birds).

**8. Fumigation is highly recommended for controlling pests in silos because**
- A. it acts as a stomach poison for crawling insects
- B. it is absorbed by the plant roots
- C. the toxic gas penetrates the stored grains to kill hidden larvae
- D. it improves the nutritional value of the stored grain

**Answer: C.** Fumigants work as respiratory poisons in gaseous form. They are ideal for storage pests like weevils whose larvae are hidden deep inside the grains where contact sprays cannot reach.

**9. A major side effect of the continuous use of broad-spectrum chemical pesticides is**
- A. the improvement of soil structure
- B. the eradication of all pests permanently
- C. the destruction of non-target, beneficial organisms like bees
- D. an increase in the photosynthetic rate of crops

**Answer: C.** Broad-spectrum pesticides kill indiscriminately, meaning they destroy both harmful pests and beneficial organisms such as pollinating bees and natural predators of pests.

**10. The introduction of ladybird beetles into a farm to feed on aphids is an example of**
- A. cultural control
- B. chemical control
- C. mechanical control
- D. biological control

**Answer: D.** Biological control involves using a pest's natural enemies (predators, parasites, or pathogens)—in this case, ladybird beetles—to reduce the pest population.
