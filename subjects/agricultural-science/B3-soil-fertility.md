## Macro and micro-nutrients

For crops like maize, yam, and cassava to grow well and yield heavily, they need specific chemical elements from the environment. These essential elements are known as **plant nutrients**. A nutrient is essential if a plant cannot complete its life cycle without it. 

### Classifying plant nutrients

There are 16 generally accepted essential plant nutrients. They are classified into two main groups based entirely on the **quantity** the plant requires, not on their importance.

**1. Macronutrients (Major elements):** These are nutrients required by plants in large quantities. There are nine macronutrients, divided into:
- **Non-mineral (from air and water):** Carbon (C), Hydrogen (H), Oxygen (O). These make up the bulk of the plant's weight.
- **Primary mineral (from the soil):** Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K). Plants consume these very quickly, so they form the basis of most commercial fertilizers (NPK).
- **Secondary mineral (from the soil):** Calcium (Ca), Magnesium (Mg), Sulphur (S).

**2. Micronutrients (Trace elements):** These are nutrients required by plants in very small (trace) quantities. If applied in large amounts, they become toxic. The seven essential micronutrients are:
- Iron (Fe), Zinc (Zn), Copper (Cu), Manganese (Mn), Boron (B), Molybdenum (Mo), and Chlorine (Cl).

### Factors affecting nutrient availability

Even if abundant nutrients are present in the soil, plants might not be able to absorb them. The availability of nutrients is affected by several dynamic factors:

**Soil pH (Acidity and Alkalinity):** This is the most critical factor. Most nutrients are highly available when the soil pH is neutral or slightly acidic (around 6.0 to 7.0). In highly acidic soils, essential nutrients like phosphorus are locked up, while toxic elements like aluminium become too available.
**Leaching:** Heavy rainfall washes soluble nutrients (especially nitrogen and potassium) deep into the soil profile, completely out of the reach of plant roots.
**Soil Texture:** Sandy soils have large pores and hold very few nutrients. Clay and humus have high negative electrical charges, allowing them to attract and hold positively charged nutrient ions (like K⁺ and Ca²⁺).
**Organic Matter Content:** Humus releases a slow, steady supply of nutrients as it decays and greatly improves the soil's ability to retain existing nutrients.
**Crop Removal:** When crops are harvested, the nutrients stored in their tissues are permanently removed from the farm ecosystem.

**The Carbon, Water and Nitrogen Cycles**
Nutrients are constantly recycled between the physical environment and living organisms through biogeochemical cycles.

**1. The Carbon Cycle:**
- **Photosynthesis:** Green plants take in carbon (IV) oxide (CO₂) from the air to manufacture carbohydrates.
- **Respiration and Decomposition:** Plants, animals, and decaying microbes break down food for energy, breathing CO₂ back into the air.
- **Combustion:** Burning firewood and fossil fuels releases large amounts of CO₂.

**2. The Water Cycle:**
- Heat from the sun turns liquid water into vapour (**evaporation**), and plants lose water vapour through their leaves (**transpiration**). The vapour cools into clouds (**condensation**) and falls back to earth (**precipitation**). Rainwater enters the soil (**infiltration**) to become groundwater.

**3. The Nitrogen Cycle:**
- **Nitrogen Fixation:** Atmospheric nitrogen gas (N₂) is converted into usable compounds by symbiotic bacteria (e.g., *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules) and free-living bacteria (e.g., *Azotobacter*).
- **Ammonification:** Decay-causing fungi and bacteria break down dead organisms into ammonium compounds.
- **Nitrification:** Ammonium is oxidised into nitrates in two steps. First, ***Nitrosomonas*** converts ammonia to nitrites. Second, ***Nitrobacter*** converts nitrites to nitrates, which plants absorb.
- **Denitrification:** In waterlogged soils, ***Pseudomonas*** bacteria reduce soil fertility by breaking down nitrates and releasing free nitrogen gas back into the air.

## The living population of the soil (flora and fauna)

The soil is alive with microscopic and macroscopic organisms that form its biological fraction. 
- **Soil Flora (Plant-like organisms and microbes):** Includes bacteria, fungi (moulds, mushrooms), algae, and actinomycetes (which give soil its earthy smell).
- **Soil Fauna (Animal organisms):** Includes earthworms, termites, ants, nematodes (microscopic roundworms), protozoa, and burrowing rodents.

### Examining the roles of soil flora and fauna

Soil organisms have both positive and negative effects on agricultural land and soil fertility.

**Beneficial roles:**
- **Decomposition and Humus Formation:** Fungi, bacteria, termites, and millipedes are primary decomposers. They break down dead plant and animal wastes, releasing trapped nutrients and forming dark, spongy **humus** that improves water retention.
- **Nitrogen Fixation:** Bacteria like *Rhizobium* and *Azotobacter* enrich the soil by capturing nitrogen gas from the air and converting it into nitrates for plants.
- **Improving Aeration and Drainage:** The burrowing action of earthworms, termites, and ants creates tunnels that allow air and water to penetrate deeply into the soil.
- **Mixing the Soil:** Earthworms consume soil and organic matter, passing it out as **worm casts** (vermicompost) which are extremely rich in plant nutrients. They constantly mix fertile topsoil with subsoil.

**Harmful roles:**
- **Pests and Diseases:** Nematodes attack plant roots (causing root-knot disease in tomatoes). Some fungi and bacteria cause wilts and root rots. Termites can destroy living crops and wooden farm structures.
- **Denitrification:** Denitrifying bacteria like *Pseudomonas* reduce fertility in poorly drained soils by converting valuable nitrates back into useless nitrogen gas.
- **Nutrient Competition:** If a farmer adds large amounts of dry, unrotted straw to the soil, microbes multiply rapidly and consume all available nitrogen to break it down, temporarily starving the growing crops.

## Maintenance of soil fertility

Every time a crop is harvested, nutrients leave the farm. To sustain yields, these nutrients must be replaced and the soil structure protected.

### Comparing the different methods of maintaining soil fertility

Farmers use several traditional and modern agricultural practices to maintain fertility.

| Method | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|-------------|------------|---------------|
| **Crop Rotation** | Growing different crops on the same land in a planned sequence, usually including a legume. | Prevents complete exhaustion of specific nutrients; breaks pest life cycles; legumes add nitrogen. | Requires skill and strict planning; does not suit farmers specializing in just one cash crop. |
| **Bush Fallowing** | Cultivating land for a few years, then abandoning it to allow natural bush to restore fertility. | Cheap; relies on nature; perfectly rebuilds humus and soil structure. | Requires massive amounts of land; highly impractical today due to population growth. |
| **Cover Cropping** | Planting fast-growing creeping legumes (e.g., *Mucuna*, *Centrosema*) to cover the bare soil. | Protects soil from erosion; suppresses weeds; adds nitrogen and organic matter. | Cover crops may compete with the main crop for water if not properly managed. |
| **Organic Manuring** | Adding natural wastes (compost, cow dung, poultry droppings) to the soil. | Drastically improves soil structure and water capacity; slow nutrient release. | Bulky, heavy, and expensive to transport; contains low nutrient concentrations. |
| **Inorganic Fertilization** | Applying chemically synthesized nutrients (like NPK, Urea) to the soil. | Very high concentrations of nutrients; fast-acting; easy to transport. | Expensive; easily leached by rain; does not improve soil physical structure. |

### Differentiating between organic and inorganic fertilizers

Fertilizers are materials added to the soil to supply essential plant nutrients. 
- **Organic Fertilizers:** Derived from decayed plant and animal matter (compost, green manure, dung). They have a very low nutrient concentration but greatly improve the physical structure of the soil. They are bulky and slow-release.
- **Inorganic Fertilizers:** Manufactured chemically in factories or mined. They have extremely high nutrient concentrations and are compact. They release nutrients immediately but do not improve soil structure and can cause acidity if overused.

**Methods of applying fertilizers:**
1. **Broadcasting:** Uniformly scattering fertilizer over the entire field by hand or machine. Fast, but wastes fertilizer on weeds.
2. **Ring/Annular placement:** Digging a shallow circular trench around the base of a plant. Excellent for widely spaced tree crops like cocoa and oil palm.
3. **Row/Band placement:** Applying fertilizer in continuous bands along crop rows, close to the roots of maize or yam.
4. **Top Dressing:** Applying fertilizer to a crop that is already established and actively growing, usually to supply fast-acting nitrogen.
5. **Foliar Application:** Dissolving micronutrients in water and spraying them directly onto the plant leaves.

### Determining common fertilizer ratios

Inorganic compound fertilizers are sold in bags with three numbers, such as **15:15:15** or **20:10:10**. This is the **fertilizer ratio**.
These numbers indicate the exact percentage (by weight) of the three primary macronutrients, always listed in the order **N - P - K**:
- **N:** Percentage of Nitrogen.
- **P:** Percentage of Phosphorus (as P₂O₅).
- **K:** Percentage of Potassium (as K₂O).
A 100 kg bag of NPK 15:15:15 contains 15% N, 15% P, and 15% K. The remaining 55% is filler material (sand or clay) to prevent caking and make spreading easier.

**Worked Example 1: Finding the weight of a nutrient applied.**
A farmer applies 400 kg of NPK 20:10:10 fertilizer to a hectare. Determine the amount of potassium applied.
**Step 1:** State the formula: $\text{Amount} = (\text{Percentage} / 100) \times \text{Total weight}$.
**Step 2:** Identify the percentage. Potassium (K) is the last number in 20:10:10, which is 10%.
**Step 3:** Calculate: $(10 / 100) \times 400\text{ kg} = 0.10 \times 400\text{ kg} = 40\text{ kg}$. The answer is 40 kg.

**Worked Example 2: Finding the quantity of fertilizer needed.**
A farmer needs to apply 92 kg of nitrogen per hectare using Urea (46% nitrogen). Calculate the total quantity of Urea required.
**Step 1:** State the formula: $\text{Quantity} = (\text{Target Amount} / \text{Percentage}) \times 100$.
**Step 2:** Substitute values: $(92 / 46) \times 100$.
**Step 3:** Calculate: $2 \times 100 = 200\text{ kg}$. The answer is 200 kg of Urea.

## Nutrient deficiency symptoms

When a plant lacks an essential nutrient, normal chemical processes break down, causing visible signs of starvation called deficiency symptoms.

### Identifying the deficiency symptoms and their causes

Different nutrients cause distinct visual symptoms:
1. **Chlorosis (Yellowing of leaves):** Caused by a lack of chlorophyll, most often due to severe **Nitrogen (N)** deficiency. Because nitrogen is highly mobile, the plant moves it to new growth, so yellowing starts from the **older, lower leaves**.
2. **Purplish or Reddish leaves:** Caused by **Phosphorus (P)** deficiency. Leaves turn abnormally dark green with distinct purple tints, especially on the undersides.
3. **Scorched or Burnt leaf margins:** Caused by **Potassium (K)** deficiency. The edges of the leaves turn brown and look burnt.
4. **Sickle leaves and Mottling:** Caused by **Zinc (Zn)** deficiency. Leaves fail to expand, becoming small, malformed (sickle-shaped), and mottled with pale patches.
5. **Apical necrosis (Death of growing tips):** Caused by **Calcium (Ca)** or Boron (B) deficiency. The terminal buds die. In tomatoes, lack of calcium causes the bottom of the fruit to rot (blossom-end rot).
6. **Stunting:** A general retarded growth caused by a severe deficiency of primary macronutrients, especially Nitrogen and Phosphorus.

### Suggesting remedies for nutrient deficiencies

Once symptoms are identified, rapid action is required to prevent yield loss:
- **For Nitrogen deficiency (Chlorosis, stunting):** Apply fast-acting nitrogen fertilizers like Urea or NPK as a top-dressing.
- **For Phosphorus deficiency (Purplish leaves):** Apply Single Superphosphate at planting. Ensure the soil is not overly acidic by adding lime.
- **For Potassium deficiency (Burnt margins):** Apply Muriate of Potash or sprinkle wood ash on the soil.
- **For Calcium deficiency (Apical necrosis, blossom-end rot):** Apply agricultural limestone (calcium carbonate), which provides calcium and corrects soil acidity.
- **For Micronutrient deficiencies (Zinc, Iron):** Use a foliar spray by dissolving trace elements in water and spraying directly onto the leaves for rapid stomatal absorption.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Macronutrients** | Essential plant nutrients required in large quantities (e.g., N, P, K). |
| **Micronutrients** | Essential plant nutrients required in very small, trace amounts (e.g., Fe, Zn). |
| **Nitrogen Fixation** | The conversion of atmospheric nitrogen gas into usable nitrates by bacteria. |
| **Nitrification** | The bacterial conversion of ammonia into nitrites, and then into nitrates. |
| **Humus** | The dark, spongy organic material formed from decomposed plant and animal matter. |
| **Cover Cropping** | Planting creeping legumes to cover bare soil, prevent erosion, and add nitrogen. |
| **Fertilizer Ratio** | The percentage by weight of Nitrogen, Phosphorus, and Potassium (N-P-K) in a fertilizer bag. |
| **Top Dressing** | Applying fertilizer to the soil surface while a crop is already actively growing. |
| **Chlorosis** | The yellowing of plant leaves due to a lack of chlorophyll (often nitrogen deficiency). |
| **Apical Necrosis** | The death of the terminal growing tips of a plant (often calcium deficiency). |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse macronutrients and micronutrients:** Remember N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, C, H, and O are macro. Metals like Iron, Zinc, Copper, and Manganese are micro.
- **Identify specific cycle bacteria:** *Rhizobium* (fixes nitrogen in legumes), *Nitrosomonas* and *Nitrobacter* (nitrification), *Pseudomonas* (denitrification).
- **Fertilizer calculation trap:** When asked for the amount of a nutrient in a bag, do not just pick the percentage number. Always multiply the percentage by the total weight.
- **Match symptoms exactly:** Nitrogen = yellowing of older leaves. Phosphorus = purplish leaves. Potassium = burnt leaf margins. Calcium = death of growing tips.
- **Application methods:** Ring placement is specifically suited for widely spaced tree crops like cocoa and citrus.

## Quick summary

1. Plant nutrients are grouped into macronutrients (needed in large amounts) and micronutrients (needed in trace amounts).
2. Nutrient availability is determined by soil pH, leaching, soil texture, crop removal, and soil microbes.
3. The nitrogen cycle utilizes bacteria to make nitrogen available to plants (fixation and nitrification) and sometimes removes it (denitrification).
4. Soil flora and fauna (bacteria, earthworms) decompose organic matter, form humus, and improve soil aeration, though some can act as pests.
5. Soil fertility is maintained using crop rotation, cover cropping, organic manures, and inorganic fertilizers.
6. Organic manures improve soil structure but are bulky; inorganic fertilizers act quickly but do not improve soil physical properties.
7. Fertilizer ratios like NPK 15:15:15 indicate the percentage by weight of Nitrogen, Phosphorus, and Potassium.
8. Nutrient deficiencies cause visual symptoms (chlorosis for N, purplish leaves for P, apical necrosis for Ca) which can be remedied with specific fertilizers or liming.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following groups contains only micronutrients (trace elements)?**
- A. Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium
- B. Calcium, Magnesium, Sulphur
- C. Iron, Zinc, Copper
- D. Carbon, Hydrogen, Oxygen

**Answer: C.** Iron, Zinc, and Copper are trace elements required by plants in minute quantities. The other options list macronutrients.

**2. The conversion of nitrites to nitrates in the nitrogen cycle is carried out by**
- A. *Nitrosomonas*
- B. *Rhizobium*
- C. *Nitrobacter*
- D. *Pseudomonas*

**Answer: C.** *Nitrobacter* oxidises nitrites into nitrates. *Nitrosomonas* handles the first step (ammonia to nitrites), and *Pseudomonas* causes denitrification.

**3. Which of the following is a major disadvantage of organic manure when compared to inorganic fertilizer?**
- A. It destroys soil structure over time
- B. It increases soil acidity quickly
- C. It releases nutrients too fast, causing burning
- D. It is bulky and difficult to transport

**Answer: D.** Organic manure is extremely bulky and has low nutrient concentrations, requiring huge amounts that are difficult to transport. Options A, B, and C are disadvantages of inorganic fertilizers.

**4. A farmer notices that the older leaves of his maize plants are turning yellow. The soil is most likely deficient in**
- A. Potassium
- B. Calcium
- C. Nitrogen
- D. Phosphorus

**Answer: C.** Nitrogen deficiency causes chlorosis (yellowing) that starts in the older leaves because nitrogen is highly mobile and moves to support new growth.

**5. How much phosphorus (P₂O₅) is applied to a farm if a farmer uses 300 kg of NPK 15:15:15 fertilizer?**
- A. 15 kg
- B. 30 kg
- C. 45 kg
- D. 90 kg

**Answer: C.** 
**Step 1:** The ratio for Phosphorus (middle number) is 15%.
**Step 2:** $\text{Amount of P} = (15 / 100) \times 300\text{ kg}$.
**Step 3:** $0.15 \times 300 = 45\text{ kg}$. Picking option A (just the percentage) is a common mistake.

**6. The most appropriate method of applying fertilizers to widely spaced tree crops like cocoa and citrus is**
- A. broadcasting
- B. ring placement
- C. foliar spray
- D. side dressing

**Answer: B.** Ring (or annular) placement involves digging a shallow circular trench around the base of the tree to place nutrients where the mature roots spread out.

**7. A soil organism that greatly improves soil aeration, drainage, and fertility by mixing soil and producing nutrient-rich casts is the**
- A. nematode
- B. earthworm
- C. tick
- D. virus

**Answer: B.** Earthworms burrow extensively, improving soil structure, and their casts are very rich in humus.

**8. Which of the following methods of maintaining soil fertility also serves as a biological method of weed control?**
- A. Application of NPK fertilizer
- B. Cover cropping
- C. Liming
- D. Minimum tillage

**Answer: B.** Cover cropping involves planting fast-growing creeping legumes that smother weeds by blocking sunlight, while simultaneously adding nitrogen.

**9. The death of the growing tips of a plant (apical necrosis) and blossom-end rot in tomatoes are classic symptoms of a deficiency in**
- A. Nitrogen
- B. Zinc
- C. Calcium
- D. Potassium

**Answer: C.** Calcium is essential for building new cell walls in growing tips and developing fruits. A lack of it causes the tips and fruit ends to die and rot.

**10. A farmer needs to supply 60 kg of nitrogen to his field. If he uses Urea containing 40% nitrogen, what total quantity of Urea must he buy?**
- A. 24 kg
- B. 60 kg
- C. 100 kg
- D. 150 kg

**Answer: D.** 
**Step 1:** $\text{Quantity of Urea} = (\text{Target nutrient} / \text{Percentage}) \times 100$.
**Step 2:** $\text{Quantity} = (60 / 40) \times 100$.
**Step 3:** $1.5 \times 100 = 150\text{ kg}$. A common mistake is finding 40% of 60 (which gives 24 kg).
