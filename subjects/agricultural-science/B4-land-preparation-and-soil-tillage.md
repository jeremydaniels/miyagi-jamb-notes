## Principles and practices of land preparation and soil tillage

**Content a: Principles and practices of land preparation and soil tillage.**

### Land preparation

**Land preparation** involves all the activities carried out on a piece of land to make it ready for planting. Before seeds or stems can be planted, especially on a virgin forest land or land that has been left fallow, the farmer must prepare it. 

The normal sequence of land preparation practices is:

1. **Land clearing:** This is the removal of the natural vegetation (trees, bushes, and grasses) from the land. It can be done manually using cutlasses and axes (slashing), mechanically using bulldozers, or chemically using herbicides. Sometimes, farmers use "slash and burn," where the cut vegetation is allowed to dry and is then set on fire. While burning releases quick ash nutrients (like potassium) and kills weed seeds, it destroys soil organic matter and beneficial soil organisms.
2. **Stumping:** This is the removal of the base of trees and their roots from the soil. It is a very difficult and labour-intensive task. Stumping is **essential if the farm is to be mechanised** (using tractors), because hidden roots and stumps can break ploughs and harrows.
3. **Farm layout and plotting:** Marking out the land into plots, roads, and paths to ensure efficient use of space and easy movement.
4. **Soil tillage:** The actual mechanical manipulation of the soil to prepare a seedbed.

### Soil tillage

**Tillage** is the mechanical breaking, loosening, and turning of the soil to create a favourable environment for seed germination and crop growth. The physical condition of the soil after tillage is called **tilth**. A "good tilth" means the soil is loose, crumbly, and well-aerated.

Tillage operations are usually carried out in stages:

1. **Primary tillage (Ploughing):** This is the first heavy digging or turning of the soil after land clearing. It cuts into the soil, shatters it, and turns it over, burying weeds and crop residues. It leaves the soil in large, rough lumps (clods). Ploughs (e.g., disc plough, mouldboard plough) are used.
2. **Secondary tillage (Harrowing):** This follows ploughing. It involves crushing the large soil clods left by the plough into a finer tilth, removing weed roots, and levelling the soil surface. Harrows are used for this.
3. **Tertiary tillage (Seedbed preparation):** This is the final shaping of the soil before planting. It includes making **ridges**, **mounds (heaps)**, or **raised beds**. This is typically done using a ridger or a traditional hoe.

## Comparing tillage methods for different groups of crops

**Objective i: compare the different methods of land preparation and soil tillage in relation to different groups of crops.**

Not all crops require the soil to be heavily ploughed and ridged. Over time, different tillage systems have been developed. The three main methods of tillage are **Conventional tillage**, **Minimum tillage**, and **Zero tillage (No-till)**.

| Tillage Method | Description | Most Suitable Crop Groups |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conventional Tillage** | Total mechanical manipulation of the soil. Involves land clearing, stumping, ploughing, harrowing, and making ridges, beds, or mounds. Leaves no vegetation on the surface. | **Root and tuber crops** (Yam, Cassava, Sweet potato, Cocoyam) because they need deep, loose soil to expand. **Vegetables** (Tomatoes, Pepper, Amaranthus) which need a very fine tilth and raised beds. |
| **Minimum Tillage** | The soil is worked as little as possible. Only the specific spot or row where the seed will be planted is disturbed (e.g., scraping a small hole or making a slit). The rest of the land is left untouched. | **Cereals** (Maize, Sorghum, Millet) and **Legumes** (Cowpea, Groundnut, Soybean) planted on fragile soils to prevent erosion. |
| **Zero Tillage (No-till)** | The soil is not ploughed or disturbed at all before planting. Seeds are planted directly into the unprepared soil using a dibber or planter. Weeds are heavily controlled using **chemical herbicides** (like glyphosate) instead of ploughing them under. | **Cereals and Legumes** on erosion-prone lands. **Tree crops** (Cocoa, Oil palm, Rubber) where seedlings are just transplanted into dug holes without ploughing the whole plantation. |

### Crop requirements and land preparation

* **Root and Tuber Crops:** Yam and cassava develop underground. If the soil is hard, the tubers will be small and deformed. Therefore, they **require** conventional tillage, specifically the making of ridges or large mounds (heaps), to provide a deep layer of loose soil for the tubers to swell, and to prevent waterlogging which causes tuber rot.
* **Cereals and Legumes:** Maize, guinea corn, and cowpeas have fibrous or relatively shallow taproot systems. They can germinate and grow well in minimal tilth. While they are often grown on ridges in Nigeria, they can be successfully grown using zero or minimum tillage to save costs.
* **Tree Crops:** Extensive ploughing is a waste of time and money for tree crops. Land is cleared, but instead of tillage, individual holes are dug for the seedlings.
* **Swamp Rice:** Requires a special preparation called **puddling**—tilling the soil while it is flooded with water to create an impenetrable mud layer that holds water on the surface.

## Advantages and disadvantages of the different tillage methods

**Objective ii: state the advantages and the disadvantages of the different methods of land preparation and soil tillage.**

### 1. Conventional Tillage

**Advantages:**
* It completely buries weeds and crop residues, providing excellent weed control at the start of the season.
* It loosens the soil deeply, improving aeration (oxygen for roots).
* It promotes the activities of soil microbes by mixing organic matter into the soil.
* It makes root penetration easy, which is absolutely essential for root and tuber crops.
* It exposes soil-dwelling pests (insects and nematodes) to the sun and predators (birds).

**Disadvantages:**
* It is very expensive and consumes a lot of labour, time, and fuel.
* It leaves the soil bare and loose, making it **highly vulnerable to severe wind and water erosion**.
* Continuous ploughing with heavy machines destroys the soil structure and can cause soil compaction or a hard layer underground called a **hardpan**.
* Increases the loss of soil moisture through evaporation.

### 2. Minimum Tillage

**Advantages:**
* Reduces the cost of production (less fuel and machinery wear).
* Reduces the risk of soil erosion since parts of the soil remain covered by crop residues or weeds.
* Prevents the rapid breakdown of soil structure and avoids hardpan formation.
* Conserves soil moisture.

**Disadvantages:**
* Weed control is not as effective as in conventional tillage; weeds may compete with crops.
* Not suitable for root and tuber crops because the soil is not loosened deeply.
* Seed germination may be slightly lower if the seedbed is not properly prepared.

### 3. Zero Tillage (No-till)

**Advantages:**
* Excellent for **soil conservation**; it drastically reduces soil erosion by leaving a protective cover (mulch/residue) on the soil surface.
* Conserves maximum soil moisture.
* Saves time, labour, and machinery costs significantly.
* Preserves soil organic matter and beneficial organisms (like earthworms) since the soil is not turned upside down.

**Disadvantages:**
* **Heavy reliance on herbicides:** Because weeds are not ploughed under, farmers must use chemical herbicides to kill them, which is expensive and can cause environmental pollution.
* Strictly unsuitable for root and tuber crops (yam, cassava).
* Soil may become too compact over time, restricting the root growth of some crops.
* Pests and disease pathogens left in crop residues on the surface can easily infect the new crop.

## Factors affecting choice of tillage methods

**Content b: Factors affecting choice of tillage methods: Zero tillage, minimum tillage, etc.**
**Objective iii: give reasons for the choice of tillage methods.**

A farmer must decide whether to use conventional, minimum, or zero tillage based on several factors. The reasons for choosing a particular tillage method include:

1. **The type of crop to be grown:** This is the most crucial factor. If a farmer is planting yam or cassava, they *must* choose conventional tillage (ploughing and ridging). If planting maize, they can choose zero or minimum tillage.
2. **Topography (slope) of the land:** On a steep slope, conventional tillage will lead to massive topsoil being washed away by rain (erosion). A farmer will choose **zero tillage** or minimum tillage on hilly or sloping lands to hold the soil in place.
3. **Soil type and texture:** Heavy clay soils usually require conventional tillage to open them up and improve drainage. On the other hand, loose, sandy soils that are easily eroded are better managed with minimum or zero tillage.
4. **Availability of herbicides:** Zero tillage requires the use of herbicides to kill weeds before planting. If a farmer cannot afford herbicides or they are not available, they will have to rely on conventional tillage (ploughing) to clear the weeds.
5. **Climatic factors (Rainfall):** In areas with very heavy and intense rainfall (e.g., the Niger Delta region of Nigeria), excessive tillage exposes the soil to severe erosion. Minimum or zero tillage might be preferred to leave a protective cover on the soil. Conversely, in areas with poor drainage and heavy rain, high ridges (conventional tillage) might be needed to keep crop roots out of waterlogged soil.
6. **Financial capacity and resources:** Conventional tillage using tractors and ploughs is expensive. A poor farmer might be forced to use minimum tillage with hand tools, while a large-scale commercial farmer might choose conventional tillage or specialised no-till seeders depending on their budget.
7. **Weed population and type:** If a farm is heavily infested with stubborn perennial weeds (e.g., spear grass or elephant grass), conventional deep ploughing might be chosen to bury them completely, or zero tillage combined with very strong systemic herbicides.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Land preparation** | All activities carried out to get a piece of land ready for planting (clearing, stumping, tillage). |
| **Stumping** | The removal of tree stumps and roots from cleared land; essential for farm mechanisation. |
| **Tillage** | The mechanical manipulation (ploughing, harrowing, ridging) of soil to prepare a seedbed. |
| **Tilth** | The physical condition of the soil after tillage (e.g., a "fine tilth"). |
| **Primary tillage** | The initial, deep opening and turning of the soil, usually using a plough, leaving large clods. |
| **Secondary tillage** | The breaking down of soil clods into a fine tilth, usually using a harrow. |
| **Conventional tillage** | Complete turning of the soil involving ploughing, harrowing, and ridging. Leaves no surface vegetation. |
| **Minimum tillage** | Disturbing the soil as little as possible, only at the spots where seeds will be placed. |
| **Zero tillage (No-till)** | Planting seeds directly into unprepared soil without any ploughing, relying on herbicides for weed control. |
| **Hardpan** | A hardened, compacted layer of soil below the surface, often caused by continuous heavy tractor ploughing. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Distinguish between clearing and tillage:** Land clearing removes vegetation; tillage works the soil itself. You cannot plough before clearing.
* **Stumping allows mechanisation:** A common JAMB question asks why stumping is done. The primary reason is to prevent damage to tractor implements like ploughs and harrows.
* **Tubers demand conventional tillage:** Never select minimum or zero tillage for yam, cassava, or sweet potato in an exam. They require deep tillage (ridging or mounding).
* **Zero tillage and herbicides:** Remember that zero tillage replaces the weed-killing action of the plough with chemical herbicides. High herbicide use is its main feature and drawback.
* **Erosion control:** If a question asks for the best tillage practice on a steep slope, the answer is zero tillage (or minimum tillage), because conventional tillage encourages erosion.
* **Sequence matters:** Memorise the correct order: Clearing $\rightarrow$ Stumping $\rightarrow$ Ploughing (Primary) $\rightarrow$ Harrowing (Secondary) $\rightarrow$ Ridging (Tertiary).

## Quick summary

1. Land preparation includes clearing, stumping, plotting, and soil tillage. Stumping is crucial for tractor use.
2. Tillage prepares a good seedbed (tilth). Primary tillage (ploughing) opens the soil; secondary tillage (harrowing) breaks clods; tertiary tillage (ridging) creates mounds or beds.
3. Conventional tillage completely turns the soil, which is necessary for root and tuber crops (yam, cassava) but exposes the soil to erosion.
4. Minimum tillage only disturbs the soil where seeds are planted, saving cost and reducing soil structure breakdown.
5. Zero tillage involves planting without ploughing. It prevents erosion and conserves moisture but relies heavily on chemical herbicides for weed control.
6. The choice of tillage depends heavily on the crop type, land topography (slope), soil texture, climate, and available resources (machinery or herbicides).
7. Cereals, legumes, and tree crops can successfully be grown using minimum or zero tillage.

## Practice questions

**1. The main reason for stumping during land preparation is to**
- A. increase soil fertility
- B. prevent soil erosion
- C. facilitate the use of heavy farm machinery
- D. improve soil aeration

**Answer: C.** Stumping removes roots and bases of trees which can break or damage tractor implements (ploughs and harrows) during mechanised farming.

**2. Which of the following sequences represents the correct order of land preparation on a virgin land?**
- A. Clearing $\rightarrow$ Stumping $\rightarrow$ Harrowing $\rightarrow$ Ploughing
- B. Stumping $\rightarrow$ Clearing $\rightarrow$ Ploughing $\rightarrow$ Harrowing
- C. Clearing $\rightarrow$ Stumping $\rightarrow$ Ploughing $\rightarrow$ Harrowing
- D. Clearing $\rightarrow$ Ploughing $\rightarrow$ Stumping $\rightarrow$ Harrowing

**Answer: C.** The land must first be cleared of vegetation, then stumps removed, followed by primary tillage (ploughing) and then secondary tillage (harrowing) to break the clods.

**3. A major disadvantage of conventional tillage is that it**
- A. encourages the buildup of pests
- B. exposes the soil to severe erosion
- C. prevents the breakdown of organic matter
- D. makes it difficult for roots to penetrate

**Answer: B.** Conventional tillage leaves the soil bare and completely loose, making it highly susceptible to being washed or blown away by water and wind.

**4. Which of the following groups of crops strictly requires conventional tillage (ridging or mounding)?**
- A. Maize and sorghum
- B. Cocoa and oil palm
- C. Yam and cassava
- D. Cowpea and groundnut

**Answer: C.** Yam and cassava are root/tuber crops. They require deep, loose soil provided by ridges or mounds to allow the tubers to expand without restriction.

**5. In zero tillage, weeds are primarily controlled by**
- A. manual weeding with hoes
- B. burning the field
- C. the use of chemical herbicides
- D. continuous grazing by animals

**Answer: C.** Since the soil is not ploughed to bury weeds in zero tillage, farmers rely heavily on chemical herbicides to clear weeds before planting.

**6. A farmer cultivating on a steep slope is advised to adopt zero tillage mainly to**
- A. increase the soil temperature
- B. check soil erosion
- C. promote the leaching of nutrients
- D. destroy hardpans

**Answer: B.** Steep slopes are highly prone to water erosion. Zero tillage leaves the soil undisturbed and covered with residue, which significantly reduces the washing away of topsoil.

**7. The physical condition of the soil after it has been ploughed and harrowed is referred to as**
- A. mulch
- B. profile
- C. tilth
- D. hardpan

**Answer: C.** Tilth refers to the physical condition of the tilled soil (e.g., fine tilth, rough tilth) regarding its suitability for seed planting.

**8. Which of the following is a tertiary tillage operation?**
- A. Ploughing
- B. Ridging
- C. Harrowing
- D. Stumping

**Answer: B.** Ploughing is primary, harrowing is secondary, and shaping the soil into ridges, beds, or mounds is tertiary tillage.

**9. Continuous use of heavy machinery for conventional tillage can lead to the formation of**
- A. root nodules
- B. a hardpan
- C. compost
- D. loose tilth

**Answer: B.** The weight of heavy tractors and the continuous scraping at a specific soil depth by ploughs can compact the subsoil, creating a dense, impermeable layer called a hardpan.

**10. One reason why a farmer might choose minimum tillage over conventional tillage for a maize farm is to**
- A. deeply loosen the soil for the roots
- B. ensure complete burial of all crop residues
- C. save on the cost of fuel and labour
- D. completely eradicate all soil pests

**Answer: C.** Minimum tillage involves fewer machinery passes over the field, which significantly cuts down the expenses related to tractor fuel, machinery wear, and labour.
