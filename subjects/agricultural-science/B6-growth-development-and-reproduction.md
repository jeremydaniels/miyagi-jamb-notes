## Gametogenesis

**Content a: Gametogenesis**

In flowering plants (angiosperms), sexual reproduction takes place in the flower. Before a plant can reproduce, it must create sex cells. **Gametogenesis** is the biological process by which these sex cells, or **gametes**, are formed in the reproductive organs of the plant.

For sexual reproduction to work, the joining of a male and female gamete must result in an offspring with the normal double set of chromosomes (**diploid** or 2n). Therefore, the gametes themselves must contain only half the normal number of chromosomes (**haploid** or n). The type of cell division that halves the chromosome number is called **meiosis**. 

### Process of gamete formation

**Objective: examine the process of gamete formation.**

There are two separate processes of gamete formation in plants: **microsporogenesis** (formation of male gametes) and **megasporogenesis** (formation of female gametes). 

#### 1. Formation of male gametes (Microsporogenesis)
The male reproductive organ of a flower is the **stamen**, consisting of a stalk (filament) and an **anther**. The male gametes are formed inside the pollen sacs of the anther.

**Step-by-step process:**
1. Inside a developing anther, there are special diploid (2n) cells known as **microspore mother cells** (or pollen mother cells).
2. Each microspore mother cell divides by **meiosis** to produce four haploid (n) cells called **microspores**.
3. These microspores separate and develop tough protective walls: an outer wall (exine) and an inner wall (intine). At this stage, they are known as **pollen grains**.
4. The single haploid nucleus inside the pollen grain then divides by normal cell division (mitosis) into two distinct nuclei:
   - A **tube nucleus**, which will later guide the growth of the pollen tube.
   - A **generative nucleus**, which will eventually divide again to form the two actual male gametes (sperm nuclei).
5. The mature pollen grain is now ready for release when the anther splits open.

#### 2. Formation of female gametes (Megasporogenesis)
The female reproductive organ is the **pistil** (or carpel), comprising a stigma, a style, and an **ovary**. Inside the ovary are one or more **ovules**. The female gamete (the egg cell) is formed inside the ovule.

**Step-by-step process:**
1. Inside the ovule, a single diploid (2n) cell called the **megaspore mother cell** enlarges.
2. This cell divides by **meiosis** to produce four haploid (n) cells called **megaspores**.
3. Usually, three of these megaspores degenerate and die, leaving only one large, surviving megaspore.
4. The nucleus of this surviving megaspore divides by mitosis three times. This results in eight haploid nuclei contained within a large sac called the **embryo sac**.
5. These eight nuclei arrange themselves precisely inside the embryo sac:
   - Three move to the top (the antipodal cells).
   - Two remain in the centre and fuse to form a single diploid **secondary nucleus** (or polar nuclei).
   - Three move to the bottom, near a small opening in the ovule called the micropyle. The middle one of these three is the **egg cell** (the female gamete), and the two beside it are called synergids.

## Pollination

**Content b: Pollination**

Because plants cannot physically move to find a mate, they must rely on external methods to bring their male and female gametes together. **Pollination** is the transfer of mature pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the mature, receptive stigma of the same flower or another flower of the same species.

### Types of pollination

**Objective: Identify the different types of pollination.**

There are two primary types of pollination based on the destination of the pollen: **self-pollination** and **cross-pollination**.

#### 1. Self-pollination
This is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the stigma of the **same flower** (autogamy), or to the stigma of **another flower on the same plant** (geitonogamy). 
- **Nigerian Crop Examples:** Cowpea, groundnut, rice, tomato, and cotton.
- **Advantages:** It is highly reliable. It ensures seed production even if no external agents (like insects) are present.
- **Disadvantages:** It leads to inbreeding. This reduces genetic variety and can weaken the crop over generations, lowering its ability to adapt to diseases or environmental changes.

#### 2. Cross-pollination
This is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower on one plant to the stigma of a flower on **another plant of the same species** (allogamy). 
- **Nigerian Crop Examples:** Maize, pawpaw, oil palm, cocoa, and rubber.
- **Advantages:** It mixes genes from different plants, producing offspring with greater genetic variation and often greater vigour and yield (hybrid vigour).
- **Disadvantages:** It depends heavily on external agents like wind or insects; if these agents are absent, pollination and subsequent crop yield may fail.

**Comparison of Self-pollination and Cross-pollination**

| Feature | Self-pollination | Cross-pollination |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plants involved** | Only one plant | Two different plants (of the same species) |
| **Genetic variation** | Low (offspring are genetically very similar to the parent) | High (offspring show new gene combinations) |
| **Dependence on agents** | Often does not require external agents | Heavily depends on wind, insects, birds, or water |
| **Pollen production** | Produces fewer pollen grains (less wastage) | Produces massive amounts of pollen grains to account for high wastage |

#### Agents of Pollination
Plants are structurally adapted to attract or utilize specific agents to carry their pollen. The two most important agents for agricultural crops are **wind** and **insects**. 

**Comparison of Wind-pollinated and Insect-pollinated Flowers**

| Feature | Wind-pollinated flowers (Anemophilous) | Insect-pollinated flowers (Entomophilous) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Examples** | Maize, rice, sorghum (guinea corn), grasses | Cowpea, cocoa, citrus, mango, cotton |
| **Petals** | Small, dull-coloured (green/brown), or absent | Large, brightly coloured to attract insects |
| **Scent and Nectar** | No scent, no nectar | Strongly scented, sweet nectar present |
| **Pollen grains** | Very large quantities, light, smooth, easily blown | Smaller quantities, sticky or spiky to stick to insects |
| **Stigma** | Large, feathery, hangs outside the flower to catch pollen | Sticky, usually enclosed within the flower |
| **Anthers** | Hang outside the flower on long filaments | Hidden inside the flower so insects brush past them |

## Fertilization

**Content c: Fertilization**

Once pollination has successfully placed a pollen grain onto a stigma, the next phase is fertilization. **Fertilization** is the fusion of the male gamete (sperm nucleus) and the female gamete (egg cell nucleus) to form a diploid cell called a **zygote**. 

In flowering plants, this process is highly specialized and is known as **double fertilization** because two separate nuclear fusions occur simultaneously inside the embryo sac.

### Process of fertilization

**Objective: analyse the process of fertilization.**

The journey from a pollen grain on the stigma to fertilization in the ovule happens step by step:

1. **Pollen germination:** When a compatible pollen grain lands on a mature, receptive stigma, it absorbs a sugary fluid secreted by the stigma.
2. **Pollen tube growth:** The pollen grain swells and germinates, sending out a fine tube called the **pollen tube**. The **tube nucleus** positions itself at the growing tip of this tube, guiding its path downwards through the tissues of the style towards the ovary.
3. **Division of the generative nucleus:** As the pollen tube grows down the style, the **generative nucleus** inside it divides by mitosis to form **two male gametes** (sperm nuclei). Both male gametes travel down the pollen tube.
4. **Entering the ovule:** The pollen tube reaches the ovary, finds an ovule, and enters it, usually through a tiny opening called the **micropyle**. 
5. **Release of gametes:** Once inside the embryo sac, the tip of the pollen tube bursts open. The tube nucleus degenerates (having finished its guiding role), and the two male gametes are released directly into the embryo sac.
6. **Double Fertilization occurs:**
   - **First fusion:** One male gamete (n) fuses with the **egg cell** (n) to form a diploid **zygote** (2n). This zygote will eventually develop into the plant **embryo**.
   - **Second fusion:** The second male gamete (n) moves to the centre of the embryo sac and fuses with the diploid **secondary nucleus** (2n) to form a triploid (3n) cell called the **primary endosperm nucleus**. This will develop into the **endosperm**, which stores food for the growing seed.

## Embryo formation and development

**Content d: Embryo formation and development**

Following double fertilization, the flower undergoes massive structural changes. The petals, sepals, stamens, style, and stigma usually wither and fall off, as their reproductive jobs are done. The plant now channels all its resources into developing the fertilized ovule into a **seed** and the surrounding ovary into a **fruit**.

### Process of embryo formation

**Objective: trace the process of embryo formation and development to the formation of seeds and fruits.**

The internal and external development happens in synchronized stages:

#### 1. Development of the Embryo and Seed
Inside the ovule, rapid cellular division transforms the microscopic structures into a mature seed:
- The **zygote** (2n) divides repeatedly by mitosis. It first forms a short chain of cells called the suspensor (which pushes the embryo deep into the food supply) and then develops into the **proembryo**.
- The proembryo differentiates to become the mature **embryo**. The embryo is a miniature plant consisting of:
  - The **plumule** (the embryonic shoot, which will grow into leaves and stems).
  - The **radicle** (the embryonic root).
  - One or two **cotyledons** (seed leaves). Plants with one cotyledon are monocots (like maize); plants with two are dicots (like cowpea).
- The **primary endosperm nucleus** (3n) divides rapidly to form the **endosperm**, a starchy tissue that acts as a food store. In some seeds (like maize), the endosperm remains large and distinct. In others (like groundnut or cowpea), the growing cotyledons absorb all the endosperm's food before the seed is fully mature, meaning the mature seed stores its food directly in the cotyledons.
- The outer layers of the ovule, called the **integuments**, dry up, harden, and become the tough protective seed coats: the outer **testa** and the inner **tegmen**.
- The micropyle remains as a tiny hole in the seed coat. It is crucial because it allows water and oxygen to enter the seed during germination.

**Conclusion of this phase:** The entire **ovule** transforms into the **seed**.

#### 2. Development of the Fruit
While the seed is developing inside, the surrounding **ovary** expands and ripens. 
- The ovary wall thickens and develops into the **pericarp** (the fruit wall). 
- Depending on the plant species, the pericarp may become fleshy, juicy, and sweet (as in a mango, tomato, or pawpaw) to attract animals for seed dispersal. Alternatively, it may become hard and dry (as in a cowpea pod, maize grain, or okra capsule).

**Conclusion of this phase:** The entire **ovary** transforms into the **fruit**.

#### Summary of Post-Fertilization Changes
This transformation is heavily tested in JAMB. You must know exactly what each floral part becomes after fertilization:

| Flower Part Before Fertilization | Structure After Fertilization |
| --- | --- |
| **Ovary** | **Fruit** |
| **Ovary wall** | **Pericarp** (fruit wall) |
| **Ovule** | **Seed** |
| **Integuments** (outer layers of ovule) | **Seed coats** (Testa and Tegmen) |
| **Zygote** | **Embryo** (plumule, radicle, cotyledons) |
| **Secondary nucleus (fertilized)** | **Endosperm** (food storage tissue) |
| **Micropyle** | **Micropyle** of the seed (hole for water entry) |
| Petals, Sepals, Stamens, Style, Stigma | Usually wither, dry up, and fall off |

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Gametogenesis | The biological process of gamete (sex cell) formation through meiosis |
| Microsporogenesis | The formation of male gametes (pollen grains) inside the anther |
| Megasporogenesis | The formation of female gametes (embryo sac/egg cell) inside the ovule |
| Pollination | The transfer of pollen grains from the anther to a receptive stigma |
| Double fertilization | The unique process in flowering plants where two male nuclei fuse separately with the egg cell and secondary nucleus |
| Zygote | The diploid (2n) cell formed when the male gamete fuses with the egg cell |
| Embryo | The developing young plant inside a seed, formed from the zygote |
| Endosperm | The triploid (3n) food storage tissue in a seed formed by the fusion of a male nucleus and the secondary nucleus |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Pollination vs. Fertilization:** A very common mistake is confusing these two. Pollination is just the physical *movement* of pollen (by wind or insects). Fertilization is the chemical *fusion* of the nuclei inside the ovule.
- **What becomes what:** JAMB frequently asks about the fate of flower parts. Memorize this golden rule: **Ovary = Fruit, Ovule = Seed, Zygote = Embryo, Integuments = Testa**.
- **Double fertilization outcomes:** Remember that the first male nucleus + egg cell = **diploid zygote** (2n). The second male nucleus + secondary nucleus = **triploid endosperm** (3n). 
- **Types of pollination:** Questions often test if you know crops that use wind versus insects. Maize, rice, and grasses are classic wind-pollinated examples; they have large, feathery stigmas and no brightly coloured petals. Cowpea and cocoa use insects.
- **Meiosis in gametogenesis:** Gamete formation *always* involves a reduction division (meiosis) so that the resulting pollen and egg cells are haploid (n).

## Quick summary

1. Gametogenesis is the formation of gametes. In plants, male gametes form in the anther (microsporogenesis) and female gametes form in the ovule (megasporogenesis) through meiosis.
2. Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma. 
3. Self-pollination involves one plant, leading to low genetic variation, while cross-pollination involves two plants of the same species and promotes hybrid vigour.
4. Wind-pollinated flowers have dull petals, large amounts of light pollen, and feathery stigmas. Insect-pollinated flowers have bright petals, nectar, and sticky pollen.
5. Fertilization begins when a pollen tube grows down the style, delivering two male nuclei to the embryo sac.
6. Flowering plants undergo double fertilization: one male nucleus fuses with the egg to form the zygote (embryo), and the other fuses with the secondary nucleus to form the endosperm (food store).
7. After fertilization, the ovule develops into the seed, and the entire ovary develops into the fruit.
8. The outer layers of the ovule (integuments) harden to become the seed coat (testa), while the ovary wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp).

## Practice questions

**1. The biological process of gamete formation in flowering plants involves a cell division that halves the chromosome number. This division is called**

- A. mitosis
- B. double fertilization
- C. meiosis
- D. binary fission

**Answer: C.** Meiosis is the reduction division that halves the chromosome number from diploid (2n) to haploid (n) to produce gametes. 

**2. The transfer of pollen grains from the anther of one flower to the stigma of another flower on a different plant of the same species is known as**

- A. autogamy
- B. cross-pollination
- C. self-pollination
- D. geitonogamy

**Answer: B.** Cross-pollination involves two different plants of the same species, which increases genetic variation and can lead to hybrid vigour.

**3. Which of the following is a structural characteristic of a wind-pollinated flower?**

- A. Brightly coloured petals
- B. Presence of sweet-smelling nectar
- C. Large, feathery stigmas
- D. Small quantities of sticky pollen

**Answer: C.** Wind-pollinated flowers have large, feathery stigmas that hang outside the flower to effectively catch pollen blowing in the air. The other options are adaptations for insect-pollinated flowers.

**4. In flowering plants, double fertilization is said to occur when**

- A. two pollen grains land on the stigma at the same time
- B. the generative nucleus divides into two tube nuclei
- C. one male nucleus fuses with the egg cell, and the other fuses with the secondary nucleus
- D. the zygote divides twice to form the proembryo

**Answer: C.** Double fertilization is the defining feature of angiosperm reproduction, involving two separate fusion events in the embryo sac simultaneously.

**5. After fertilization in a flowering plant, the mature ovule develops into the**

- A. fruit
- B. seed
- C. pericarp
- D. embryo

**Answer: B.** The fertilized ovule matures into the seed. The ovary, which entirely surrounds it, becomes the fruit.

**6. Which part of the flower develops into the tough outer seed coat (testa)?**

- A. Integuments
- B. Ovary wall
- C. Synergids
- D. Stigma

**Answer: A.** The integuments are the outer protective layers of the ovule that harden and dry out to become the testa (seed coat) after fertilization.

**7. The triploid (3n) tissue that stores food for the developing plant embryo is known as the**

- A. cotyledon
- B. suspensor
- C. radicle
- D. endosperm

**Answer: D.** The endosperm is formed by the fusion of a haploid male nucleus and the diploid secondary nucleus, making it triploid (3n). It serves as a primary food store for the seed.

**8. During the early stages of fertilization, the structure that guides the male nuclei down the style to the ovary is the**

- A. antipodal cell
- B. pollen tube
- C. micropyle
- D. filament

**Answer: B.** The germinating pollen grain grows a pollen tube down the style, which acts as a protected pathway to deliver the male gametes directly to the ovule.

**9. The immediate product of the fusion between a male gamete and an egg cell is called a**

- A. zygote
- B. proembryo
- C. plumule
- D. primary endosperm nucleus

**Answer: A.** The fusion of the haploid male and female gametes produces a diploid cell known as the zygote, which later undergoes mitosis to develop into the embryo.

**10. A farmer notices that a crop produces massive amounts of light, smooth pollen but has flowers with no scent or bright petals. The crop is most likely pollinated by**

- A. bees
- B. birds
- C. wind
- D. butterflies

**Answer: C.** Massive amounts of light pollen and lack of scent or bright petals are classic adaptations for wind pollination (anemophily), as seen in crops like maize and grasses. Wind is a random process, so the plant must produce massive amounts of pollen to ensure success.
