## a. Sexual propagation

**Sexual propagation** is the reproduction of plants through the use of **seeds**. A seed is the product of pollination and fertilization, meaning it contains genetic material from both a male and a female parent. 

**Advantages of sexual propagation:**
- Seeds are lightweight, making them easy and cheap to transport and store for long periods.
- It introduces genetic variation, which helps crops adapt to changing environments and develop resistance to certain diseases.
- Many viral diseases do not easily pass from the parent plant to the seed, producing disease-free offspring.
- It is the only practical, large-scale way to propagate staple crops like cereals and legumes.

**Disadvantages of sexual propagation:**
- Plants grown from seeds take a longer time to mature and bear fruit compared to those grown vegetatively.
- Due to genetic variation, the offspring may not look or perform exactly like the high-yielding parent (lack of uniformity).
- Seedless crops (like certain varieties of bananas and pineapples) cannot be propagated this way.

### Classify crops propagated by sexual methods

Many staple crops in Nigeria and West Africa are propagated exclusively or primarily by seeds. They can be classified into the following groups:

- **Cereals:** Maize (*Zea mays*), rice (*Oryza sativa*), sorghum or guinea corn (*Sorghum bicolor*), and millet.
- **Legumes:** Cowpea (*Vigna unguiculata*), groundnut (*Arachis hypogaea*), and soybean (*Glycine max*).
- **Vegetables:** Tomato, pepper, okra, onion, and amaranthus (green).
- **Tree crops:** Cocoa, oil palm, and rubber. (While modern commercial farming increasingly uses vegetative methods for some tree crops to ensure quick yields, they all naturally produce seeds and have traditionally been propagated sexually).

### Determine seed viability and seed rate

**Seed viability** is the ability of a seed's embryo to remain alive and successfully germinate into a healthy seedling under proper conditions. A farmer must determine if a batch of seeds is viable before planting to avoid wasting time, land, and resources on dead seeds.

**Methods of determining seed viability:**

1. **The Floatation (Water) Test:**
   - A handful of seeds is dropped into a bowl of water and stirred.
   - **Result:** Healthy, viable seeds are dense and will **sink** to the bottom. Dead, empty, or insect-damaged seeds contain air spaces and will **float** to the top. The floating seeds are discarded.

2. **The Germination Test:**
   - A random sample of seeds (for example, $100\text{ seeds}$) is selected.
   - They are planted in a tray with moist soil or placed between wet filter papers.
   - They are given the right conditions (warmth, moisture, and air) for a few days.
   - **Result:** The farmer counts how many seeds sprout. If $90\text{ seeds}$ out of $100$ sprout, the viability is $90\%$. Generally, a viability of $80\%$ or higher is excellent for planting.

3. **The Tetrazolium Test (Chemical Test):**
   - Seeds are soaked in a colourless chemical called tetrazolium chloride. 
   - **Result:** The living, respiring embryos turn the chemical pink or red. Dead embryos cause no colour change.

**Calculating Seed Rate:**

**Seed rate** is the total quantity of seeds required to plant a specific area of land, usually measured in kilograms per hectare ($\text{kg/ha}$). It depends on the spacing (plant density), the number of seeds planted per hole (stand), and the weight of the individual seeds.

**Formula for Seed Rate:**
$\text{Seed rate} = \frac{\text{Area of land}}{\text{Spacing area}} \times \text{Seeds per stand} \times \text{Weight of one seed}$

*Note: Always ensure your units match. Area is typically in square metres ($\text{m}^2$), and weight is often given in grams ($\text{g}$) and must be converted to kilograms ($\text{kg}$) for the final answer.*

**Worked Example:**
A farmer wants to plant cowpea on a $1\text{ ha}$ farmland. The recommended spacing is $1.0\text{ m} \times 0.5\text{ m}$. If the farmer plants $2\text{ seeds}$ per stand, and $1,000\text{ seeds}$ weigh $250\text{ g}$, calculate the seed rate in $\text{kg/ha}$.

- **Step 1:** Convert the land area into square metres ($\text{m}^2$).
  $1\text{ ha} = 10,000\text{ m}^2$.
- **Step 2:** Calculate the area occupied by one stand (spacing area).
  $\text{Spacing area} = 1.0\text{ m} \times 0.5\text{ m} = 0.5\text{ m}^2$.
- **Step 3:** Calculate the number of stands per hectare.
  $\text{Number of stands} = \frac{10,000\text{ m}^2}{0.5\text{ m}^2} = 20,000\text{ stands/ha}$.
- **Step 4:** Calculate the total number of seeds needed.
  $\text{Total seeds} = 20,000\text{ stands} \times 2\text{ seeds/stand} = 40,000\text{ seeds/ha}$.
- **Step 5:** Calculate the weight of one seed in grams ($\text{g}$).
  If $1,000\text{ seeds}$ weigh $250\text{ g}$, then $1\text{ seed}$ weighs $\frac{250\text{ g}}{1,000} = 0.25\text{ g}$.
- **Step 6:** Calculate the total weight of the seeds in grams.
  $\text{Total weight} = 40,000\text{ seeds} \times 0.25\text{ g/seed} = 10,000\text{ g/ha}$.
- **Step 7:** Convert the total weight to kilograms ($\text{kg}$).
  Since $1,000\text{ g} = 1\text{ kg}$, then $\frac{10,000\text{ g}}{1,000\text{ g/kg}} = 10\text{ kg/ha}$.
- **Answer:** The seed rate is **$10\text{ kg/ha}$**.

### Differentiate between types of seed germination

**Seed germination** is the process by which a dormant seed begins to sprout and grow into a seedling. 

When a seed germinates, the part of the stem below the cotyledon (seed leaf) is called the **hypocotyl**, and the part above the cotyledon is the **epicotyl**. Depending on which part grows faster and pushes out first, germination is differentiated into two types: **Epigeal** and **Hypogeal**.

| Feature | Epigeal Germination | Hypogeal Germination |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | The cotyledons are pushed **above** the soil surface. | The cotyledons remain **below** the soil surface. |
| **Mechanism** | The **hypocotyl** elongates very rapidly, carrying the cotyledons upward. | The **epicotyl** elongates rapidly, leaving the cotyledons underground. |
| **Role of cotyledons** | They often turn green and perform early photosynthesis until true leaves form. | They serve only as a stored food source and slowly shrivel underground. |
| **Plant group** | Common in most **dicotyledons**. | Common in most **monocotyledons**. |
| **Examples in West Africa** | Cowpea, groundnut, melon, tomato, castor oil. | Maize, rice, sorghum, millet, oil palm. |

### Examine the conditions for seed germination

For a viable seed to break dormancy and germinate, certain environmental conditions must be met. These conditions are:

1. **Adequate Moisture (Water):** 
   Water softens the hard seed coat (testa) so the embryo can break out. It also enters the seed and activates the enzymes that digest the stored food in the cotyledons or endosperm, converting it into soluble nutrients for growth.
2. **Oxygen (Air):** 
   Oxygen is essential for aerobic respiration. The rapidly growing embryo needs a massive amount of energy, which is released when stored food is broken down in the presence of oxygen.
3. **Optimum Temperature (Warmth):** 
   The enzymes responsible for growth only function properly within a specific temperature range. In Nigeria, the optimum temperature for most tropical crops is between $25\text{ °C}$ and $30\text{ °C}$. Seeds will not germinate if the soil is freezing or boiling hot.
4. **Seed Viability:** 
   The seed must be alive and healthy. A dead seed will not germinate even if all environmental conditions are perfect.

> **Common mistake:** Many students incorrectly list **light** as a necessary condition for seed germination. For the vast majority of agricultural crops (like maize, cowpea, and yam), light is **not** required for germination; seeds germinate perfectly well underground in complete darkness. Seedlings only need light *after* they emerge from the soil to begin photosynthesis.

## b. Asexual (vegetative propagation)

**Asexual or vegetative propagation** is the reproduction of plants using vegetative parts (such as stems, roots, or leaves) rather than seeds. 

**Advantages:**
- Offspring are genetically identical to the parent plant (**true to type** or clones), ensuring desirable traits (like sweet fruit or disease resistance) are perfectly preserved.
- Plants grow and produce fruit much faster than those grown from seeds.
- It is the only way to propagate seedless plants (e.g., seedless oranges, bananas, plantains).

**Disadvantages:**
- The lack of genetic variation means a disease that kills one plant could wipe out the entire farm.
- Vegetative materials (like cassava stems or yam tubers) are bulky, heavy, and difficult to transport and store compared to tiny seeds.
- Viral diseases present in the parent plant are easily transmitted to the offspring.

**Safety Precautions:** Tools used for vegetative propagation (budding knives, secateurs, cutlasses) must be extremely sharp to ensure clean cuts that heal quickly. They must also be sterilized to prevent transferring diseases from one plant to another.

### Classify crops into different vegetative propagation methods

Farmers use both artificial techniques (like grafting) and natural plant organs (like tubers) to propagate crops vegetatively. Crops can be classified based on these methods:

| Propagation Method / Organ | Description | Examples of Crops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stem Cuttings** | Severing a healthy, mature stem section at a slant and planting it in moist soil so it develops roots and shoots. | Cassava, sugarcane, sweet potato, croton. |
| **Budding** | Transferring a single bud (the **scion**) from a desired plant into a T-shaped cut in the bark of a rooted plant (the **rootstock**). | Citrus (oranges, lemons), rubber, cocoa. |
| **Grafting** | Joining a larger stem section with multiple buds (the **scion**) to a **rootstock** (using a V-shaped cleft or slanted whip cut) so their vascular tissues unite. | Mango, cocoa, citrus, rubber. |
| **Layering** | Forcing a branch to produce roots while it is still attached to the parent plant. *Ground layering* bends a branch into the soil. *Air layering (marcotting)* wraps moist soil around a scraped branch high in the tree. | Guava, cocoa, vine crops (tomatoes). |
| **Stem Tubers** | Swollen underground stems containing buds (eyes) that sprout into new plants. | Yam, Irish potato. |
| **Root Tubers** | Swollen roots capable of generating adventitious shoots. | Sweet potato, carrot. (Cassava storage roots have no buds and cannot be planted; cassava is propagated by stem cuttings.) |
| **Rhizomes** | Fleshy, horizontal underground stems. | Ginger, turmeric, spearmint. |
| **Suckers** | Shoots arising from the underground base of a mature plant. | Banana, plantain, pineapple. |
| **Bulbs and Corms** | Swollen fleshy leaf bases (bulbs) or solid, short underground stems (corms). | Onion, garlic (bulbs); cocoyam (corms). |

## c. Nursery and nursery management

A **nursery** is a specially prepared plot of land or a collection of containers where delicate seeds are planted and raised into young seedlings before they are permanently moved to the main field (a process called transplanting). 

Crops that require a nursery are usually those with very small, delicate seeds (like tomatoes and peppers) or slow-growing seedlings that need intense care (like cocoa, oil palm, and rubber).

### Determine appropriate nursery sites, types; their advantages and disadvantages

**Choosing an appropriate nursery site:**
To ensure seedlings grow vigorously and survive transplanting, a farmer must carefully select the site based on several factors:
1. **Nearness to a water source:** Nurseries require frequent watering, especially in the dry season. A reliable water supply (stream, tap, or well) nearby is mandatory.
2. **Topography:** The land should be relatively flat or gently sloping. Steep slopes cause soil erosion, while perfectly flat, depressed areas may lead to waterlogging.
3. **Soil quality:** The soil must be deep, well-drained, loamy soil, rich in organic matter to support delicate root development.
4. **Accessibility:** It should be easy to reach from the farmhouse and close to the main field to reduce transport stress during transplanting.
5. **Protection:** The site must be protected from strong winds, intense sunlight (which requires building a shade shed), and grazing animals.

**Types of nurseries, their advantages and disadvantages:**

| Type of Nursery | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Polythene Bags (Polybags)**<br>*Black plastic bags filled with topsoil, arranged in rows under shade.* | - Highly portable to the field.<br>- Zero root damage during transplanting (the bag is simply cut open).<br>- Soil-borne diseases do not spread easily from bag to bag. | - Expensive to purchase the bags.<br>- Filling thousands of bags with soil is highly labour-intensive.<br>- Generates non-biodegradable plastic waste. |
| **Ground Beds (Bed Nurseries)**<br>*Raised or sunken soil beds prepared directly on the ground.* | - Very cheap to prepare.<br>- Holds a massive number of seedlings.<br>- Roots can grow deep into natural soil. | - High risk of severe root damage when lifting seedlings.<br>- Weeds are a bigger problem.<br>- Diseases can spread rapidly through the continuous soil bed. |
| **Seed Boxes / Trays**<br>*Wooden or plastic shallow boxes filled with sterilized soil.* | - Highly portable and easy to move around or protect from heavy rain.<br>- Can be placed on raised platforms away from soil pests (like nematodes and rodents). | - Roots can easily become cramped due to shallow depth.<br>- Soil dries out very quickly and requires constant watering.<br>- Only suitable for a small number of seedlings. |

### Apply the techniques of transplanting seedlings

**Transplanting** is the process of moving a seedling from the nursery to its permanent position in the field. This causes a sudden change in environment and root disturbance, leading to **transplanting shock** (temporary wilting). 

To ensure the seedling survives, strict transplanting techniques must be applied step-by-step:

1. **Hardening off:** About a week before transplanting, the artificial shade over the nursery is gradually removed, and the frequency of watering is reduced. This toughens the seedlings and forces them to adapt to the harsh, sunny conditions of the open field.
2. **Pre-watering:** The nursery is watered heavily the evening before or the morning of transplanting to soften the soil. This prevents the delicate root hairs from snapping when the seedling is lifted.
3. **Lifting with a ball of earth:** A hand trowel is pushed deep under the seedling to lift it out with the soil still wrapped tightly around its roots (the "ball of earth"). This protects the fragile root system.
4. **Timing (Late Afternoon/Evening):** Transplanting is strictly done late in the afternoon, early evening, or on a cool, cloudy morning. This minimizes the rate of transpiration, giving the plant a cool overnight period to recover from the shock before the hot sun strikes it the next day.
5. **Planting out:** The seedling is placed in a properly dug hole in the main field at the exact same depth it was growing in the nursery.
6. **Firming and watering:** The soil is firmed gently around the base of the plant to remove air pockets, and the seedling is watered immediately.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Sexual propagation** | Reproduction of plants using seeds. |
| **Vegetative propagation** | Asexual reproduction of plants using stems, roots, or leaves instead of seeds. |
| **Seed viability** | The ability of a seed's embryo to remain alive and successfully germinate. |
| **Seed rate** | The quantity of seeds required to plant a specific area of land (e.g., $\text{kg/ha}$). |
| **Epigeal germination** | Germination where the cotyledons are pushed above the soil surface. |
| **Hypogeal germination** | Germination where the cotyledons remain below the soil surface. |
| **Scion** | The upper part (bud or stem) of a plant used in budding or grafting. |
| **Rootstock** | The lower rooted part of a plant onto which a scion is grafted or budded. |
| **Marcotting** | Another name for air layering, a method of inducing roots on a branch high up in a tree. |
| **Nursery** | A specially prepared place where seeds are raised into young seedlings before transplanting. |
| **Hardening off** | The gradual reduction of shade and water in a nursery to prepare seedlings for the harsh main field. |
| **Transplanting shock** | The stress and temporary wilting a seedling experiences when moved from the nursery to the field. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Seed rate calculations:** Always ensure your units are consistent. Remember $1\text{ hectare} = 10,000\text{ m}^2$. Also, check spacing calculations carefully. For example, a spacing of $0.5\text{ m} \times 0.5\text{ m}$ equals $0.25\text{ m}^2$, NOT $0.5\text{ m}^2$.
- **Epigeal vs Hypogeal:** "Epi-" means above/on top (cotyledons above ground). "Hypo-" means under/below (cotyledons stay underground).
- **Conditions for germination:** DO NOT choose "light" as a general condition for seed germination. Water, oxygen, and optimum temperature are the compulsory three (along with a viable seed).
- **Scion vs Rootstock:** The **scion** is the "shoot" you want to multiply (it determines the fruit quality). The **rootstock** provides the "roots" (it provides disease resistance and physical stability).
- **Transplanting time:** Always transplant in the **cool of the evening** or late afternoon. If a question option says "when the sun is highest" or "mid-day," it is completely wrong because high transpiration at noon causes severe, often fatal, transplanting shock.

## Quick summary

1. Crops can be propagated sexually using seeds (which provides genetic variation but takes longer) or asexually using vegetative parts (which produces exact clones faster).
2. Seed viability can be determined using the floatation test, germination test, or tetrazolium test to ensure seeds are alive before planting.
3. Seed rate ($\text{kg/ha}$) is calculated using the land area, plant spacing, number of seeds per stand, and the weight of the seeds.
4. Germination requires adequate moisture, oxygen, optimum temperature, and a viable embryo. Light is not generally required.
5. In epigeal germination (e.g., cowpea), cotyledons are carried above ground; in hypogeal germination (e.g., maize), they remain underground.
6. Major vegetative propagation methods include cuttings, budding (using a single bud), grafting (using a stem), and layering. Natural storage organs like tubers, bulbs, and rhizomes are also used.
7. Nurseries (polybags, ground beds, seed trays) are used to raise delicate seedlings in a protected, water-accessible environment before transplanting.
8. Seedlings must be hardened off, lifted with a ball of earth, and transplanted late in the evening to minimize transplanting shock.

## Practice questions

**1. Calculate the seed rate in $\text{kg/ha}$ for a maize farm if the area is $1\text{ ha}$, spacing is $0.5\text{ m} \times 0.5\text{ m}$, $1\text{ seed}$ is planted per stand, and $1,000\text{ seeds}$ weigh $500\text{ g}$.**
- A. $10\text{ kg/ha}$
- B. $20\text{ kg/ha}$
- C. $25\text{ kg/ha}$
- D. $40\text{ kg/ha}$

**Answer: B.** 
- *Step 1:* Land area is $1\text{ ha} = 10,000\text{ m}^2$. 
- *Step 2:* Spacing area = $0.5\text{ m} \times 0.5\text{ m} = 0.25\text{ m}^2$. 
- *Step 3:* Number of stands = $\frac{10,000\text{ m}^2}{0.25\text{ m}^2} = 40,000\text{ stands/ha}$. 
- *Step 4:* Total seeds = $40,000\text{ stands} \times 1\text{ seed/stand} = 40,000\text{ seeds/ha}$. 
- *Step 5:* Weight of one seed = $\frac{500\text{ g}}{1,000} = 0.5\text{ g}$. 
- *Step 6:* Total weight in grams = $40,000\text{ seeds} \times 0.5\text{ g/seed} = 20,000\text{ g/ha}$. 
- *Step 7:* Convert to kg: $\frac{20,000\text{ g}}{1,000\text{ g/kg}} = 20\text{ kg/ha}$. 
Option D is a common mistake for students who forget to multiply by the individual seed weight, incorrectly leaving the figure at $40,000$ and dividing by $1000$.

**2. Which of the following is NOT a required condition for the germination of most agricultural seeds?**
- A. Adequate moisture
- B. Light
- C. Oxygen
- D. Optimum temperature

**Answer: B.** Light is generally not required for seeds to break dormancy; most germinate effectively under the soil in total darkness. Water, oxygen, and a favourable temperature are compulsory.

**3. In seed viability testing using the floatation method, the seeds that float to the top are usually**
- A. highly viable
- B. heavy and healthy
- C. dead or insect-damaged
- D. the best for planting

**Answer: C.** Dead, empty, or weevil-damaged seeds contain air spaces that make them buoyant, so they float. Viable, healthy seeds are heavy and will sink.

**4. The type of germination in which the cotyledons are pushed above the soil surface is known as**
- A. hypogeal germination
- B. epigeal germination
- C. monocot germination
- D. vegetative germination

**Answer: B.** In epigeal germination, the rapidly growing hypocotyl pushes the cotyledons above the ground. This is common in dicots like cowpea and groundnut.

**5. Which of the following crops undergoes hypogeal germination?**
- A. Cowpea
- B. Groundnut
- C. Maize
- D. Melon

**Answer: C.** Maize is a cereal and a monocot; it undergoes hypogeal germination where the epicotyl grows rapidly, leaving the cotyledon below the soil surface.

**6. In budding and grafting, the part of the plant that is inserted into the rooted base plant is called the**
- A. rootstock
- B. scion
- C. sucker
- D. hypocotyl

**Answer: B.** The scion is the bud or cut stem from the desired superior plant that is inserted into the rootstock. The rootstock is the base that provides the root system.

**7. Ginger and turmeric are typically propagated asexually by the use of**
- A. root tubers
- B. stem tubers
- C. suckers
- D. rhizomes

**Answer: D.** Ginger and turmeric are naturally propagated using rhizomes, which are fleshy, horizontal underground stems that sprout new shoots.

**8. Marcotting is an alternative name for which vegetative propagation technique?**
- A. Air layering
- B. Ground layering
- C. T-budding
- D. Whip grafting

**Answer: A.** Marcotting is another term for air layering, where a section of bark is removed from a branch high in the air and wrapped in moist soil to induce rooting.

**9. A major advantage of raising seedlings in polythene bags rather than on ground beds is that**
- A. polythene bags cost nothing to acquire
- B. there is minimal root damage during transplanting
- C. filling the bags with soil requires less labour
- D. polythene bags hold more seedlings per square metre

**Answer: B.** Because the seedling grows its own isolated root system inside the bag, the bag is simply cut away during transplanting. This ensures the roots remain completely undisturbed, vastly reducing transplanting shock.

**10. Transplanting of seedlings is best done late in the afternoon or early evening in order to**
- A. ensure the soil is warm enough for the roots
- B. increase the rate of photosynthesis
- C. minimize transplanting shock and transpiration
- D. allow pests to feed on the leaves overnight

**Answer: C.** Transplanting in the evening reduces water loss (transpiration) caused by the hot sun. This gives the plant a cool, shaded period overnight to recover from the stress (transplanting shock) of root disturbance.
