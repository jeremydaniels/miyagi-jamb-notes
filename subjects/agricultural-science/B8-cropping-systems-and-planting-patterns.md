## Cropping Systems

**Content a: Cropping systems: Monocropping, mixed-, multiple-, inter-, relay-, strip- and rotational cropping**

A **cropping system** refers to the type and sequence of crops grown on a piece of land over a period of time, as well as the spatial arrangement of these crops in the field. Different systems are suited to different environments, farmer needs, and technological levels.

- **Monocropping (Monoculture):** The practice of growing only **one type of crop** on a piece of land at a given time. Examples include a large commercial farm planted entirely with maize or an extensive oil palm plantation. It makes mechanisation (using tractors) very easy but encourages the rapid build-up of specific pests and depletes specific soil nutrients quickly.
- **Mixed cropping:** Growing **two or more crops** on the same piece of land at the same time, **without any definite row arrangement**. Seeds or cuttings are planted randomly. It provides insurance against crop failure (if one crop fails, another may survive) and suppresses weeds, but makes mechanisation very difficult.
- **Multiple cropping:** Growing **two or more crops** on the same field **within one calendar year**. This aims to maximise the total crop output from a single piece of land in one year, especially where land is scarce or irrigation is available.
- **Intercropping:** Growing **two or more crops** on the same land at the same time, but in **definite row arrangements** (for example, alternating rows of maize and cowpea). It maximises land use and reduces pest spread while still allowing for careful movement through the field.
- **Relay cropping:** Planting a second crop into a standing crop **before the first crop is harvested**. For example, planting cassava cuttings into a field of maize that is almost mature. The second crop takes over immediately after the first is harvested, ensuring the land is never left bare.
- **Strip cropping:** Planting two or more different crops in **alternating, broad bands or strips** across a field, usually along the contour of a slope. A common pattern alternates a tall row crop (like maize) with a dense ground-covering crop (like groundnut). It is an excellent cultural (agronomic) method for controlling soil erosion.
- **Rotational cropping (Crop rotation):** Growing different types of crops on the same piece of land in a **definite, planned sequence** from year to year. A good rotation alternates deep-rooted crops (yam) with shallow-rooted crops (maize), avoids planting crops of the same family back-to-back to break pest life cycles, and always includes a leguminous crop (cowpea) to restore soil nitrogen.

## Applying Cropping Systems to Solve Problems

**Objective ii: apply different cropping systems to solve problems in agriculture.**

Farmers select specific cropping systems to address practical, environmental, or economic challenges on their farms:

1. **Problem: Severe soil erosion on sloping land**
   - **Solution:** Apply **strip cropping** across the slope to slow down water runoff. The dense cover strips trap soil washed down from the row-crop strips. **Mixed cropping** or **intercropping** with heavy cover crops (like melon or sweet potato) also protects bare soil from the direct impact of heavy rain.
2. **Problem: High pest and disease incidence**
   - **Solution:** Adopt **rotational cropping**. Changing the crop family every season breaks the life cycle of pests and diseases that starve when their preferred host is removed. **Intercropping** can also help by using one crop as a physical barrier or repellent for pests of the other.
3. **Problem: Rapid loss of soil fertility**
   - **Solution:** Implement **rotational cropping** or **intercropping** that includes leguminous crops (e.g., cowpea, groundnut, soybean). Legumes fix atmospheric nitrogen, naturally enriching the soil. Alternating deep-rooted and shallow-rooted crops in a rotation prevents total nutrient depletion from a single soil layer.
4. **Problem: Severe shortage of farm land**
   - **Solution:** Use **multiple cropping**, **mixed cropping**, or **intercropping**. These systems maximise the total yield from a small piece of land by growing more than one crop in the same space or within the same year.
5. **Problem: High economic risk of total crop failure (due to weather or pests)**
   - **Solution:** Rely on **mixed cropping** or **intercropping**. Growing a variety of crops provides insurance; if a drought destroys the maize, a drought-resistant crop like cassava or sorghum will likely still yield a harvest, ensuring the farmer has food.

## Planting Patterns: Broadcasting, Row Spacing and Drilling

**Content b: Planting patterns: Broadcasting, row spacing and drilling**

A **planting pattern** refers to how seeds or planting materials are distributed spatially across the field. The choice of pattern depends on the crop type, seed size, the level of mechanisation available, and the desired plant density.

- **Broadcasting:** The random scattering of seeds over the surface of a prepared seedbed, usually by hand, after which the seeds are lightly covered with soil. It is used for very small seeds like rice, sesame (benniseed), and pasture grasses. It is fast and cheap but results in an uneven plant density and makes weeding and harvesting difficult because there are no paths.
- **Row spacing (Row planting):** Planting seeds or vegetative materials at specific, measured distances in distinct, straight lines. The space between two rows is the **inter-row spacing**, and the space between two plants within the same row is the **intra-row spacing**. It is used for most major staple crops (maize, cowpea, cassava, yam). It ensures an optimal plant density and creates clear paths for weeding, fertilising, and harvesting, though it requires more time and labour to set up.
- **Drilling:** Dropping seeds in a continuous line into shallow furrows (drills) and covering them with soil in a single operation. It is typically done using a tractor-drawn seed drill. It is ideal for small grains (wheat, rice, sorghum) that benefit from being in rows. It places seeds at a very precise, uniform depth, ensuring excellent germination, but requires expensive machinery and a very fine seedbed.

## Differentiating Between the Various Planting Patterns

**Objective: differentiate between the various planting patterns.**

To differentiate them in exam questions, focus on how the seeds are placed, the tools used, and the ease of subsequent farm operations.

| Feature | Broadcasting | Row spacing | Drilling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Seed arrangement** | Random and scattered | Distinct, exact intervals in straight lines | Continuous lines inside shallow furrows |
| **Typical seed size** | Small seeds (rice, grass) | Medium to large seeds/cuttings | Small to medium grains |
| **Seed rate** | High (wasteful, seeds may be eaten by birds) | Low (economical) | Low (highly economical) |
| **Mechanisation later**| Difficult (no clear paths to drive or walk) | Easy | Easy |
| **Depth of planting** | Uneven | Controlled by hand or simple tools | Very precise (machine controlled) |

## Plant Densities: Single, Double and Multiple Stands

**Content c: Plant densities: single, double and multiple stands**

A **stand** (or hole) is the specific spot in the soil where one or more seeds or planting materials are placed. **Plant density** (or plant population) is the total number of individual plants growing on a given area of land, usually expressed as plants per hectare.

- **Single stand:** Planting exactly **one seed or cutting** per hole. This is used when the seeds have very high viability (germination rates) or for large plants that need immense space, like yam, oil palm, and cocoa. If a seed fails to germinate, the farmer performs **supplying** (or gap filling) by planting a new seed in the empty spot.
- **Double stand:** Planting **two seeds** per hole. This acts as insurance; if one seed fails to germinate, the other likely will. If both grow, they share the stand. It is a common practice with crops like maize or cowpea.
- **Multiple stands:** Planting **three or more seeds** per hole. This is common when the farmer suspects the seed quality is poor. Once the seeds germinate, the farmer usually performs **thinning**, which is the removal of the extra, weaker seedlings, leaving only one or two strong plants per stand to prevent severe competition.

## Examining Plant Densities and Their Effects on Crop Yield

**Objective i: examine the various types of plant densities and their effects on crop yield.**

The density of plants drastically affects how they grow because plants must compete for limited resources: sunlight, water, space, and soil nutrients.

1. **Low plant density (Too wide spacing)**
   - Plants have plenty of space, nutrients, and sunlight, so individual plants grow very large and yield well (e.g., very large maize cobs).
   - However, because there are few plants per hectare, the **overall yield per hectare is low**. The farmer is wasting valuable land.
   - The large open spaces allow weeds to grow aggressively, increasing weeding costs. Moisture is also easily lost from the bare soil by evaporation.

2. **Optimum plant density (Correct spacing)**
   - This is the ideal balance where plants are close enough to cover the ground but far enough apart to avoid severe competition.
   - It produces the **maximum economic yield per hectare**.
   - The leaves of the crop quickly form a canopy that shades the soil, naturally suppressing weeds and conserving soil moisture.

3. **High plant density (Too close spacing or unthinned multiple stands)**
   - Plants compete fiercely for limited light, water, and nutrients.
   - To reach for sunlight, the plants grow excessively tall, thin, and weak (spindly). They may easily fall over (lodge) during strong winds or heavy rain.
   - Individual plants produce tiny fruits, small seeds, or no marketable yield at all.
   - The crowded, humid conditions limit air circulation, encouraging the rapid spread of fungal diseases and pests.
   - The total marketable yield per hectare **drops significantly**.

## Computing Plant Density Per Hectare

**Objective ii: compute plant density per hectare.**

In JAMB exams, you are frequently asked to calculate the number of stands or the total plant population on a farm. 

**The essential formulas:**
$$ \text{Area of farm (m}^2\text{)} = \text{Length (m)} \times \text{Width (m)} $$
*(Note: 1 hectare (ha) is always equal to 10,000 square metres (m²).)*
$$ \text{Area per stand (m}^2\text{)} = \text{Inter-row spacing (m)} \times \text{Intra-row spacing (m)} $$
$$ \text{Number of stands} = \frac{\text{Total Area (m}^2\text{)}}{\text{Area per stand (m}^2\text{)}} $$
$$ \text{Total Plant Population} = \text{Number of stands} \times \text{Number of seeds per stand} $$

**Important Rule:** Always convert spacing given in centimetres (cm) into metres (m) before multiplying! (Divide the cm value by 100). For example, 50 cm = 0.5 m.

---

**Worked Example 1: Single stand calculation**
A farmer plants maize on 1 hectare of land at a spacing of 80 cm by 50 cm. If she plants one seed per stand, calculate the plant density (total plant population) per hectare.

**Step 1:** Find the total area of the land in square metres (m²).
The land area is 1 hectare. Since 1 hectare = 10,000 m², the total area is 10,000 m².

**Step 2:** Convert the spacing from centimetres to metres.
The spacing is 80 cm by 50 cm.
Distance between rows = 80 cm ÷ 100 = 0.8 m.
Distance between plants = 50 cm ÷ 100 = 0.5 m.

**Step 3:** Calculate the area occupied by a single stand.
Area per stand = 0.8 m × 0.5 m = 0.4 m².

**Step 4:** Calculate the total number of stands.
Number of stands = 10,000 m² ÷ 0.4 m² = 25,000 stands.

**Step 5:** Calculate the total plant population.
Since the farmer plants 1 seed per stand:
Total plants = 25,000 stands × 1 seed per stand = 25,000 plants.
**Answer:** The plant density is 25,000 plants per hectare.

---

**Worked Example 2: Double stand on a larger farm**
A cowpea farm measures 2.5 hectares. The crop is planted at a spacing of 100 cm by 50 cm. If two seeds are sown per stand, what is the total plant population on the farm?

**Step 1:** Find the total area in square metres (m²).
Area = 2.5 ha × 10,000 m²/ha = 25,000 m².

**Step 2:** Convert spacing to metres.
The spacing is 100 cm by 50 cm.
In metres: 1.0 m by 0.5 m.

**Step 3:** Calculate the area per stand.
Area per stand = 1.0 m × 0.5 m = 0.5 m².

**Step 4:** Calculate the number of stands.
Number of stands = 25,000 m² ÷ 0.5 m² = 50,000 stands.

**Step 5:** Calculate the total plant population.
The farmer plants **two** seeds per stand (double stand).
Total plants = 50,000 stands × 2 seeds per stand = 100,000 plants.
**Answer:** The total plant population on the farm is 100,000 plants.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Cropping system** | The type and sequence of crops grown on a field over time and their spatial arrangement |
| **Monocropping** | Growing only one type of crop on a piece of land at a time |
| **Mixed cropping** | Growing two or more crops together at the same time without definite row arrangements |
| **Intercropping** | Growing two or more crops together at the same time in definite row arrangements |
| **Crop rotation** | Growing different crops in a planned sequence on the same land over successive years |
| **Relay cropping** | Planting a second crop into a standing first crop before the first is harvested |
| **Broadcasting** | Randomly scattering seeds over the surface of the soil |
| **Drilling** | Sowing seeds in continuous lines inside shallow furrows, usually using a machine |
| **Stand** | The exact spot or hole where one or more seeds/cuttings are planted |
| **Thinning** | Removing excess seedlings from a multiple stand to reduce competition |
| **Supplying** | Replanting seeds in stands where the initial seeds failed to germinate |
| **Plant density** | The number of individual plants per unit area of land (usually per hectare) |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Centimetres to Metres:** The most common and fatal mistake in plant density calculations is forgetting to convert spacing (like 80 cm) to metres (0.8 m) before finding the area. If you divide 10,000 by (80 × 50), you will get an incredibly tiny fraction! Always convert to metres first.
- **Seeds per stand:** Always read the question carefully to see if it specifies one seed, two seeds, or three seeds per stand. You must multiply the number of stands by this number to get the final plant population.
- **Mixed cropping vs. Intercropping:** JAMB frequently tests this difference. The key word is **rows**. If there are no rows (random), it is mixed cropping. If there are definite rows, it is intercropping.
- **Hectare conversion:** 1 hectare is always exactly 10,000 m². Memorise this conversion factor.
- **Solving erosion:** If a question asks which cropping system best solves water erosion on a steep slope, look for **strip cropping** or the use of cover crops.
- **Solving pests/diseases:** If the question asks about breaking the life cycle of pests, the answer is usually **crop rotation**.

## Quick summary

1. A cropping system defines how crops are arranged on land spatially and over time.
2. Monocropping grows a single crop, making mechanisation easy but risking severe pest build-up and total crop failure.
3. Mixed cropping and intercropping grow multiple crops together; mixed cropping lacks row arrangements, while intercropping uses distinct rows.
4. Crop rotation maintains soil fertility and breaks pest life cycles by growing different crops sequentially (including legumes and alternating deep/shallow-rooted crops) over several years.
5. Planting patterns include broadcasting (random scattering of small seeds), row spacing (placing seeds at exact intervals), and drilling (continuous lines in furrows).
6. A single stand has one seed per hole; multiple stands have three or more and require thinning to prevent crowding.
7. High plant density causes severe competition, weak (spindly) plants, lodging, and very low overall yields, while optimum density maximises economic yield per hectare.
8. To compute plant population: Convert spacing to metres, calculate the area of one stand, divide the total land area in m² by the stand area, and multiply the result by the number of seeds per stand.

## Practice questions

**1. A cropping system where different crops are grown on the same piece of land in a definite, planned sequence from year to year is called**
- A. mixed cropping
- B. crop rotation
- C. intercropping
- D. multiple cropping

**Answer: B.**
Crop rotation involves changing the type of crop grown on a piece of land sequentially over several years to maintain soil fertility and break the life cycles of pests and diseases.

**2. The major difference between mixed cropping and intercropping is that in intercropping,**
- A. only two crops are grown
- B. the crops are grown in definite row arrangements
- C. the soil fertility is depleted much faster
- D. harvesting is always done at exactly the same time

**Answer: B.**
While both systems involve growing multiple crops at the same time, intercropping arranges the crops in distinct, measurable rows, whereas mixed cropping involves random scattering with no row arrangement.

**3. Which of the following cropping systems is primarily used to solve the problem of severe soil erosion on sloping land?**
- A. Monocropping
- B. Relay cropping
- C. Strip cropping
- D. Crop rotation

**Answer: C.**
Strip cropping, which involves planting alternating broad bands of tall row crops and dense cover crops across a slope, slows down water runoff and is an excellent cultural (agronomic) method for controlling soil erosion.

**4. A farmer planted maize at a spacing of 100 cm by 50 cm on a 1-hectare land. If two seeds were sown per stand, what is the plant density per hectare?**
- A. 20,000 plants per hectare
- B. 40,000 plants per hectare
- C. 50,000 plants per hectare
- D. 10,000 plants per hectare

**Answer: B.**
Step 1: Determine the area of 1 hectare in square metres. 1 ha = 10,000 m².
Step 2: Convert the spacing from cm to m. 100 cm = 1.0 m; 50 cm = 0.5 m.
Step 3: Calculate the area per stand. 1.0 m × 0.5 m = 0.5 m².
Step 4: Calculate the number of stands. 10,000 m² ÷ 0.5 m² = 20,000 stands.
Step 5: Calculate the total plants. 20,000 stands × 2 seeds per stand = 40,000 plants.
The plant density is 40,000 plants per hectare. Option A comes from forgetting to multiply by the two seeds per stand.

**5. Sowing small seeds randomly over the surface of a prepared seedbed is a planting pattern known as**
- A. drilling
- B. row spacing
- C. broadcasting
- D. precision planting

**Answer: C.**
Broadcasting is the random scattering of small seeds (like rice or pasture grasses) over the field by hand.

**6. Calculate the total number of stands on a 2-hectare farm if the crop is planted at a spacing of 80 cm by 50 cm.**
- A. 50,000 stands
- B. 25,000 stands
- C. 100,000 stands
- D. 5,000 stands

**Answer: A.**
Step 1: Calculate the total area in square metres. 2 hectares × 10,000 m²/ha = 20,000 m².
Step 2: Convert the spacing to metres. 80 cm = 0.8 m; 50 cm = 0.5 m.
Step 3: Calculate the area per stand. 0.8 m × 0.5 m = 0.4 m².
Step 4: Calculate the total number of stands. 20,000 m² ÷ 0.4 m² = 50,000 stands.
The farm will have 50,000 stands. Option B is the result of mistakenly calculating for only 1 hectare instead of 2.

**7. If crops are planted at a very high density far beyond the optimum level, the most likely result is that**
- A. the plants will produce very large, healthy individual fruits
- B. weeds will grow very aggressively between the plants
- C. the plants will become weak, tall, and spindly due to severe competition
- D. the total marketable yield per hectare will increase significantly

**Answer: C.**
High plant density causes intense competition for light, water, and nutrients, forcing the plants to grow excessively tall and thin (spindly) and significantly reducing the marketable yield. Option A occurs at low densities.

**8. Which cropping system ensures that a piece of land is continuously covered by planting a second crop into a standing first crop shortly before the first is harvested?**
- A. Relay cropping
- B. Crop rotation
- C. Monocropping
- D. Strip cropping

**Answer: A.**
Relay cropping involves planting the second crop into the first crop before it is harvested, ensuring continuous ground cover and overlapping growth.

**9. What is the area occupied by a single plant stand if the crop is spaced at 50 cm by 40 cm?**
- A. 2000 m²
- B. 2.0 m²
- C. 0.02 m²
- D. 0.2 m²

**Answer: D.**
Step 1: Convert the spacing from centimetres to metres. 50 cm = 0.5 m, and 40 cm = 0.4 m.
Step 2: Multiply the values to find the area per stand. 0.5 m × 0.4 m = 0.2 m².
The area occupied by a single stand is 0.2 m². Option A is the common mistake of multiplying 50 by 40 without first converting from centimetres to metres.

**10. A major disadvantage of broadcasting as a planting pattern is that**
- A. it requires very expensive, specialised tractors to perform
- B. it uses very few seeds, leading to a sparse farm
- C. it results in uneven plant density and makes later weeding difficult
- D. it takes too much time to plant compared to row spacing

**Answer: C.**
Broadcasting scatters seeds randomly, causing an uneven plant distribution (too thick in some places, bare in others) which makes later farm operations like weeding and harvesting very difficult because there are no clear paths. It is actually very fast to do, not slow, and requires more seeds (a high seed rate), not fewer.
