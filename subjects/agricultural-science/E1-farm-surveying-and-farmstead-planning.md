## Meaning and importance

**Farm surveying** is the process of measuring and mapping out the position, topography (shape and elevation), size, and boundaries of an area of farmland. It involves taking mathematical measurements of distances, angles, and heights on the land, and then using these measurements to draw an accurate map or plan of the farm. 

### Examine the relevance of farm surveying to agriculture

Surveying is the first step in setting up a modern, commercial farm. Its importance (relevance) includes:

1. **Determining the exact size of the land:** Surveying tells the farmer the exact area of the farm in hectares ($ha$) or square metres ($m^2$). This helps in calculating the amount of seeds, fertilizers, and chemicals needed (for example, knowing the plant density per hectare).
2. **Establishing boundaries:** It marks the exact boundaries of the land, which prevents future boundary disputes with neighbours.
3. **Acquiring legal documents:** In Nigeria, under the Land Use Act, a farmer needs an accurate survey plan signed by a registered surveyor to get a Certificate of Occupancy (C of O) or to use the land as collateral for a bank loan.
4. **Planning the farmstead layout:** A survey map shows the high and low areas of the land, helping the farmer decide the best places to build the farmhouse, animal sheds, and processing units.
5. **Soil and water conservation:** By showing the slope and contours of the land, surveying helps the farmer design terraces, drainage channels, and irrigation systems to prevent soil erosion and manage water.
6. **Determining project feasibility:** It helps determine if the land is suitable for certain projects, such as building a fish pond (which requires a specific slope and elevation).

## Common surveying equipment, their uses and care

Surveyors use a variety of tools depending on the type of measurement required. These range from simple measuring tapes to highly complex optical instruments.

### Classify common surveying equipment

Surveying equipment can be classified based on the type of measurement they take.

| Classification | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Distance measuring instruments** | To measure lengths and distances along the ground | Gunter's chain, measuring tape |
| **Marking and sighting instruments** | To mark points on the ground and create straight lines | Ranging pole, pegs, arrows (pins) |
| **Angular measuring instruments** | To measure horizontal and vertical angles | Prismatic compass, theodolite |
| **Levelling instruments** | To measure differences in height (elevation) and determine land slope | Dumpy level, plumb bob, Abney level |
| **Right-angle instruments** | To set out exact $90^\circ$ angles | Cross staff |

#### 1. Gunter's Chain
The Gunter's chain is an older but very famous tool used to measure horizontal distances. It consists of a series of steel links joined by small rings. 
- **Standard measurements:** A standard Gunter's chain is $66 \text{ feet}$ (exactly $20.12 \text{ m}$) long. It is made up of exactly **$100 \text{ links}$**. 
- Each link is $7.92 \text{ inches}$ ($20.12 \text{ cm}$) long. 
- Brass tags of different shapes are attached at every $10 \text{ links}$ to make it easy to read the distance without counting every single link.
- **Area calculation:** $10 \text{ square chains}$ equal $1 \text{ acre}$.
- **Uses:** Used for measuring distances, especially on rough ground where a tape might break.
- **Care:** Clean dirt off after use; oil the metal joints to prevent rusting; fold it properly to avoid tangles; store in a dry place.

#### 2. Measuring Tape
Tapes are made of steel, coated linen, or fibreglass and usually come in lengths of $30 \text{ m}$, $50 \text{ m}$, or $100 \text{ m}$.
- **Uses:** Used for measuring horizontal distances accurately. It is easier to read than a chain but can snap or stretch.
- **Care:** Wipe clean and dry after use; wind it back into its casing carefully without twisting; oil steel tapes lightly to prevent rust.

#### 3. Ranging Pole
A ranging pole is a long, straight wooden or metal pole, usually $2 \text{ m}$ to $3 \text{ m}$ long, painted with alternating bands of **red and white** (or black and white) so it can be seen from far away. It has a pointed metal end to stick into the soil.
- **Uses:** Used for marking survey stations (points) and for sighting straight lines over long distances.
- **Care:** Clean after use; do not use it to strike objects or dig holes; store horizontally to prevent bending.

#### 4. Arrows (Pins)
These are thin steel pins, about $40 \text{ cm}$ long, with a pointed end and a circular loop at the top. They are often tied with a piece of red cloth so they are not lost in the grass.
- **Uses:** Used along with a chain or tape to mark the end of each chain length during measurement.
- **Care:** Wipe off soil after use; oil lightly to prevent rust; string them together through the loops for storage.

#### 5. Pegs
Short, stout wooden posts with one sharply pointed end and a flat top.
- **Uses:** Driven into the ground with a mallet to permanently mark the corners or stations of a survey.
- **Care:** Keep dry to prevent rotting; do not hit them with metal hammers that can split the wood (use a wooden mallet).

#### 6. Cross Staff
A simple instrument mounted on a short pole, consisting of a wooden or metal head with sighting slits cut at exactly $90^\circ$ to each other.
- **Uses:** Used to set out right angles ($90^\circ$) to the main survey line (for example, when dividing a farm into rectangular plots).
- **Care:** Clean the sighting slits; store safely to prevent the pole from breaking.

#### 7. Prismatic Compass
A small, hand-held compass with a glass prism that allows the user to look at a distant ranging pole and read the bearing (angle relative to Magnetic North) at the same time.
- **Uses:** Used for taking bearings and measuring horizontal angles to determine the direction of farm boundaries.
- **Care:** Handle with great care because the glass and magnetic needle are delicate; keep away from iron objects or electrical fields when in use or storage; store in its leather case.

#### 8. Theodolite
A highly precise, expensive optical instrument mounted on a tripod stand, consisting of a small telescope that can rotate vertically and horizontally.
- **Uses:** Used for measuring both horizontal and vertical angles very accurately over large areas of land. 
- **Care:** Extremely delicate. Must be handled by trained personnel; clean the lenses only with soft lens tissue; pack it securely in its custom hard case and avoid dropping it; keep dry.

#### 9. Dumpy Level
An optical instrument consisting of a telescope fixed exactly horizontally on a tripod, containing a spirit level (bubble). It is used together with a tall, marked ruler called a **levelling staff**.
- **Uses:** Used to measure differences in height (elevation) between different points on the farm, which is essential for planning irrigation, drainage, or fish ponds.
- **Care:** Handle optics carefully; never move it while the locking screws are tight; clean gently and store in its case.

#### 10. Plumb Bob
A solid, heavy piece of metal (usually brass) shaped like a cone, hanging on a string.
- **Uses:** Used to ensure that an instrument (like a theodolite or a cross staff) is set up exactly vertically over a survey peg on the ground.
- **Care:** Keep the string untangled; avoid denting the sharp metal tip.

## Common survey methods

Surveyors choose a method depending on the size of the farm, the accuracy needed, and the instruments available. The approaches range from measuring simple lines to complex angles.

### Differentiate between the common survey methods

| Feature | Chain Surveying | Compass Surveying | Theodolite Surveying | Levelling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Main Principle** | Triangulation | Traversing | Triangulation or Traversing | Determining height differences |
| **Main Instruments** | Chain/tape, ranging pole, pegs | Prismatic compass, tape | Theodolite, tape | Dumpy level, levelling staff |
| **What is measured?** | Only linear distances (no angles) | Distances and horizontal angles | Distances, horizontal and vertical angles | Vertical heights (elevations) |
| **Suitability** | Small, open, flat land | Uneven, bushy, or forested land | Very large areas needing high accuracy | Sloping land requiring drainage/irrigation |
| **Level of Accuracy** | Low to medium | Medium | Very high | Very high |

#### 1. Chain Surveying
Chain surveying is the simplest and oldest method of surveying. It uses only linear measurements (distances) taken with a chain or tape, without measuring angles. 
- **Principle:** The main principle is **triangulation**. The area of land is divided into a network of connected triangles, because a triangle is the only shape that cannot change its form if the lengths of its sides remain fixed. 
- **Application:** A baseline is measured straight across the middle of the land. Other lines are measured from this baseline to the boundaries. 
- **Suitability:** It is best suited for small, relatively flat areas of open ground (like a small vegetable farm). It is very difficult to use in dense forests or hilly terrain.

A line drawn at exactly 90 degrees from the baseline to the boundary is labelled "Offset". Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

#### 2. Compass Surveying
This method uses a prismatic compass to measure the direction (bearings) of the boundary lines and a tape or chain to measure their lengths.
- **Principle:** The main principle is **traversing**. The surveyor moves from one station to the next, recording the distance and the angle at each point to form a connected framework (a traverse).
- **Suitability:** Useful for medium-sized farms, especially where the ground is uneven, heavily forested, or has many obstacles (like a cocoa plantation) where forming large triangles is impossible. 

#### 3. Theodolite Surveying
This method uses a theodolite to measure angles and a tape or electronic distance meter to measure lengths.
- **Principle:** It can use either triangulation or traversing, but it does so with extreme precision. The theodolite measures both horizontal angles (direction) and vertical angles (elevation).
- **Suitability:** Used for very large farm estates, government agricultural projects, and anywhere that requires high accuracy.

#### 4. Levelling
Levelling is not for mapping boundaries; it is specifically for finding the height (elevation) of the land. It uses a dumpy level and a levelling staff.
- **Principle:** The dumpy level establishes a perfectly horizontal line of sight. By reading the numbers on a levelling staff held at different points on the land, the surveyor can calculate which point is higher and by how much.
- **Suitability:** Essential for producing contour maps, planning terracing for soil conservation, laying water pipes, or constructing channels for fadama (irrigation) farming.

## Principles of farmstead planning

A **farmstead** is the area of a farm where the farmer's dwelling house, farm buildings, processing units, storage structures (silos, barns), and machinery sheds are located. It is the headquarters or administrative centre of the farm. 

**Farmstead planning (or layout)** is the careful and logical arrangement of these buildings and facilities on the land to ensure efficiency, safety, health, and profitability. 

### Apply survey principles to farmstead outlay

A surveyor’s map provides the topography and boundaries needed to apply good layout (outlay) principles. A well-planned farmstead uses **zoning** to group similar activities together and separate conflicting ones based on the survey data.

1. **Zoning:** The farm is divided into specific zones based on elevation and soil boundaries mapped out during the survey:
   - **Residential zone:** The farmhouse, located uphill and upwind.
   - **Livestock zone:** Animal sheds, located downwind from the residence.
   - **Machinery zone:** Tractor sheds and workshops, located near the main access road to avoid heavy tractors crossing planted fields.
   - **Crop zone:** Located on the most fertile soils shown on the map.
2. **Spacing to prevent fire:** Buildings must be spaced far enough apart so that if a fire starts in one (like a hay barn or a processing unit), it does not easily spread to the others. 
3. **Minimising land waste:** Farm roads and paths must be mapped out carefully using right angles (with a cross staff) to keep them short and straight. Crooked or overly wide roads waste valuable farmland.
4. **Security and supervision:** The manager's or farmer's house should be placed in a central or elevated position identified during levelling, where they can easily see and supervise workers, machinery, and livestock to prevent theft.

### Identify the factors to be considered in farmstead planning

When deciding exactly where to place the farmstead and its various buildings, a farmer must carefully weigh the following environmental and logistical factors:

1. **Topography (Slope of the land):** 
   The farmhouse and main farm buildings should be located on higher ground. This ensures proper drainage and prevents flooding during the rainy season. However, the slope should not be so steep that it causes soil erosion. Fish ponds, on the other hand, must be located on lower ground where water naturally collects.
2. **Soil type and fertility:** 
   Buildings and roads should be constructed on the poorest, least fertile soils on the farm (like rocky or highly sandy soils). The rich, fertile soils (like loamy soil) must be reserved strictly for planting crops. The soil under buildings must also have a good load-bearing capacity so structures do not sink.
3. **Accessibility:** 
   The farmstead must be easily accessible from the main public road. This makes it easy to bring in heavy inputs (like fertilizer and machinery) and to transport harvested produce (like yam or cassava) to the market without delay. Good farm roads should connect all zones.
4. **Water supply:** 
   A reliable and permanent source of water (like a river, borehole, or well) must be close to the farmstead. Water is essential for the farmer's family, for watering livestock, for mixing chemicals, and for processing crops.
5. **Direction of wind (Very common exam topic):** 
   Livestock buildings (especially poultry houses and pigsties, which can smell bad) must be located **downwind** of the residential farmhouse and the processing areas. This ensures that the wind blows foul odours, dust, and insects *away* from the farmer's living quarters, not towards them.
6. **Direction of the sun (Orientation):** 
   Livestock houses should be oriented (aligned) along an **East-West axis** (the long sides facing North and South). This prevents the direct, harsh morning and evening sunlight from entering the building and overheating the animals.
7. **Health and hygiene:** 
   The farmstead should be located away from swamps or stagnant water to prevent mosquitoes (malaria) and tsetse flies. Waste disposal pits should be situated far from the water source to prevent pollution.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Farm surveying | The measurement and mapping of the position, size, and boundaries of farmland. |
| Gunter's chain | A measuring chain consisting of 100 metal links, measuring exactly 66 feet (20.12 m). |
| Theodolite | An optical instrument used to measure horizontal and vertical angles accurately. |
| Dumpy level | An instrument used for levelling and determining elevation differences on the land. |
| Chain surveying | A survey method using only linear measurements and based on the principle of triangulation. |
| Offset | A horizontal distance measured at a right angle ($90^\circ$) from the main survey baseline to a boundary. |

| Topography | The physical shape of the land surface, including its hills, valleys, and slopes. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Wind direction rule:** Always remember that livestock houses must be located **downwind** from the farmhouse so that bad smells blow away from the humans. Many students confuse upwind and downwind.
- **Sun orientation rule:** Livestock buildings should have an **East-West longitudinal orientation**. This prevents morning and afternoon sun from shining directly in. 
- **Soil allocation:** Never build the farmstead on the most fertile soil. Fertile soil is for crops; buildings go on poorer soils.
- **Gunter's chain measurements:** Memorise the numbers. $1 \text{ chain} = 66 \text{ feet} = 20.12 \text{ m} = 100 \text{ links}$.
- **Hectares conversion:** Remember that $1 \text{ hectare} = 10,000 \text{ m}^2$. If you are given dimensions in metres, calculate the area in square metres first, then divide by $10,000$.
- **Chain surveying principle:** It is based on **triangulation** (making triangles), NOT traversing. Traversing requires measuring angles (compass surveying).
- **Theodolite vs Dumpy Level:** The theodolite measures **angles** (vertical and horizontal); the dumpy level measures **height/elevation differences**. Do not mix them up.

## Quick summary

1. Farm surveying is the measuring and mapping of land to determine its size, boundaries, and topography, which is crucial for getting loans, designing layouts, and calculating inputs.
2. Distance is measured using tapes or Gunter's chains; angles are measured with prismatic compasses or theodolites; elevation is measured with dumpy levels; and lines are marked with ranging poles and pegs.
3. Chain surveying is best for small, flat land and uses triangulation. Compass surveying uses traversing and is better for uneven, bushy areas.
4. Theodolite surveying is used for very large areas needing high accuracy, while levelling determines the slope for drainage and irrigation.
5. A farmstead is the administrative and building centre of the farm.
6. When planning a farmstead layout, buildings should be placed on poor soils and high ground, leaving fertile soil for crops.
7. Animal houses must be located downwind of residential areas to prevent foul odours, and oriented East-West to avoid direct sunlight.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary reason for farm surveying is to**
- A. determine the chemical composition of the soil
- B. determine the exact size and boundaries of the farmland
- C. allocate land to local farmers
- D. calculate the amount of rainfall in the area

**Answer: B.** Farm surveying involves measuring distances and angles to map out the exact boundaries, topography, and size of the land.

**2. Which of the following surveying instruments is used for setting out right angles?**
- A. Prismatic compass
- B. Theodolite
- C. Cross staff
- D. Ranging pole

**Answer: C.** The cross staff is specifically designed with slits at exactly $90^\circ$ to each other to set out right angles (offsets). The prismatic compass measures bearings.

**3. In farmstead planning, livestock buildings should be located**
- A. upwind of the farmhouse
- B. downwind of the farmhouse
- C. on the most fertile soil on the farm
- D. upstream of the water source

**Answer: B.** Livestock houses (like pigsties) should be downwind so the wind blows foul odours away from the residential farmhouse. If they were upwind, the wind would blow smells directly into the house.

**4. A rectangular cassava farm has a length of $500 \text{ m}$ and a width of $200 \text{ m}$. What is the area of the farm in hectares?**
- A. $100 \text{ hectares}$
- B. $1 \text{ hectare}$
- C. $10 \text{ hectares}$
- D. $1000 \text{ hectares}$

**Answer: C.** 
**Step 1:** State the formula for the area of a rectangle.
$Area = length \times width$
where $length$ is the long side of the farm and $width$ is the short side.
**Step 2:** Calculate the area in square metres.
$Area = 500 \text{ m} \times 200 \text{ m} = 100,000 \text{ m}^2$.
**Step 3:** Convert square metres to hectares using the conversion factor ($1 \text{ hectare} = 10,000 \text{ m}^2$).
$100,000 \text{ m}^2 / 10,000 = 10 \text{ hectares}$.
The most common mistake (Option A) is dividing by $1,000$ instead of $10,000$.

**5. The standard length of a Gunter's chain is**
- A. $10.00 \text{ m}$
- B. $66.00 \text{ m}$
- C. $20.12 \text{ m}$
- D. $100.00 \text{ m}$

**Answer: C.** A Gunter's chain is exactly $66 \text{ feet}$ long. Converting to metres ($66 \text{ feet} \times 0.3048 \text{ m/ft}$), it is approximately $20.12 \text{ m}$. Option B comes from confusing feet with metres, and Option D is the number of links, not metres.

**6. The basic principle of chain surveying is**
- A. levelling
- B. traversing
- C. offsetting
- D. triangulation

**Answer: D.** Chain surveying works by dividing the land into a network of triangles (triangulation) because a triangle is a rigid shape whose form cannot change if the side lengths are fixed. Traversing is the principle for compass surveying.

**7. Which of the following is NOT a good practice in the care of a surveying tape?**
- A. Wiping it dry after use
- B. Winding it back carefully without twisting
- C. Leaving it under the sun to dry for several days
- D. Oiling steel tapes lightly

**Answer: C.** Leaving measuring equipment exposed to harsh weather for several days can cause fading, stretching (in plastics/linen), or rust. They should be wiped, dried quickly, and stored in their casing in a cool, dry place.

**8. If a surveyor wants to produce a contour map showing the slopes and valleys for an irrigation project, which instrument is most essential?**
- A. Dumpy level
- B. Gunter's chain
- C. Prismatic compass
- D. Cross staff

**Answer: A.** A dumpy level (used with a levelling staff) measures differences in elevation (height), which is the exact data required to map contours and slopes for irrigation or drainage.

**9. Why is it advised to construct the farmhouse and machinery sheds on the least fertile soil?**
- A. Poor soil provides a stronger foundation for heavy structures
- B. To preserve the fertile soil purely for crop production
- C. Less fertile soil is easier to survey
- D. Pests do not attack buildings on poor soil

**Answer: B.** Fertile soil is a limited and highly valuable resource. Buildings do not need nutrients, so they should be placed on poorer, rocky, or sandy soils to reserve the rich, fertile loamy soil for planting crops.

**10. A farmer wants to determine the area of a triangular plot of land. The baseline (base) of the triangle is $400 \text{ m}$ and the perpendicular offset (height) is $250 \text{ m}$. What is the area of the land in hectares?**
- A. $10 \text{ hectares}$
- B. $5 \text{ hectares}$
- C. $50,000 \text{ hectares}$
- D. $2 \text{ hectares}$

**Answer: B.** 
**Step 1:** State the formula for the area of a triangle.
$Area = \frac{1}{2} \times base \times height$
where $base$ is the baseline length and $height$ is the perpendicular offset.
**Step 2:** Calculate the area in square metres.
$Area = \frac{1}{2} \times 400 \text{ m} \times 250 \text{ m} = 200 \times 250 = 50,000 \text{ m}^2$.
**Step 3:** Convert to hectares ($1 \text{ hectare} = 10,000 \text{ m}^2$).
$50,000 \text{ m}^2 / 10,000 = 5 \text{ hectares}$.
Option A comes from forgetting to multiply by $\frac{1}{2}$ in the triangle formula (calculating it as a rectangle). Option C comes from forgetting to convert square metres to hectares.
