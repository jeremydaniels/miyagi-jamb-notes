# JAMB Agricultural Science Notes: Processing and Storage

## What JAMB expects

This topic is part of Section E (Agricultural Technology) of the JAMB syllabus. It covers the ways agricultural produce is handled after harvest to convert it into usable forms, as well as the facilities and methods used to keep it safe for future use.

- **b. Storage**

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

## Importance of Agricultural Processing

**Objective: identify the importance of agricultural processing.**

**Agricultural processing** is the series of actions that change agricultural produce from its raw, harvested state into a more usable, storable, or consumable form. Most crops cannot be eaten or stored effectively straight from the field. 

The main reasons for processing agricultural produce include:

1. **Extending shelf life and preventing spoilage:** Fresh produce contains a lot of moisture, which encourages the growth of bacteria and fungi. Processing methods like drying, smoking, or converting into flour remove moisture, allowing the food to be kept for much longer.
2. **Removing toxins:** Some crops contain naturally occurring poisons. For example, fresh bitter cassava contains cyanogenic glycosides, which can release deadly hydrogen cyanide. Processing it into gari safely removes these toxins.
3. **Improving taste, flavour, and appearance:** Processing makes food more appetizing and digestible. Examples include roasting groundnuts or fermenting locust beans to make *iru* or *dawadawa*.
4. **Reducing bulkiness:** Raw crops can be heavy and take up a lot of space. Processing reduces their weight and volume, making them much easier and cheaper to transport. For instance, milling paddy rice removes the bulky husks.
5. **Adding economic value:** Processed goods sell for higher prices than raw agricultural materials. A farmer earns more selling milled rice or gari than selling raw paddy or fresh cassava tubers.
6. **Creating employment:** Processing industries (like rice mills, gari processing factories, and oil extraction plants) provide jobs for millions of Nigerians.
7. **Providing raw materials for industry:** Processing extracts useful components needed by other industries, such as extracting oil from palm fruits for soap making.

## Differentiating Methods of Processing

**Objective: differentiate between the various methods of processing agricultural produce.**

Processing methods in West Africa are generally divided into two categories: **traditional methods** (often done at the village or household level) and **modern industrial methods**. 

The syllabus requires you to differentiate between them. The table below outlines their main differences:

| Feature | Traditional Processing | Modern Processing |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scale of production** | Small scale (subsistence or local market). | Large scale (commercial or export market). |
| **Tools and equipment** | Simple, manually operated tools (e.g., mortar and pestle, grinding stone, calabash, manual graters). | Complex, mechanized, or automated machines (e.g., hydraulic presses, milling machines, destoners). |
| **Source of power** | Human labour, sometimes animal power. | Electricity, fossil fuels (petrol/diesel generators), and large engines. |
| **Time and labour** | Highly labour-intensive and time-consuming. | Very fast and requires less manual labour per unit produced. |
| **Quality of product** | Quality varies from batch to batch. May contain impurities (e.g., stones in traditional rice, uneven frying in gari). | Consistent, high-quality products. Impurities are effectively removed. |
| **Capital requirement** | Low capital investment; tools are cheap and locally made. | High capital investment required to buy, run, and maintain machinery. |
| **Drying methods** | Relies heavily on the weather (sun drying), which is impossible during heavy rains. | Uses artificial drying (ovens, kilns, mechanical dryers), allowing continuous work regardless of the weather. |

## Traditional and Modern Processing of Specific Crops

**Content item a: Processing: traditional and modern methods of food processing e.g. gari, rice and groundnut processing, etc.**

JAMB expects you to know the step-by-step processes for converting common West African crops into their final products, contrasting how it is done traditionally versus in a modern setting.

### 1. Processing Cassava into Gari

Fresh cassava tubers spoil within 2 to 3 days of harvesting. Processing them into gari is the most common way to preserve them and make them safe to eat.

**Step-by-step process:**
1. **Peeling:** The thick brown and inner white rind of the cassava tuber is removed.
2. **Washing:** The peeled tubers are washed in clean water to remove dirt and sand.
3. **Grating:** The tubers are crushed into a fine mash. This breaks the plant cells and exposes them to fermentation.
4. **Fermentation and Pressing (Dewatering):** The mash is packed into a porous bag (like a jute or polypropylene sack). Heavy weights are placed on it for **2 to 4 days**. 
   - *Why is this important?* Lactic acid bacteria ferment the mash, giving gari its unique sour taste. More importantly, this step breaks down the toxic cyanogenic glycosides, and the poisonous hydrogen cyanide escapes as a gas, making the gari safe for human consumption.
5. **Sieving:** The pressed, dewatered solid cake is broken apart and rubbed through a sieve to remove coarse fibres and ungrated lumps.
6. **Roasting (Garrifying):** The sieved mash is placed in a wide, shallow pan over a fire and stirred constantly until it is dry and crisp. The heat drives off remaining moisture (down to about 10%) and eliminates any leftover cyanide. Palm oil may be added to produce yellow gari.
7. **Cooling and Bagging:** The hot gari is spread out to cool before being packaged for storage.

**Traditional vs Modern methods:**
- *Traditional:* Peeling by hand with knives; pressing by placing heavy stones or wooden logs on the sacks; roasting in cast-iron pots over a smoky wood fire, which is tedious and hurts the eyes.
- *Modern:* Mechanical peeling machines (though hand peeling is still common); motorized mechanical graters; hydraulic jacks or screw presses for fast dewatering; mechanized fryers with automated paddles to stir the gari.

### 2. Processing Rice

Harvested rice is known as **paddy**. Paddy has a tough, inedible outer husk (hull) that must be removed.

**Step-by-step process:**
1. **Threshing:** Beating the harvested rice panicles to separate the paddy grains from the stalks.
2. **Winnowing:** Throwing the grains into the air so the wind blows away the lighter chaff and empty husks, leaving the heavier grain behind.
3. **Parboiling:** Soaking the paddy in hot water and then steaming it. 
   - *Why is this important?* Parboiling hardens the starch inside the grain, which heavily reduces breakage during milling. It also loosens the husk and drives valuable B vitamins and minerals from the outer bran layer deep into the starchy endosperm, making the rice much more nutritious.
4. **Drying:** Sun-drying the parboiled paddy until the moisture content drops to a safe level (usually 12–14%).
5. **Hulling / Milling:** Removing the tough outer husk to produce brown rice. 
6. **Polishing:** Scraping off the brown bran layer to produce white rice.
7. **Destoning and Grading:** Removing small stones, sand, and broken grains before bagging.

**Traditional vs Modern methods:**
- *Traditional:* Threshing by beating against drums; parboiling in small clay pots; milling by pounding in a heavy wooden mortar with a pestle, which causes a lot of the grains to break. Sorting out stones is done by hand.
- *Modern:* Use of combine harvesters that harvest and thresh simultaneously; large commercial steamers for parboiling; mechanical hullers and polishers; automated **destoning machines** that use gravity to separate stones from rice.

### 3. Processing Groundnut (for Oil Extraction)

Groundnuts (peanuts) are widely processed in Nigeria to extract cooking oil, leaving a protein-rich residue.

**Step-by-step process:**
1. **Decorticating (Shelling):** Removing the outer pod to extract the kernels (seeds).
2. **Roasting:** Heating the kernels slightly. This helps to rupture the oil-bearing cells and improves the flavour of the oil.
3. **De-skinning:** Rubbing the roasted nuts to remove the thin red seed coat (testa), followed by winnowing.
4. **Milling (Crushing):** Grinding the roasted kernels into a smooth paste.
5. **Kneading and Pressing (Extraction):** Warm water is added to the paste, and it is kneaded vigorously. The oil separates and is squeezed out. 
6. **Frying the Residue:** The leftover paste (which still contains a little oil and a lot of protein) is shaped and fried in the extracted oil to produce groundnut cake, famously known in Nigeria as **kuli-kuli**.

**Traditional vs Modern methods:**
- *Traditional:* Shelling by hand; milling on a flat grinding stone; manual kneading in a mortar or large bowl to squeeze out the oil.
- *Modern:* Mechanical decorticators; motorized grinding machines; mechanical **screw presses (expellers)** that apply immense pressure to squeeze out almost all the oil instantly.

## What is Farm Storage?

**Content item b: Storage**

**Storage** is the keeping of harvested agricultural produce safely until it is needed for consumption, sale, or as planting material (seeds) for the next season. 

Storage bridges the gap between the harvest season (when food is plentiful) and the lean season. It stabilizes market prices by preventing food from flooding the market all at once, ensures food security, and protects crops from pests (like weevils and rats), diseases, and bad weather.

---

## Identifying and Comparing Storage Methods

**Objective: Identify and compare different storage methods.**

Storage methods, just like processing, can be divided into traditional and modern facilities.

### Traditional Storage Facilities

1. **Yam Barn:** 
   Used for storing fresh yam tubers. It consists of a vertical framework made of growing wooden poles (called **live poles**) and horizontal wooden crossbars. Yams are tied individually to the framework using ropes.
   - *Why live poles?* Live trees do not rot, and termites cannot eat them. Their leaves also provide a canopy that shades the yams from direct sunlight, reducing heat and rotting. The open structure allows maximum air circulation (ventilation).
2. **Maize Crib:**
   A rectangular structure built on stilt legs about 1 metre off the ground, used for storing maize on the cob. The walls are made of wire mesh, bamboo, or palm fronds to allow the wind to blow through and dry the maize. 
   - *Crucial feature:* The stilt legs are fitted with downward-pointing metal cones or discs called **rat guards**, which prevent rodents from climbing up into the crib.
3. **Rhombus (Rumbu):**
   A traditional granary widely used in the drier Northern parts of Nigeria (Sudan and Sahel savannas). It is an earthen or mud bin with a thatched roof, used for storing unthreshed grains like sorghum and millet. The mud walls keep the inside cool.
4. **Underground Pits:**
   Pits dug into the earth and lined with dry grass or leaves. They are used in some cultures for the emergency concealment of grains or for short-term storage of yams.
5. **Fireplace Storage:**
   Tying produce (like seed maize, onions, or dried meat) above the kitchen fireplace. The heat and smoke dry the produce continuously and repel insects.

### Modern Storage Facilities

1. **Silos:**
   Tall, large, cylindrical structures made of aluminium, galvanized steel, or concrete. They are used for storing bulk amounts of threshed, dried grains (maize, rice, cowpea). 
   - *Features:* They are highly mechanized, airtight, and equipped with sensors to monitor temperature and moisture. Because they can be sealed tightly, it is easy to introduce chemical fumigants to kill pests like weevils. Grains must be dried properly (12–14% moisture) before going into a silo to prevent fungal rot.
2. **Cold Storage (Refrigeration / Cold Rooms):**
   Facilities where the temperature is artificially lowered to near or below freezing. Cold slows down the action of enzymes and stops the growth of spoilage bacteria and fungi.
3. **Bagging and Warehouses:**
   Clean, dried grains or processed foods (like flour and gari) are packed into jute or polypropylene bags. These bags are stacked on wooden platforms (pallets) inside a large, dry, well-ventilated building called a warehouse. Pallets keep the bags off the cold, damp floor.
4. **Hermetic Storage (e.g., PICS bags):**
   A modern, chemical-free technology. Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags use three layers of thick plastic. Once sealed, the crop uses up the remaining oxygen, and any insects inside quickly suffocate and die.
5. **Canning and Bottling:**
   Food is heated to kill all microorganisms and then sealed in airtight metal cans or glass jars.

### Comparison of Traditional vs Modern Storage

| Feature | Traditional Storage (e.g., Crib, Barn, Rhombus) | Modern Storage (e.g., Silo, Cold Room) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Materials used** | Local, cheap materials (wood, mud, thatch, bamboo). | Expensive, industrial materials (steel, concrete, plastics). |
| **Capacity** | Small capacity, suited for individual peasant farmers. | Massive capacity, suited for commercial farms or national reserves. |
| **Pest control** | Relies on natural methods (smoke, rat guards) which can easily fail. High losses to pests. | Uses fumigation, hermetic sealing, and temperature control. Low losses to pests. |
| **Duration of storage** | Usually short to medium term (a few months). | Long term (can store crops safely for years). |
| **Moisture control** | Depends entirely on ambient weather conditions. | Environment can be artificially controlled (aeration fans, heaters). |

## Applying Appropriate Storage Methods to Different Crops

**Objective: apply appropriate storage methods to different crops.**

Different crops have different moisture contents and physical properties, meaning a storage method that works perfectly for one crop might destroy another. 

| Crop Type | Specific Examples | Best Storage Methods |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cereals (Grains)** | Maize, Rice, Sorghum, Millet | - **Maize on the cob:** Maize crib.<br>- **Bulk dried grains:** Silos, bagging in warehouses.<br>- **Unthreshed sorghum/millet:** Rhombus (in dry savannas). |
| **Legumes / Pulses** | Cowpea (Beans), Groundnut, Soybean | - **Dried seeds:** Hermetic bags (PICS bags) are excellent for cowpeas to stop weevils.<br>- **Bulk storage:** Silos (often requiring fumigation with chemicals like Phostoxin).<br>- **Small scale:** Air-tight drums. |
| **Roots and Tubers** | Yam, Cassava, Sweet potato | - **Yam:** Yam barn (best for ventilation), underground pits.<br>- **Cassava:** Cannot be stored fresh for long. It is either left unharvested in the ground (field storage) until needed, or processed immediately into gari or cassava flour, which can then be bagged. |
| **Highly Perishable Vegetables & Fruits** | Tomatoes, Pepper, Onions, Oranges, Plantain | - **Fresh:** Cold storage (refrigerators, cold rooms) to prevent rapid rotting.<br>- **Processed:** Canning, or sun-drying (e.g., dried pepper or okra). |
| **Animal Products** | Meat, Fish, Milk, Eggs | - **Fresh:** Deep freezers, cold rooms.<br>- **Processed:** Smoking, salting, sun-drying, or canning. |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Agricultural Processing** | Converting raw agricultural produce into a more usable, storable, or consumable form. |
| **Parboiling** | Soaking and steaming rice in its husk before milling to harden it and improve nutritional value. |
| **Decortication** | The process of removing the outer shell or pod of a crop, such as shelling groundnuts. |
| **Fermentation (in gari)** | The action of microorganisms breaking down sugars, which provides a sour taste and removes toxic cyanide. |
| **Live poles** | Living trees used as structural supports in a yam barn to prevent termite attack and provide shade. |
| **Rat guard** | A downward-pointing metal cone placed on the legs of a maize crib to stop rodents from climbing up. |
| **Silo** | A large, tall cylindrical structure made of metal or concrete used for storing bulk dried grains. |
| **Rhombus (Rumbu)** | A traditional mud or earthen granary used in Northern Nigeria for storing grains. |
| **Hermetic storage** | Storage in completely airtight bags (like PICS bags) where insects suffocate due to lack of oxygen. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse threshing with milling.** Threshing separates the grain from the plant stalk; milling (or hulling) removes the tight outer husk from the grain itself.
- **Why do we parboil rice?** Remember that parboiling is done *before* milling. It hardens the grain to stop it breaking in the mill, and it drives vitamins from the bran into the endosperm. It does *not* fully cook the rice.
- **Where are the rat guards?** In an exam, if asked where rat guards are placed, the correct answer is on the **legs (stilts)** of the maize crib, not on the walls or the roof.
- **Removing cyanide from cassava:** The fermentation and pressing stage is what primarily breaks down the cyanogenic glycosides in cassava. The roasting (garrifying) stage drives off the remaining poisonous gas.
- **What is the function of live poles?** Live poles in a yam barn provide shade to keep tubers cool and prevent the wooden framework from being eaten by termites. They are not meant to grow new yams.
- **Moisture in Silos:** Grains must be thoroughly dried (usually around 12–14% moisture) before being put into a silo. Wet grains in a silo will quickly heat up, mould, and rot.
- **Processing reduces bulk:** A common mistake is thinking processing increases bulk. It actually reduces it, making transport easier and cheaper.

## Quick summary

1. Processing changes raw agricultural produce into forms that are easier to eat, transport, and store, while also removing natural toxins and adding value.
2. Traditional processing relies on manual labour and simple tools (mortars, grinding stones), while modern processing uses machines, requires high capital, and operates on a large scale.
3. Gari processing involves peeling, washing, grating, fermenting and pressing (to remove toxic cyanide), sieving, and roasting.
4. Rice processing involves threshing, winnowing, parboiling (to harden the grain and improve nutrition), drying, milling, polishing, and destoning.
5. Traditional storage facilities include yam barns (using live poles for shade and termite resistance), maize cribs (using rat guards on legs), and the rhombus (earthen bins for northern grains).
6. Modern storage facilities include silos (for bulk dried grains), cold rooms (for perishables), and hermetic PICS bags (which suffocate pests).
7. Different crops require specific storage: yams need the ventilation of a barn; fresh tomatoes need cold storage; bulk dried maize needs a silo; and cassava must be processed or left in the soil.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary purpose of adding rat guards to a maize crib is to**
- A. strengthen the wooden legs of the crib
- B. prevent rodents from climbing into the stored crops
- C. protect the maize from insect pests like weevils
- D. collect rainwater running off the roof

**Answer: B.** Rat guards are downward-pointing metal cones fitted on the legs of the crib. Their smooth, sloping surface makes it impossible for rodents (rats and mice) to climb up into the stored maize.

**2. Which of the following traditional storage structures is most commonly used for storing unthreshed sorghum and millet in Northern Nigeria?**
- A. Yam barn
- B. Maize crib
- C. Silo
- D. Rhombus

**Answer: D.** The rhombus (or rumbu) is a traditional mud/earthen granary with a thatched roof, widely used in the dry savannas of Northern Nigeria for grain storage.

**3. The process of soaking, steaming, and drying paddy rice before milling is known as**
- A. threshing
- B. winnowing
- C. parboiling
- D. polishing

**Answer: C.** Parboiling hardens the starch in the grain to reduce breakage during milling and drives vitamins into the endosperm. A common mistake is choosing polishing, which is the removal of the bran layer after milling.

**4. During the processing of cassava into gari, the grated mash is pressed and left for a few days mainly to**
- A. increase the starch content
- B. allow fermentation and the removal of poisonous cyanide
- C. improve the colour of the gari
- D. kill insect pests

**Answer: B.** Fermentation gives gari its characteristic sour taste and breaks down cyanogenic glycosides, allowing the toxic hydrogen cyanide gas to escape.

**5. Which of the following crops is best stored fresh in a barn constructed with live poles?**
- A. Cassava
- B. Yam
- C. Sweet potato
- D. Maize

**Answer: B.** Fresh yam tubers are traditionally stored in a yam barn because they require maximum ventilation to prevent rotting. The live poles provide necessary shade. Cassava cannot be stored fresh for long and must be processed.

**6. A modern method of storing bulk dried grains that allows for easy temperature control and chemical fumigation is the use of**
- A. a maize crib
- B. jute bags
- C. an underground pit
- D. a silo

**Answer: D.** Silos are large, airtight modern structures used to store bulk grains. Because they can be sealed, it is easy to introduce fumigants to kill pests.

**7. Which of the following is NOT an advantage of processing agricultural produce?**
- A. It increases the bulkiness of the produce for easy transport
- B. It extends the shelf life of the produce
- C. It improves the taste and flavour
- D. It increases the market value of the crop

**Answer: A.** Processing *reduces* bulkiness by removing unwanted parts (like water, husks, and shells). This makes the produce lighter, less bulky, and cheaper to transport.

**8. In groundnut processing, after the oil has been extracted from the crushed paste, the protein-rich residue is commonly fried to make**
- A. groundnut soup
- B. roasted groundnut
- C. groundnut cake (kuli-kuli)
- D. groundnut butter

**Answer: C.** The residue left over after kneading and pressing out the oil is shaped and fried to produce groundnut cake, locally known as kuli-kuli.

**9. The most appropriate method for storing freshly harvested tomatoes to prevent rapid spoilage is**
- A. storing in a silo
- B. tying them to live poles
- C. refrigeration or cold storage
- D. storing in a rhombus

**Answer: C.** Tomatoes are highly perishable vegetables with high moisture content. Cold storage (refrigeration) slows down enzymatic action and microbial growth, keeping them fresh longer.

**10. A major difference between traditional and modern agricultural processing methods is that modern methods**
- A. rely entirely on natural fermentation
- B. are cheaper and use locally available materials
- C. result in a lower quality of finished products
- D. use mechanical equipment and operate on a large scale

**Answer: D.** Modern processing uses automated, high-capital machinery to process goods rapidly on a commercial scale, resulting in consistent, high-quality products. Traditional methods use simple tools and are small scale.
