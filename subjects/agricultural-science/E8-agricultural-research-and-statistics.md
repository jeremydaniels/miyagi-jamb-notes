## Concept of planning agricultural experiments

**Agricultural research** is the systematic, scientific investigation of farming problems. Its primary goal is to discover new facts, improve farming methods, and increase both the yield and quality of agricultural produce. For example, a researcher might want to know which variety of cowpea grows best in the Sudan savanna or which feed combination makes pigs gain weight fastest. 

To find out, researchers set up an **agricultural experiment**. Planning a good experiment requires understanding several key concepts, which form the foundation of experimental design.

### 1. Hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is an educated guess or a proposed explanation that can be tested through an experiment. Before starting any trial, the researcher states two types of hypotheses:

| Feature | Null Hypothesis ($H_0$) | Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$ or $H_A$) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | States that there is **no difference**, no effect, or no relationship between the treatments being tested. | States that there **is a significant difference**, effect, or relationship. |
| **Role in experiment** | This is the default position. The researcher analyses the data to see if there is enough evidence to reject this assumption. | This is usually the theory the researcher believes is true and hopes to prove with the experiment. |
| **Example** | Applying poultry manure **does not** change the yield of cassava compared to no manure. | Applying poultry manure **increases** the yield of cassava compared to no manure. |

### 2. Treatment and Control

- **Treatment:** A treatment is the specific condition, substance, practice or factor being applied to the subjects of the experiment to test its effect. If you are testing fertilizers, the treatments might be "50 kg per ha of NPK", "100 kg per ha of NPK", and "organic compost". 
- **Control (or Control group):** This is the standard used for comparison. It is a plot or group of animals that is kept under normal conditions and receives **no treatment** (or receives the standard old treatment). Without a control group, you cannot prove that your new treatment actually caused the changes you see. For example, a "zero fertilizer" plot is the control that shows how much the crop would have yielded naturally.

### 3. Variables

A variable is any characteristic or quantity that can be measured and can change in an experiment.

| Type of Variable | Meaning | Example in a poultry feeding trial |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Independent Variable** | The factor that the researcher deliberately changes or controls (the cause or treatment). | The percentage of fish meal in the feed. |
| **Dependent Variable** | The outcome that is measured to see how it responds to the treatment (the effect). | The weight gain of the broilers (in kg). |
| **Controlled (Constant) Variables** | The factors kept exactly the same for all groups so they do not ruin the experiment. | The age, breed, and sex of the birds, the amount of water given, the temperature of the pen. |

## Application of basic concepts in field experiments

**Objective: use basic concepts in agricultural experiments.**

To actually carry out a valid experiment, researchers must apply basic experimental design concepts on the farm. Three of the most important practical concepts used to ensure an experiment is fair and accurate are **randomization**, **replication**, and **local control (blocking)**.

### 1. Randomization

**Randomization** is the process of assigning treatments to experimental plots or animals purely by chance (like picking numbers from a hat or using a random number generator). It ensures that every plot or animal has an equal chance of receiving any treatment. 

**Why it matters:** It removes human bias. If a farmer subconsciously puts the new experimental seed in the most fertile, well-watered soil, the seed will perform well simply because of the soil, not because the seed is genetically better. Randomization prevents this unfair advantage and helps spread unknown variations evenly across all treatments.

### 2. Replication

**Replication** means repeating the same treatment on several different plots or subjects. Instead of giving a new drug to just one cow, the researcher gives it to ten cows. Instead of planting a new maize variety in one plot, the researcher plants it in four separate plots.

**Why it matters:** 
- It improves the reliability of the results. 
- It helps reduce experimental error. If one plant dies from a random insect attack, having many other plants with the same treatment means the experiment is not ruined.
- It allows the researcher to calculate an average (mean) performance for the treatment.

### 3. Local Control (Blocking)

**Local control** or **blocking** is used when the experimental area is not uniform. For example, a field might slope downwards, meaning the soil at the bottom is wetter and more fertile than the soil at the top. 

To handle this, researchers divide the field into "blocks" (sections) where the conditions within each block are uniform. Every treatment is then applied once inside every block (a design known as Randomized Complete Block Design, or RCBD). This ensures that the soil fertility gradient does not secretly ruin the experiment.

## Understanding and interpreting experimental results

Once an experiment is finished, the researcher collects all the data (such as the yields of all the plots, the heights of the plants, or the weights of the animals). Before making final decisions, the researcher must interpret what the numbers mean. An essential part of this interpretation is understanding experimental error.

### Experimental Error

In everyday language, an "error" is a mistake (like forgetting to water the plants, reading a scale incorrectly, or writing down 50 kg instead of 5 kg). In science, those are called **human errors** or mistakes.

**Experimental error** is entirely different. It is the natural, unavoidable variation among living subjects that received the exact same treatment. 

For example, if you plant two identical yam setts in the exact same soil, give them the exact same fertilizer, and harvest them on the same day, one might yield 2.5 kg and the other 2.7 kg. This tiny difference might be due to a slight difference in sunlight, a hidden root pest, or tiny genetic differences in the setts. This natural variation is the experimental error. 

A good experiment tries to **reduce experimental error** as much as possible by:
1. Using a large number of replicates (more plants or animals).
2. Choosing uniform experimental material (e.g., animals of the same breed, age, and starting weight).
3. Using strict controlled variables (giving exactly the same amount of feed and water).
4. Using appropriate blocking if the field is not uniform.

## How to draw inferences from experimental results

**Objective i: draw inferences from experimental results.**

To **draw an inference** means to look at the data and make a logical conclusion based on statistics. Did the treatment actually work, or was the difference just due to chance (experimental error)?

Researchers use statistical tests to compare the differences between the treatments against the experimental error.

- **Statistically Significant (Reject the Null Hypothesis):** If the difference in yield between the treatment and the control is very large and consistent across replicates, the statistical test will show it is "significant". The inference is that the treatment genuinely caused the effect. The researcher **rejects the null hypothesis** and accepts the alternative hypothesis.
- **Not Statistically Significant (Accept the Null Hypothesis):** If the difference is very small and falls within the normal range of experimental error (chance), it is "not significant". The inference is that the treatment had no real effect. The researcher **accepts the null hypothesis**.

**Example of an inference:**
If a new organic fertilizer produces a mean maize yield of 4.2 tonnes per ha, and the control (no fertilizer) produces 4.0 tonnes per ha, a statistical test might show this small difference is not statistically significant. The inference drawn is that the new organic fertilizer does not significantly improve maize yield compared to no fertilizer.

## Methods to compute simple measures of central tendency

**Objective ii: compute simple measures of central tendency.**

When researchers collect data from many replicates, they end up with a long list of numbers. To interpret these numbers, they calculate a single value that represents the "center" or typical value of the group. These are called **measures of central tendency**. The three you must know how to compute for JAMB are the mean, the median, and the mode.

### 1. The Mean (Arithmetic Mean)

The mean is the mathematical average of all the values in a data set. It is the most commonly used measure in agricultural statistics because it uses every single number in the data.

**Formula:**
$$ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n} $$

Where:
- $\bar{x}$ = the mean
- $\sum x$ = the sum of all the values
- $n$ = the total number of items or observations

### 2. The Median

The median is the exact middle value when the data is arranged in order of size (sorted from smallest to largest). It is very useful when there is an extreme outlier (like one plant that grew unusually tall), because the median is not easily affected by extreme values.

- **If the number of items ($n$) is odd:** The median is the single middle number.
- **If the number of items ($n$) is even:** There is no single middle number. The median is calculated by finding the average of the **two** middle numbers.

**Crucial rule:** You MUST sort the numbers before finding the median. If you pick the middle number of an unsorted list, your answer will be wrong.

### 3. The Mode

The mode is the value that appears most frequently (most often) in the data set. 
- A data set can have one mode (unimodal), two modes (bimodal, if two numbers tie for the highest frequency), or no mode at all (if every number appears only once).
- The mode is especially useful for descriptive traits (e.g., the most common flower colour) or discrete numbers (e.g., the most common litter size in pigs).

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Ungrouped data with an odd number of items**
A researcher recorded the yield of cowpea (in tonnes per ha) from 5 different plots as follows: 10, 14, 12, 22, 12. Calculate the mean, median, and mode.

**Step 1: Calculate the Mean**
Formula: $\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n}$
Sum of values ($\sum x$) = $10 + 14 + 12 + 22 + 12 = 70$ tonnes per ha
Number of plots ($n$) = 5
Mean = $\frac{70}{5} = 14$ tonnes per ha.

**Step 2: Calculate the Median**
First, arrange the data in order from smallest to largest: 10, 12, **12**, 14, 22.
Since there are 5 items (an odd number), the median is the 3rd number.
Median = 12 tonnes per ha.

**Step 3: Calculate the Mode**
Look at the data: 10, 14, 12, 22, 12.
The number 12 appears twice, while all other numbers appear only once.
Mode = 12 tonnes per ha.

**Example 2: Ungrouped data with an even number of items**
The weight gain of 6 broilers over a week (in kg) was recorded as: 2.1, 1.8, 2.5, 2.0, 1.9, 2.3. Find the median weight gain.

**Step 1: Sort the data**
Smallest to largest: 1.8, 1.9, **2.0, 2.1**, 2.3, 2.5.

**Step 2: Find the middle values**
Since $n = 6$ (an even number), the two middle numbers are the 3rd and 4th values: 2.0 and 2.1.

**Step 3: Average the two middle values**
Median = $\frac{2.0 + 2.1}{2} = \frac{4.1}{2} = 2.05$ kg.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Agricultural research** | The systematic scientific study of farming problems to improve yield and quality. |
| **Hypothesis** | A testable guess or proposed explanation in an experiment. |
| **Null hypothesis** | The assumption that a treatment has no effect or creates no difference. |
| **Alternative hypothesis** | The assumption that a treatment has a significant effect or creates a difference. |
| **Treatment** | The specific condition or factor being applied and tested (e.g., a new fertilizer). |
| **Control** | The standard group that receives no new treatment, used for comparison. |
| **Independent variable** | The factor the researcher actively changes (the cause). |
| **Dependent variable** | The outcome that is measured (the effect). |
| **Randomization** | Assigning treatments purely by chance to remove bias. |
| **Replication** | Repeating a treatment on multiple subjects to ensure reliability. |
| **Local control (Blocking)** | Grouping experimental units into uniform blocks to account for field variations (like a soil slope). |
| **Experimental error** | The natural, unavoidable variation among subjects receiving the same treatment. |
| **Inference** | A logical conclusion drawn from experimental data and statistical tests. |
| **Statistical significance** | A result large enough that it is likely caused by the treatment, not by chance. |
| **Central tendency** | A single value that represents the middle or typical value of a data set. |
| **Mean** | The mathematical average of a set of numbers. |
| **Median** | The middle value of a sorted data set. |
| **Mode** | The most frequently occurring value in a data set. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Experimental error is not a mistake:** A very common JAMB trick is to offer an option that says experimental error means "the researcher weighed the fertilizer wrong." This is false. It is the natural, unavoidable variation between living things. Human mistakes are called human errors.
- **Sorting is compulsory for the median:** If you calculate the median without sorting the numbers from smallest to largest first, you will pick the wrong number.
- **Treatment vs Control:** The treatment is what is new or changed; the control is the normal standard where nothing is changed.
- **Null vs Alternative:** The null hypothesis always says there is "no difference." The alternative says there "is a difference."
- **Check the unit:** Always include units (kg, tonnes per ha, etc.) in your calculations and final answers.

## Quick summary

1. Agricultural research uses controlled, scientific experiments to solve farming problems and improve yields.
2. A hypothesis is a testable guess. The null hypothesis assumes no difference, while the alternative hypothesis assumes a significant difference.
3. Treatments are the specific conditions applied to the subjects, while the control is a standard group that receives no new treatment.
4. Randomization removes human bias, blocking controls field variations, and replication increases reliability by repeating the treatments on multiple subjects.
5. Experimental error is the natural, unavoidable variation among subjects receiving the same treatment; it is not a human mistake.
6. Researchers draw inferences by using statistical tests to see if differences are significant enough to reject the null hypothesis.
7. Measures of central tendency (mean, median, and mode) summarize complex experimental data into a single typical value.
8. The mean is the mathematical average, the median is the exact middle value of sorted data, and the mode is the most frequently occurring value.

## Practice questions

**1. In an agricultural experiment, a statement that there is no difference between two treatments is known as the**

- A. alternative hypothesis
- B. null hypothesis
- C. experimental error
- D. dependent variable

**Answer: B.** The null hypothesis is the default position that assumes the treatment has no effect and creates no significant difference.

**2. A researcher wants to test the effect of a new pesticide on cowpea pests. The group of cowpea plants that is NOT sprayed with the pesticide serves as the**

- A. treatment
- B. replicate
- C. variable
- D. control

**Answer: D.** The control (or control group) is the standard that receives no treatment, allowing the researcher to see what would happen naturally for comparison.

**3. The main reason for randomizing the assignment of treatments to plots in a field experiment is to**

- A. increase the experimental error
- B. ensure all plots yield the same amount
- C. remove bias from the experiment
- D. make the calculation of the mean easier

**Answer: C.** Randomization assigns treatments purely by chance, preventing the researcher from subconsciously putting a favored treatment on the best soil, thus removing bias.

**4. In statistics, natural variation in yield between two crop plots that received the exact same fertilizer treatment is referred to as**

- A. human error
- B. experimental error
- C. treatment effect
- D. significant difference

**Answer: B.** Experimental error is the unavoidable, natural variation between biological subjects receiving the same treatment. The most common mistake is confusing this with "human error" (a mistake made by the researcher).

**5. A farmer tests how different watering rates affect the height of tomato plants. In this experiment, the height of the tomato plants is the**

- A. independent variable
- B. dependent variable
- C. constant variable
- D. treatment

**Answer: B.** The dependent variable is the outcome being measured (the effect). The independent variable is the watering rate (the cause or treatment).

**6. The yields of yam from 5 mounds (in kg) are 10, 14, 12, 22, and 12. Calculate the mean yield.**

- A. 12 kg
- B. 14 kg
- C. 17.5 kg
- D. 70 kg

**Answer: B.** 
Step 1: Find the sum of all values: $10 + 14 + 12 + 22 + 12 = 70$. 
Step 2: Divide by the number of mounds ($n = 5$): $70 / 5 = 14$ kg. 
(Option A is the median or mode. Option C comes from wrongly dividing by 4. Option D is just the sum).

**7. The weights of six pigs (in kg) are: 45, 50, 42, 48, 55, 47. Find the median weight.**

- A. 45 kg
- B. 47 kg
- C. 47.5 kg
- D. 47.8 kg

**Answer: C.** 
Step 1: Sort the data from smallest to largest: 42, 45, 47, 48, 50, 55.
Step 2: Since there are 6 items (even), find the two middle numbers: 47 and 48.
Step 3: Average them: $(47 + 48) / 2 = 95 / 2 = 47.5$ kg. 
(Option A is an unsorted middle value. Option D is the mean, a common calculation slip when asked for the median).

**8. The litter sizes of 8 sows on a farm are: 8, 10, 8, 12, 11, 8, 9, 10. What is the mode of this data?**

- A. 8
- B. 9
- C. 9.5
- D. 10

**Answer: A.** The mode is the most frequently occurring value. The number 8 appears three times, which is more than any other number.

**9. When an agricultural researcher concludes that the difference in yield between a treated plot and a control plot is "statistically significant", it means that**

- A. the difference is very small and unimportant
- B. the null hypothesis is accepted
- C. the difference is likely due to the treatment and not just chance
- D. the experiment had too much experimental error

**Answer: C.** A statistically significant result means the difference is large enough that it was caused by the treatment, allowing the researcher to safely reject the null hypothesis.

**10. Repeating a treatment on several different plots in an experiment is known as**

- A. randomization
- B. blocking
- C. sampling
- D. replication

**Answer: D.** Replication is the repetition of treatments on multiple plots to increase the reliability of the results and reduce the impact of experimental error.
