## Basic terms in ecology

Before studying the factors that decide where organisms live, you need to know the basic words that ecologists use.

### Ecology

**Ecology** is the study of living organisms in relation to each other and to their non-living surroundings (environment). The word comes from two Greek words: *oikos*, meaning "home", and *logos*, meaning "study". So ecology is, literally, "the study of organisms at home". The German biologist **Ernst Haeckel** first used the word in 1866.

Ecology asks questions such as: Why do mangrove trees grow in the Niger Delta but not in Sokoto? Why are there more earthworms in a moist farm in Ogun State than in dry sand in Yobe State? What happens to the animals of a forest when it is cut down?

There are two main approaches:

- **Autecology:** the study of a single species (or a single organism) in relation to its environment, for example, the ecology of the *Agama* lizard.
- **Synecology:** the study of groups of different species living together (communities and ecosystems), for example, the ecology of a pond.

### Environment

The **environment** is everything around an organism that affects it. It includes:

- the **abiotic** (non-living, physical and chemical) parts, such as light, temperature, water, soil and air; and
- the **biotic** (living) parts, such as the other plants, animals and microorganisms around it.

### Habitat

A **habitat** is the natural home of an organism: the place where it lives and where it finds food, shelter and mates. Examples: a pond is the habitat of *Tilapia* and *Spirogyra*; the bark of a tree is the habitat of lichens; the soil is the habitat of earthworms.

A very small part of a habitat with its own special conditions is called a **microhabitat**. Examples: the space under a stone or a rotting log (home of woodlice, millipedes and centipedes); the inside of a dead tree trunk; the gut of a cow (home of cellulose-digesting microorganisms).

### Population

A **population** is a group of organisms of the **same species** living together in the same habitat at the same time, and able to breed with one another. Examples: all the *Tilapia* fish in a pond; all the *Agama* lizards in a school compound; all the people living in Kano city. (Populations are studied in detail in topic C5.)

### Community

A **community** is all the populations of **different species** living together and interacting in the same habitat. Example: the community of a pond includes populations of *Spirogyra*, water lilies, water lettuce, water fleas, mosquito larvae, frogs, *Tilapia* and many bacteria.

### Ecosystem

An **ecosystem** is a natural unit made up of a **community** of living organisms together with their **non-living (abiotic) environment**, all interacting as one system. Energy flows through it, and nutrients are cycled within it.

**Ecosystem = community (biotic part) + physical environment (abiotic part).**

Examples: a pond, a lake (Kainji Lake), a forest, a savanna field, a mangrove swamp, even an aquarium. Ecosystems can be:

- **Aquatic** (water): freshwater (ponds, lakes, rivers) or marine (seas, oceans), with brackish water (estuaries, lagoons) in between.
- **Terrestrial** (land): forests, grasslands (savannas), deserts.

#### Components of an ecosystem

| Component | What it includes | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Abiotic (non-living)** | Climatic factors, soil (edaphic) factors, topographic factors, water and its dissolved substances | Light, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind, soil, pH, salinity |
| **Producers (autotrophs)** | Green plants and algae that make food by photosynthesis | Grasses, trees, *Spirogyra*, phytoplankton |
| **Consumers (heterotrophs)** | Animals that eat other organisms. Primary consumers (herbivores) eat plants; secondary and tertiary consumers (carnivores) eat other animals | Grasshopper, goat (primary); lizard, frog (secondary); hawk (tertiary) |
| **Decomposers** | Bacteria and fungi that break down dead organisms and wastes, releasing nutrients back to the soil or water | *Rhizopus*, mushrooms, putrefying bacteria |

(How energy flows between these components is explained in topic C2.)

### Biosphere

The **biosphere** is the part of the Earth (land, water and air) where life exists. It is the sum of all the ecosystems on Earth. It stretches from the deepest parts of the ocean to a few kilometres up in the atmosphere. It includes:

- the **lithosphere** (land, rocks and soil),
- the **hydrosphere** (water: oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, ground water), and
- the **atmosphere** (air).

### Ecological niche

A **niche** (ecological niche) is the **role** or "job" of a species in its ecosystem: what it eats, what eats it, where and when it feeds, how it reproduces, and how it affects and is affected by its surroundings. A simple way to remember it:

- the **habitat** is the organism's "address";
- the **niche** is its "profession" (its way of life).

**Example:** in a savanna field, the grasshopper and the cattle egret share the same habitat, but they have different niches. The grasshopper is a herbivore that eats grass leaves by day. The cattle egret is a carnivore that walks behind grazing cattle and eats the insects (including grasshoppers) that they disturb.

**Important rule:** two species cannot share exactly the same niche in the same place for long. If they do, one will out-compete the other (the **competitive exclusion principle**, also called **Gause's principle**). This is why species that live together usually differ slightly in their niches (niche differentiation; see topic C5).

### Biome

A **biome** is a large region of the Earth with a distinctive climate and a characteristic type of vegetation and animals, for example, the tropical rainforest, the savanna and the desert. Nigeria's biomes are studied in topic C4.

### Levels of ecological organisation

From the smallest to the largest:

**Organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere**

| Level | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organism | One individual living thing | One *Tilapia* fish |
| Population | Members of one species in one area | All the *Tilapia* in Oguta Lake |
| Community | All the populations of different species in one area | All the fish, plants, plankton and birds of Oguta Lake |
| Ecosystem | The community plus its non-living environment | Oguta Lake with its water, mud, light and dissolved gases |
| Biome | A large region with similar climate and vegetation | The tropical rainforest of southern Nigeria |
| Biosphere | All the parts of the Earth where life exists | The whole Earth's living layer |

### Ecological factors

**Ecological factors** are the conditions in the environment that affect organisms and decide where they can live. They are of two kinds, which are the two content items of this topic:

| Feature | Abiotic factors | Biotic factors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | **Non-living** physical and chemical conditions of the environment | Effects of **living** organisms on one another |
| Main groups | Climatic (temperature, light, rainfall, humidity, wind); edaphic (soil); topographic (altitude, slope); chemical (salinity, pH, dissolved gases) and physical conditions of water (turbidity, currents) | Food, predation, competition, parasitism, disease, symbiosis (mutualism, commensalism), pollination and dispersal, human activities |
| Examples | Low rainfall in Borno State limits the growth of tall trees | Tsetse flies, which carry trypanosomes, limit the keeping of cattle in parts of the humid south |
| How they are measured | With instruments (thermometer, rain gauge, Secchi disc, hygrometer, pH meter) | By counting and observing organisms (quadrats, transects, capture–recapture) |

Every organism can survive only within a certain **range of tolerance** for each factor. For example, a fish may survive between about 10 °C and 35 °C but grow best within a narrower **optimum** range. Outside its range, the organism becomes stressed and then dies. A factor that is in short supply, or too extreme, and so stops a population from growing or spreading, is called a **limiting factor**. In the dry far north of Nigeria, water is the limiting factor for most plants; on the dark floor of the rainforest, light is the limiting factor.

## Abiotic factors and the distribution of organisms

**Objective i: relate the effects of temperature, rainfall, relative humidity, wind speed and direction, altitude, salinity, turbidity, pH and edaphic (soil) conditions on the distribution of organisms.**

**Abiotic factors** are the non-living physical and chemical parts of the environment. Each one is explained below with its effects on where plants and animals are found, and with examples from Nigeria.

Nigeria is a good country for studying abiotic factors because conditions change greatly from the south coast to the far north:

- The **south** (near the Atlantic coast) is hot, very wet and humid, with a long rainy season.
- Moving **north**, rainfall falls, the dry season gets longer, humidity drops and temperatures become more extreme.
- The **plateaus** (Jos, Mambilla, Obudu) are higher and cooler.

These changes are why the vegetation changes from mangrove swamps and rainforest in the south to Guinea savanna, Sudan savanna and the Sahel in the north (see topic C4).

### 1. Temperature

**Temperature** is how hot or cold a place is. It is measured in degrees Celsius (°C).

**Why temperature matters to organisms:**

- All life processes (respiration, photosynthesis, digestion, growth) are controlled by **enzymes**. Enzymes work best at an **optimum temperature**. They work slowly when it is cold and are **denatured** (destroyed) when it is too hot, usually above about 40–45 °C for most organisms.
- **Cold-blooded (poikilothermic) animals**, such as lizards, snakes, frogs, fish and insects, cannot keep their body temperature constant. Their activity rises and falls with the temperature around them. This is why lizards bask in the morning and hide in the shade at midday (see topic A3).
- **Warm-blooded (homoiothermic) animals** (birds and mammals) can keep a constant body temperature, so they can live in a wider range of places, but they need more food to make heat.
- High temperature increases **evaporation and transpiration**, so organisms in hot places lose water quickly.

**Effects of temperature on distribution:**

1. **It decides which plants grow where.** Cocoa, oil palm and rubber need warm, humid conditions without great extremes and grow well in the rainforest of the south. Temperate crops such as **Irish potato**, cabbage, carrot and lettuce grow well on the cooler **Jos Plateau**. Tea is grown on the cool **Mambilla Plateau** in Taraba State.
2. **It causes dormancy.** In the hot dry season, some animals **aestivate**: the African lungfish (*Protopterus*) buries itself in mud, and land snails seal their shells. Many seeds stay dormant in the hot, dry soil until the rains come.
3. **It affects the distribution of cold-blooded animals.** Reptiles are commoner in warm places. In very hot places, many animals become **nocturnal** (active at night), for example many desert rodents, scorpions and the fennec fox of the Sahara.
4. **It affects breeding.** Many animals breed only when temperatures (and rainfall) are right. Termites swarm and frogs breed at the start of the rainy season.
5. **In water,** warm water holds **less dissolved oxygen** than cold water. Very warm, still ponds in the dry season may become short of oxygen, and fish come to the surface to gulp air.
6. **Adaptations to high temperature:** large ears in some animals (to lose heat), sweating and panting, burrowing to avoid the midday heat, light-coloured coats in many desert animals, and in plants, shedding leaves in the dry season (many savanna trees) or having thick, corky bark.

**Nigerian example:** temperatures in Maiduguri and Sokoto often rise above 40 °C in the hot season (about March to May), while the harmattan nights in December and January can be cold. On the Jos Plateau, at over 1,000 m, the air is much cooler all year. These differences help to explain why the plants and crops of these places are so different.

### 2. Rainfall

**Rainfall** is the amount of water that falls as rain in a place over a period of time. It is measured with a **rain gauge** in **millimetres (mm)**. Ecologists look at three things:

- the **total amount** of rain in a year;
- its **distribution** (how many months it falls, and how long the dry season is); and
- its **reliability** (whether it comes at the expected time each year).

**Why rainfall matters:** water is needed for photosynthesis, transport in plants, keeping cells turgid, germination, and as the habitat of aquatic organisms. Rainfall is the main source of water for land organisms. It is the **most important factor** deciding the type of vegetation in Nigeria.

**Effects of rainfall on distribution:**

1. **Type of vegetation.** Where rainfall is high and spread over most of the year, tall trees grow close together and form **forests**. Where rainfall is lower and there is a long dry season, trees are fewer and grasses dominate: **savanna**. Where rainfall is very low, there are only scattered shrubs, short grasses and thorny plants: **semi-desert and desert**.
2. **Type of crops grown.** High rainfall in the south suits cocoa, oil palm, rubber, plantain and kola. Moderate rainfall in the middle belt suits yam, cassava, maize and rice. Low rainfall in the north suits millet, sorghum (guinea corn), groundnut and cowpea, which are drought-tolerant.
3. **Distribution of animals.** Animals that need moisture, such as amphibians (frogs and toads), earthworms, snails, mosquitoes and leeches, are common in wet areas. Animals that can survive drought, such as camels, many lizards, desert rodents and some antelopes, are found in drier areas.
4. **Seasonal changes.** In the rainy season, ponds fill, grasses grow quickly, insects multiply, frogs breed and many animals give birth. In the dry season, many plants shed their leaves, grasses dry up, some animals migrate south or to rivers, and others aestivate.
5. **Leaching.** Very heavy rain washes nutrients down and out of the soil (leaching), which makes many rainforest soils acidic and poor once the forest is cleared.
6. **Floods.** Heavy rain may flood low-lying areas. Some organisms are adapted to flooding (rice, floating plants such as water lettuce, swamp trees such as *Raphia* palms), but floods also destroy farms and drown burrowing animals.

**Nigerian example (approximate figures, which vary between sources and from year to year):** parts of the Niger Delta coast receive more than 3,000 mm of rain a year, and some coastal places around 4,000 mm. Lagos receives roughly 1,500–2,000 mm. Rainfall falls steadily northwards, to roughly 500–700 mm or less in the far north-east around Lake Chad. The rainy season lasts about 8–10 months in the far south but only about 3–4 months in the far north.

### 3. Relative humidity

**Humidity** is the amount of water vapour in the air. **Relative humidity** (RH) is the amount of water vapour actually in the air, expressed as a **percentage** of the maximum amount the air could hold at that temperature:

**Relative humidity (%) = (amount of water vapour present ÷ maximum amount the air can hold at that temperature) × 100**

At 100% relative humidity, the air is **saturated** and cannot take in any more water vapour. Warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air, so relative humidity changes during the day as the temperature changes. It is measured with a **hygrometer** (for example, the wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer explained below).

**Why it matters:** relative humidity controls how fast water **evaporates** from organisms.

- When the air is **dry** (low relative humidity), water evaporates quickly. Plants **transpire** faster and may wilt; animals lose water through the skin and breathing.
- When the air is **humid** (high relative humidity), evaporation is slow. Organisms with thin, moist skins can survive, but sweating cools the body less well, which is why the humid south can feel very uncomfortable.

**Effects of relative humidity on distribution:**

1. **Organisms with thin, moist skins or no protection against drying out** are found mainly in humid places: **frogs and toads** (which breathe partly through the skin), **earthworms**, **slugs and snails**, **woodlice**, **mosses, liverworts and ferns**, and many **fungi**. You find them under logs, on forest floors and near water.
2. **Epiphytes** (plants growing on other plants, such as orchids, some ferns and mosses on tree trunks) are common in the humid rainforest because they can absorb moisture from the air.
3. **Fungi and moulds** grow quickly when humidity is high. This is why bread, garri and grains go mouldy quickly in the rainy season, and why fungal and fungus-like diseases of crops (such as black pod disease of cocoa, caused by the water mould *Phytophthora*) are common in the humid south.
4. **Organisms of dry places** have adaptations to reduce water loss: thick waxy cuticles, sunken stomata and spines in plants; scales, hard exoskeletons and burrowing in animals.

**Nigerian example:** in the south, relative humidity is high for most of the year, often above 80% in the mornings. During the **harmattan** (roughly November to February), the dry, dusty north-east wind from the Sahara brings very low humidity to the north. Lips and skin crack, leaves dry out, and fires spread easily in the dry grass.

### 4. Wind speed and direction

**Wind** is moving air. Two aspects of wind matter:

- **Wind speed**, measured with an **anemometer** (in metres per second, kilometres per hour or knots).
- **Wind direction**, shown by a **wind vane** or windsock. A wind is named after the direction it blows **from**: a north-east wind blows from the north-east.

**Nigerian winds:** two main air masses control Nigeria's weather:

- The **south-west (tropical maritime) wind** blows from the Atlantic Ocean. It is moist and brings the **rainy season**.
- The **north-east trade wind (the harmattan)** blows from the Sahara Desert. It is dry, dusty and cool at night, and brings the **dry season**, especially in the north.

**Effects of wind speed on organisms:**

1. **Increases transpiration and evaporation.** Wind removes the moist air around leaves and skin, so water is lost faster. Strong, dry winds such as the harmattan dry out plants and soils.
2. **Damages plants.** Strong winds and storms break branches, uproot trees (especially shallow-rooted ones) and flatten crops such as maize ("lodging"). Trees on exposed hilltops and coasts are often short and bent, growing in the direction the wind blows.
3. **Causes soil erosion.** In dry areas with little plant cover, wind blows away fertile topsoil (wind erosion), as happens in parts of the far north.
4. **Spreads fire.** Wind fans bush fires in the dry season.
5. **Affects flying animals.** Small insects cannot fly in strong winds. Birds use winds when migrating.

**Effects of wind direction on organisms:**

1. **Dispersal of seeds and fruits.** Light seeds and fruits with wings or hairs are carried in the direction the wind blows. Examples: the silk cotton tree (*Ceiba pentandra*) and kapok, whose seeds have cotton-like hairs; *Tridax* and other members of the daisy family with a parachute of hairs (pappus); *Combretum* and *Terminalia* with winged fruits.
2. **Pollination.** Wind carries pollen of grasses, maize, rice, millet, sorghum and many other wind-pollinated plants.
3. **Spread of spores, pests and diseases.** Wind carries fungal spores (such as those of rusts and smuts of cereals) and small insects. **Desert locusts** fly long distances with the wind.
4. **Rainfall pattern.** The direction of the wind decides where moist air goes and where rain falls. The rain-bearing south-west wind gives the south more rain than the north. On mountains, the side facing the moist wind (the **windward** side) usually receives more rain and has richer vegetation than the sheltered side (the **leeward** side or rain shadow).

### 5. Altitude

**Altitude** is the height of a place above sea level, measured in metres. It is measured with an **altimeter** (or found from maps or a GPS device).

**How altitude changes the environment:** as you go higher,

- the **temperature falls**, on average by roughly 6–6.5 °C for every 1,000 m (about 0.6 °C per 100 m);
- the **air pressure falls**, so there is less oxygen in each breath;
- **wind speed** usually increases;
- **rainfall** often increases on the windward side, and mist and cloud are common;
- **light intensity** and ultraviolet radiation increase;
- **soils** are often thinner and rockier on slopes.

**Textbook variation:** the rate at which temperature falls with height is given differently in different books, for example "about 1 °C for every 150 m", "0.6 °C per 100 m" or "6.5 °C per 1,000 m". These are all approximate averages; the real rate changes with the weather.

**Effects of altitude on distribution:**

1. **Vegetation changes with height**, in a similar way to how it changes with latitude. On high mountains, lowland forest gives way to **montane forest**, then to **montane grassland**, as on the Obudu, Mambilla and Jos Plateaus (topic C4).
2. **Temperate crops and plants** grow on Nigeria's plateaus because they are cooler: Irish potato, cabbage, carrot and lettuce on the Jos Plateau; tea on the Mambilla Plateau.
3. **Fewer tsetse flies and mosquitoes** live at high altitudes because it is cooler. This is one reason why the cool, grassy Jos, Mambilla and Obudu Plateaus are good for **cattle rearing**. The Obudu Cattle Ranch in Cross River State was set up on the Obudu Plateau partly for this reason.
4. **Animals at high altitudes** often have thicker fur or feathers to keep warm, and some have more red blood cells or larger lungs to take in enough oxygen.

**Nigerian examples (approximate heights):** the Jos Plateau averages over 1,200 m; the Obudu Plateau is roughly 1,500–1,600 m; the Mambilla Plateau averages about 1,500 m and includes **Chappal Waddi** (about 2,419 m), the highest point in Nigeria, on the border with Cameroon. Heights vary slightly between sources.

### 6. Salinity

**Salinity** is the amount of dissolved salts (mainly sodium chloride) in water or soil. In water, it is usually given in **parts per thousand** (‰, or grams of salt per kilogram of water). It is measured with a **salinometer** or a **hydrometer**.

| Type of water | Approximate salinity | Nigerian examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Freshwater** | Less than about 0.5 ‰ | Rivers Niger and Benue, Kainji Lake, Oguta Lake, Lake Chad, most ponds and streams |
| **Brackish water** (a mixture of fresh and sea water) | Between about 0.5 ‰ and 30 ‰ (it changes with tides and seasons) | Lagos Lagoon, the creeks and estuaries of the Niger Delta, mangrove swamps |
| **Sea water (marine)** | About 35 ‰ on average | The Atlantic Ocean (Gulf of Guinea), Bar Beach in Lagos |

**Textbook variation:** the exact salinity limits used for freshwater, brackish water and sea water differ between books; the figures above are common approximate values.

**Why salinity matters: osmosis.** Salinity decides the direction of water movement by osmosis between an organism and its surroundings.

- A **freshwater** organism has body fluids that are **more concentrated** than the water around it. Water keeps entering its body by osmosis. Freshwater fish produce large amounts of dilute urine; freshwater protists such as *Amoeba* and *Paramecium* use **contractile vacuoles** to pump out excess water.
- A **marine** organism lives in water that is as concentrated as, or more concentrated than, its body fluids. Marine bony fish tend to lose water by osmosis; they drink sea water and get rid of excess salt through their gills.
- Most organisms can tolerate only a narrow range of salinity. If a freshwater fish is put in sea water, it loses water and dies; if a marine fish is put in fresh water, it takes in too much water and dies.

**Effects of salinity on distribution:**

1. **It separates freshwater organisms from marine organisms.** For example, *Tilapia* (in many species), catfish (*Clarias*), water lilies, water lettuce (*Pistia*) and *Spirogyra* live in fresh water; sharks, mackerel, barracuda, jellyfish, sea anemones, crabs and seaweeds live in the sea.
2. **Only specially adapted organisms live in brackish water,** where salinity changes with the tides and seasons. Examples: **mangrove trees** (red mangrove, *Rhizophora*, and white mangrove, *Avicennia*), the mudskipper (*Periophthalmus*), fiddler crabs, oysters and some species of *Tilapia* and mullet. Mangroves have special adaptations to cope with salt (see topic C3).
3. **Salty soils** limit plant growth because the high salt concentration in the soil water makes it hard for roots to take in water by osmosis (the soil water may even draw water out of the roots). Plants that can grow in salty soil are called **halophytes**, for example mangroves and some coastal grasses.
4. **Seasonal changes:** in the Lagos Lagoon, salinity is higher in the dry season (when sea water flows in) and lower in the rainy season (when rivers bring in more fresh water). The organisms found in the lagoon change with the seasons.

### 7. Turbidity

**Turbidity** is the cloudiness or muddiness of water. It is caused by **suspended particles** such as clay, silt, sand, plankton and organic matter. Turbid (cloudy) water lets less light pass through it. Turbidity is measured with a **Secchi disc** (explained in full below).

**Causes of high turbidity:** soil washed into rivers by rain (erosion), floods, dredging and sand mining, sewage and waste, dense growth of plankton, and animals stirring up mud.

**Effects of turbidity on distribution:**

1. **Reduces light penetration.** Light is needed for photosynthesis. In very turbid water, light cannot reach far below the surface, so **phytoplankton** (tiny floating algae) and **submerged plants** can live only near the surface. In clear water, plants can grow at greater depths. Because producers are fewer, the whole food chain is reduced.
2. **Affects animals that hunt by sight.** Fish-eating birds (such as kingfishers) and predatory fish that hunt by sight find it harder to catch prey in muddy water. On the other hand, turbid water can help small fish hide from predators.
3. **Clogs gills and filter-feeding structures.** Suspended particles can clog the gills of fish and the feeding structures of filter-feeders such as mussels and oysters.
4. **Smothers organisms on the bottom.** When suspended particles settle, they can bury eggs, plants and bottom-living (benthic) animals.
5. **Affects temperature.** Turbid water absorbs more heat near the surface, so the surface becomes warmer.

**Nigerian example:** the River Niger and River Benue are usually muddy and brown in the rainy season, when rain washes soil from farms and riverbanks into them, and clearer in the dry season. Mining for tin on the Jos Plateau left many ponds and streams with very muddy water.

### 8. pH

**pH** is a measure of how acidic or alkaline a substance (soil or water) is, on a scale of **0 to 14**:

- **pH 7** is **neutral** (pure water).
- **Below 7** is **acidic** (the lower the number, the more acidic).
- **Above 7** is **alkaline** (basic).

pH is measured with a **pH meter**, **universal indicator** solution or **pH (indicator) paper**, or a soil-testing kit.

**Why pH matters:** enzymes work best at a particular pH. Soil pH also controls whether mineral nutrients are **available** to plants: in very acidic soils, some nutrients (such as phosphorus) become locked up, while others (such as aluminium) become so soluble that they are poisonous to roots.

**Effects of pH on distribution:**

1. **Most plants and animals prefer conditions near neutral** (soil pH roughly 6 to 7.5).
2. **Some plants tolerate or prefer acidic soils**, for example tea, pineapple, cassava and many plants of heavily leached rainforest soils. **Others prefer neutral or slightly alkaline soils**, for example many legumes.
3. **Earthworms** are scarce in very acidic soils; they prefer soils that are neutral or only slightly acidic. Since earthworms improve soil, this affects the whole soil community.
4. **Soil bacteria**, including the nitrogen-fixing bacteria and nitrifying bacteria (topic C2), work best in soils that are not too acidic.
5. **In water,** most fish and aquatic animals live best at a pH of about 6.5 to 8.5. Very acidic or very alkaline water kills fish eggs and young fish and damages gills.
6. **Farmers change soil pH.** Adding **lime** (calcium carbonate) to acidic soils raises the pH and makes them more productive.

**Nigerian examples:** heavy rainfall in the south leaches bases (such as calcium) out of the soil, so many rainforest soils are acidic. Waterlogged mangrove soils of the Niger Delta can become very acidic when they are drained and exposed to air (they are called acid sulphate soils). Acid rain has been linked to gas flaring in the Niger Delta; it can lower the pH of soils and water.

### 9. Edaphic (soil) factors

**Edaphic factors** are the features of the **soil** that affect organisms (from the Greek *edaphos*, "ground"). Soil is the medium in which plants grow, and the habitat of many animals (earthworms, termites, ants, millipedes, moles), fungi and bacteria. The soil decides which plants can grow, and so, indirectly, which animals can live there.

The main edaphic factors are:

#### a. Soil texture (particle size)

Soil is made of mineral particles of different sizes, mixed with humus, water, air and living organisms.

| Soil type | Particle size | Water and air | Fertility | Typical plants and uses in Nigeria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sandy soil** | Large particles (coarse) | Large air spaces; drains quickly; holds **little water**; well aerated; warms up quickly | Usually **poor** (nutrients are easily washed out) | Common in the far north. Millet, groundnut, cowpea, coconut (on coastal sand) |
| **Clay soil** | Very small particles (fine) | Small air spaces; holds **much water**; drains poorly; easily **waterlogged**; poorly aerated; sticky when wet, cracks when dry | Often **rich** in minerals but hard to work | River floodplains (fadama land). Rice, sugar cane |
| **Loamy soil** | A mixture of sand, silt and clay, with humus | Holds enough water and drains well; well aerated | **Most fertile** | Best for most crops: yam, maize, cocoa, vegetables |
| **Silty soil** | Particles between sand and clay in size | Holds water fairly well | Fairly fertile | River valleys and floodplains |

#### b. Soil water

Plants absorb water from the soil through their roots. **Waterlogged** soils have no air, so roots cannot respire, and only specially adapted plants (rice, mangroves, *Raphia* palms, water-loving grasses) can grow there. Very **dry** soils support only drought-tolerant plants (xerophytes). Burrowing animals such as earthworms need moist soil.

#### c. Soil air

Air spaces between soil particles supply **oxygen** for the respiration of roots, soil animals and soil microorganisms. Well-aerated soil (sandy and loamy) supports more root growth and more soil animals. Waterlogged or compacted soil (for example, under a footpath or where cattle trample) is poorly aerated.

#### d. Humus (organic matter)

**Humus** is the dark, decomposed remains of plants and animals in the soil. It:

- improves the soil's ability to hold water (especially sandy soil);
- improves the crumb structure and aeration (especially clay soil);
- releases mineral nutrients when it decays;
- provides food for earthworms, millipedes and decomposers.

Forest soils have a rich layer of humus from fallen leaves. Soils that are repeatedly burned or cropped without fallowing lose their humus.

#### e. Mineral salts (nutrients)

Plants need mineral nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium and magnesium from the soil. Where the soil is short of nutrients, plant growth is poor. Some plants are adapted to poor soils: **legumes** (such as groundnut, cowpea and soya bean) have nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules, so they can grow in soil poor in nitrogen.

#### f. Soil pH

(See section 8 above.)

#### g. Soil temperature

Soil temperature affects **germination** of seeds, root growth and the activity of soil organisms. Bare soil in the dry north becomes very hot at midday; a cover of mulch or vegetation keeps the soil cooler.

#### h. Soil organisms

Soil organisms are part of the soil environment. **Earthworms** mix and aerate the soil and drag leaves underground; **termites** build mounds and break down dead wood and grass; **bacteria and fungi** decompose organic matter and recycle nutrients. These biotic effects change the soil and so affect other organisms.

#### i. Soil depth and topography

Deep soils allow deep roots and large trees; thin soils on rocky hills support only grasses and shrubs. On slopes, soil is easily eroded and water runs off quickly.

**Summary of edaphic effects on distribution:** soil type, water, air, humus, minerals and pH together decide which plants grow in an area. For example, in Nigeria cocoa grows best on deep, well-drained, fertile loamy soils of the south-west; rice grows on clayey floodplains (fadama) of the Niger and Benue valleys and in states such as Kebbi, Niger and Ebonyi; groundnut and millet grow on the sandy soils of the north.

### 10. Other abiotic factors

The syllabus names the nine factors above. Three other abiotic factors also affect distribution and appear in topic C5:

#### Light

- **Light intensity** (brightness) controls the rate of **photosynthesis**. Plants that need bright light (sun plants such as grasses, maize and most crops) grow in open places. Plants that tolerate shade (shade plants such as many ferns, mosses and forest-floor herbs) grow under trees. In the rainforest, the tall trees form a closed canopy, so the forest floor receives very little light, and only shade-tolerant plants grow there.
- **Light in water:** light decreases with depth, so producers are found only in the upper, lighted layer of water (see turbidity).
- **Day length (photoperiod)** controls flowering in many plants and breeding in many animals. Near the equator, as in Nigeria, day length changes only a little through the year (roughly 11.5 to 12.5 hours).
- **Animals:** some animals are active by day (diurnal), others at night (nocturnal), such as bats, owls, bush babies and many moths. Earthworms and woodlice move away from light.
- Light is measured with a **light meter** (photometer).

#### Topography (physiography)

**Topography** is the shape of the land surface: hills, valleys, slopes, plains and plateaus. It includes **altitude**, **steepness of slope** and **aspect** (the direction a slope faces). Steep slopes have thin soil and fast run-off; valleys collect water and soil and may be swampy; slopes facing the rain-bearing wind receive more rain.

#### Pressure

**Atmospheric pressure** falls with altitude; **water pressure** rises with depth (by about 1 atmosphere for every 10 m of water). Deep-sea animals are adapted to great pressure; animals living at high altitudes are adapted to low pressure and low oxygen. Pressure is measured with a **barometer**.

## Biotic factors and the distribution of organisms

**Biotic factors** are the effects that living organisms have on one another. No organism lives alone; each one is affected by the plants, animals and microorganisms around it. The main biotic factors are listed below (the feeding relationships are explained in more detail in topic C2, and their effects on population size in topic C5).

### Food (availability of food)

Animals can live only where their food is found. Herbivores are found where suitable plants grow. Some animals depend on only one kind of plant, so they are found only where that plant grows: the silkworm caterpillar feeds on mulberry leaves, and many other caterpillars feed on a single host plant. Carnivores are found where their prey lives: lions follow herds of antelope. When food becomes scarce in the dry season, many grazing animals **migrate** to places with water and fresh grass. Fulani herders move their cattle southwards in the dry season (transhumance) for the same reason.

### Predation

A **predator** kills and eats another animal (its **prey**). Predators limit the numbers and distribution of their prey. Prey animals tend to avoid areas where predators are common, or live where they can hide. For example, the hawk preys on chicks and lizards; the lion preys on antelopes; the praying mantis preys on insects; the owl preys on rats. If predators are removed (for example by hunting), the prey population may grow and spread, and may then damage crops or vegetation.

### Competition

**Competition** happens when organisms need the same resource (food, water, light, space, mates, nesting sites) and the resource is in short supply.

- **Intraspecific competition:** between members of the **same** species (for example, maize plants planted too close together in a farm compete for light, water and minerals).
- **Interspecific competition:** between members of **different** species (for example, weeds compete with crops; lions and hyenas compete for the same prey).

Competition can stop a species from living in a place where a stronger competitor is already established. In the rainforest, tall trees out-compete smaller plants for light, so only shade-tolerant plants grow on the forest floor.

### Parasitism

A **parasite** lives on or in another organism (the **host**) and obtains food from it, harming the host. Parasites and the diseases they cause can limit where their hosts can live. Example: the **tsetse fly** carries trypanosomes, which cause **sleeping sickness** in humans and **nagana** (trypanosomiasis) in cattle. In parts of the humid south and middle belt where tsetse flies are common, it is difficult to keep large herds of cattle. This is one reason why most of Nigeria's cattle are kept in the drier north and on the cool plateaus, where tsetse flies are fewer.

### Disease

Disease-causing organisms (pathogens) such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists can kill large numbers of plants and animals and reduce their distribution. Examples: the cassava mosaic disease (caused by a virus spread by whiteflies) reduces cassava yields; black pod disease (caused by the fungus-like *Phytophthora*) damages cocoa in the humid south; malaria (carried by *Anopheles* mosquitoes) affects people throughout Nigeria.

### Mutualism and other symbiotic relationships

Some organisms can live only where their partners live:

- Leguminous plants such as cowpea and groundnut grow well in nitrogen-poor soils because of the **nitrogen-fixing bacteria** (*Rhizobium*) in their root nodules.
- Many plants depend on particular **pollinators** (bees, birds, bats) or **seed dispersers** (birds, monkeys, bats). If the animal disappears, the plant may not reproduce in that area.

(These relationships, including commensalism and saprophytism, are explained in topic C2.)

### Decomposers

Bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms and wastes, releasing nutrients back into the soil. Where decomposers are very active, as in the warm, humid rainforest, leaf litter is broken down quickly and nutrients are recycled fast.

## Measuring abiotic factors

**Objective ii: use appropriate equipment (secchi disc, thermometer, rain gauge) to measure abiotic factors.**

Ecologists measure abiotic factors so that they can explain why organisms are found where they are. The three instruments named in the syllabus are explained first, step by step. Other common instruments follow, and all are summarised in a table.

### 1. The Secchi disc (measuring turbidity and light penetration in water)

The **Secchi disc** is used to measure the **turbidity** (cloudiness) of water, and so how deep light can penetrate into it. It was named after the Italian scientist Angelo Secchi, who used it in the 1860s.

**Structure:**

- A flat, circular disc, usually about **20 cm** across, made of metal or plastic.
- The disc used in lakes and ponds is painted in **four quarters**, alternately **black and white**. (Discs used at sea are often plain white and larger; designs vary.)
- A weight is fixed underneath so that the disc sinks flat.
- It hangs from a **rope, chain or tape marked in centimetres or metres** (a calibrated line).

**Method (step by step):**

1. Go to the water body, ideally on a calm day, around the middle of the day (roughly 10 a.m. to 2 p.m.), when the sun is high. Do not wear sunglasses.
2. Stand or kneel on a boat, jetty or bridge on the **shady side**, so that reflected sunlight on the water surface does not dazzle you.
3. Lower the disc slowly and vertically into the water, keeping the rope straight.
4. Watch the disc as it goes down. Note the depth (from the marks on the rope) at which the disc **just disappears** from view. Call this depth **D₁**.
5. Lower the disc a little further, then **raise it slowly** until it **just reappears**. Note this depth, **D₂**.
6. Calculate the average of the two readings. This is the **Secchi depth** (Secchi disc transparency):

   **Secchi depth = (D₁ + D₂) ÷ 2**

7. Repeat the measurement two or three times (or at different places in the water body) and take the average.

**Worked example:** the disc disappears at 1.20 m and reappears at 1.00 m. Secchi depth = (1.20 + 1.00) ÷ 2 = **1.10 m**.

**Interpreting the results:**

- A **small Secchi depth** means the water is **very turbid** (cloudy): little light penetrates, so photosynthesis by phytoplankton and submerged plants is limited to a thin layer near the surface.
- A **large Secchi depth** means the water is **clear**: light penetrates deeper, so producers can live at greater depths.
- Changes in Secchi depth over time show changes in water quality, such as more silt after rain or an algal bloom caused by pollution.

**Precautions:** take readings at about the same time of day and in calm water; avoid shadows from people or the boat falling on the disc beyond what is needed to reduce glare; keep the line vertical (a slanted line gives a wrong depth); the same person should take repeated readings, because eyesight differs.

**Limitations:** the result depends on the observer's eyesight, the amount of sunlight and the surface conditions (waves). It cannot be used where the water is too shallow for the disc to disappear (the disc reaches the bottom while still visible).

### 2. The thermometer (measuring temperature)

A **thermometer** measures **temperature**, in degrees Celsius (°C).

#### Ordinary thermometers

- A **mercury or alcohol thermometer** is a thin glass tube with a bulb at the bottom. The liquid expands up the tube when it is warm and contracts when it is cold. It is used to measure the temperature of air, water or soil at one moment.
- A **soil thermometer** has a strong pointed end (or a metal casing) and is pushed into the soil to measure soil temperature at a given depth.
- **Digital (electronic) thermometers** with a probe are now commonly used too.

**How to measure air temperature correctly:** hang the thermometer in the **shade** (not in direct sunlight, which heats the bulb), about 1.2 m above the ground, and allow time for the reading to become steady before reading it at eye level. Weather stations keep thermometers inside a white, louvred wooden box called a **Stevenson screen**, which protects them from sunlight and rain while letting air flow through.

**To measure water temperature:** lower the thermometer into the water to the depth needed, leave it for about a minute, and read it while the bulb is still under the water (or use a thermometer fixed in a sampling bottle).

#### The maximum and minimum thermometer (Six's thermometer)

Ecologists usually want to know the **highest** and **lowest** temperatures in a habitat over a day, not just the temperature at one moment. The **maximum and minimum thermometer** (also called **Six's thermometer**, after its inventor James Six) records both.

**Structure:**

- A **U-shaped** glass tube with a bulb at each end.
- One bulb (the left one in most designs) is completely full of **alcohol**; this alcohol is the temperature-sensing liquid.
- The bend of the U contains a thread of **mercury**.
- There is alcohol above the mercury in both limbs; the bulb on the other side is only partly filled with alcohol, leaving a space for vapour.
- Inside each limb, resting on the mercury, there is a small **steel index** (a tiny metal pin) held in place by a light spring so that it does not slide down by itself.
- Each limb has its own scale: the **maximum scale** on one limb and the **minimum scale** (whose numbers increase downwards in the usual design, because the mercury rises in that limb as the temperature falls) on the other.

**How it works (step by step):**

1. **When the temperature rises,** the alcohol in the full bulb expands and pushes the mercury around the U-bend. The mercury rises in the **maximum** limb and pushes the steel index up in front of it.
2. **When the temperature falls,** the alcohol contracts. The mercury moves back, and now rises in the **minimum** limb, pushing that limb's index along.
3. When the mercury moves back, each index **stays where it was pushed**, held by its spring. So the index in the maximum limb marks the **highest temperature** reached, and the index in the minimum limb marks the **lowest temperature** reached.
4. **Reading:** read the scale at the **end of each index nearest the mercury** (the lower end in each limb).
5. **Resetting:** after reading, use a small **magnet** (or a press-button in some models) to pull each index back down until it rests on the mercury again. The thermometer is now ready for the next period (usually 24 hours).

**Uses:** recording the daily maximum and minimum temperatures of a habitat, a greenhouse, a school garden or a weather station; working out the **daily temperature range** (maximum minus minimum).

**Worked example:** a maximum and minimum thermometer in a school garden in Kano records a maximum of 38 °C and a minimum of 22 °C. The daily temperature range = 38 − 22 = **16 °C**.

### 3. The rain gauge (measuring rainfall)

A **rain gauge** measures the amount of **rainfall**, in **millimetres (mm)**. A reading of 10 mm means that, if none of the rain had soaked away, run off or evaporated, it would have formed a layer of water 10 mm deep over the ground.

**Structure:**

- An outer **metal or plastic cylinder**, partly sunk into the ground.
- A **funnel** at the top, of a known diameter, which collects the rain falling on its area and directs it downwards. The narrow neck of the funnel reduces evaporation of the collected water.
- A **collecting bottle or jar** inside the cylinder, under the funnel.
- A **measuring cylinder** (graduated in millimetres of rainfall for that size of funnel), used to measure the water collected.

**Method (step by step):**

1. **Choose an open site,** away from trees, buildings and walls, which could shelter the gauge or drip extra water into it. A common rule is to place it at a distance of at least twice the height of any nearby object.
2. **Set the gauge firmly** in the ground with the rim of the funnel level and some distance above the ground (commonly about 30 cm), so that rain splashing up from the ground does not enter it. Short grass around it reduces splashing.
3. **Leave it to collect rain** for a fixed period, usually **24 hours**.
4. **At the same time every day** (for example, 9 a.m.), remove the collecting bottle and pour the water into the special **measuring cylinder**.
5. **Read** the level at the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, and record the rainfall in millimetres.
6. **Empty** the bottle, replace it, and record the reading in a table.
7. Add up the daily readings to get the **monthly** and **annual** totals.

**Precautions:** read at the same time each day; keep the funnel free of leaves, insects and dirt; make sure no water is lost by evaporation or leakage; protect the gauge from animals and people.

**Uses:** recording the rainfall pattern of an area; deciding planting dates; comparing the rainfall of different places (for example, Port Harcourt and Kano) to explain differences in vegetation.

### 4. Hygrometer and the wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (measuring relative humidity)

A **hygrometer** measures **relative humidity**. The most common type in schools is the **wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer** (also called a **psychrometer**).

**Structure:** two identical thermometers mounted side by side.

- The **dry bulb** thermometer is an ordinary thermometer. It measures the **air temperature**.
- The **wet bulb** thermometer has its bulb wrapped in a piece of **muslin (cloth)**, whose end dips into a small container of **distilled water**, so the bulb is always wet.

**How it works:**

1. Water **evaporates** from the wet muslin. Evaporation takes heat from the wet bulb, so the wet bulb reading is **lower** than the dry bulb reading.
2. If the air is **dry** (low relative humidity), water evaporates **quickly**, cooling the wet bulb a lot. The **difference** between the two readings (the "wet-bulb depression") is **large**.
3. If the air is **humid** (high relative humidity), water evaporates **slowly**, so the difference is **small**.
4. If the air is **saturated** (100% relative humidity), no net evaporation takes place, and **both thermometers show the same reading**.
5. The two readings are taken, and the relative humidity is found by looking up the dry bulb temperature and the difference in a printed **hygrometric table** (or chart).

A **whirling hygrometer** (sling psychrometer) has the same two thermometers on a frame that is whirled around to move air quickly past the wet bulb, giving a more accurate reading. A **hair hygrometer** uses human hair, which lengthens when the air is humid and shortens when it is dry, to move a pointer. Digital hygrometers are also common.

### 5. Wind vane and anemometer (measuring wind direction and speed)

#### Wind vane

A **wind vane** (weather vane) shows the **direction** of the wind.

- It has a horizontal arrow (pointer) pivoted on a vertical rod, with a broad **tail fin** at one end and a narrow **arrowhead** at the other.
- Fixed arms below the arrow show the **compass directions** (N, S, E, W).
- The wind pushes on the broad tail, which swings away from the wind, so the **arrowhead points into the wind**, that is, towards the direction the wind is **coming from**.
- It should be placed on a high, open place, such as the top of a pole or roof, away from buildings and trees.

A **windsock** (a cloth cone open at both ends, seen at airports) also shows wind direction (it blows away from the wind) and roughly shows wind strength (how much it is filled out).

#### Anemometer

An **anemometer** measures **wind speed**.

- The common **cup anemometer** has three or four small hemispherical **cups** fixed on horizontal arms that spin around a vertical spindle.
- The wind pushes the cups, which spin faster as the wind gets stronger.
- The number of turns is counted by a meter connected to the spindle and converted into wind speed (in m/s, km/h or knots).
- Like the wind vane, it should be placed in a high, open position.

Wind speed can also be estimated without an instrument by observing its effects on trees, smoke and flags, using the **Beaufort scale** (from 0, calm, to 12, hurricane).

### 6. pH meter and indicator paper (measuring pH)

- A **pH meter** has a glass **electrode (probe)** that is dipped into a water sample or a mixture of soil and distilled water. It shows the pH directly on a scale or digital display. It must be **calibrated** with buffer solutions of known pH.
- **Universal indicator** (solution or paper) changes colour according to pH. The colour is compared with a **colour chart**: red for strongly acidic, orange and yellow for weakly acidic, green for neutral (pH 7), blue and purple for alkaline.
- **Litmus paper** shows only whether a substance is acidic (blue litmus turns red) or alkaline (red litmus turns blue). It does not give a pH number.

**Measuring soil pH with universal indicator (step by step):**

1. Put a small amount of soil (about 1 cm deep) into a test tube.
2. Add a little **barium sulphate** powder (or a similar powder) to help the clay particles settle, so the liquid becomes clear.
3. Add **distilled water** (never tap water, which may have its own pH) and a few drops of **universal indicator**.
4. Shake well and allow the soil to settle.
5. Compare the colour of the clear liquid with the colour chart to read the pH.

### 7. Light meter (measuring light intensity)

A **light meter** (photometer or lux meter) measures **light intensity**. It has a **light-sensitive cell** (photocell) that produces a small electric current proportional to the light falling on it; this moves a needle or gives a digital reading, usually in **lux**. It is held facing upwards at a fixed height, for example to compare the light on the open ground with the light under the canopy of a forest.

### 8. Hydrometer and salinometer (measuring salinity)

- A **hydrometer** measures the **density** (specific gravity) of a liquid. It is a weighted glass float with a narrow stem marked with a scale. It floats **higher** in denser liquid. Salty water is denser than fresh water, so the hydrometer floats higher in sea water than in fresh water. The reading is converted to salinity using a table.
- A **salinometer** measures salinity directly, usually by measuring the **electrical conductivity** of the water (salty water conducts electricity better than fresh water).
- Salinity can also be found in the laboratory by **evaporating** a known volume of water and weighing the salt left behind, or by chemical titration.

### 9. Soil auger (collecting soil samples)

A **soil auger** is a tool for **boring into the ground and taking soil samples** from different depths. It is a long metal rod with a **T-shaped handle** at the top and a **screw-like (spiral) bit** or a hollow tube at the bottom.

**How to use it:**

1. Place the tip on the ground and turn the handle so that the auger screws into the soil.
2. Pull it out; the soil caught in the spiral (or tube) is a sample from that depth.
3. Repeat to go deeper, laying out the samples in order to show the soil layers (the **soil profile**).

The samples can then be tested for water content, humus content, air content, pH, texture and soil organisms.

### 10. Other instruments

- **Barometer:** measures **atmospheric pressure** (in millibars or mm of mercury). Falling pressure often signals rain.
- **Altimeter:** measures **altitude** (height above sea level).
- **Sunshine recorder:** records the number of hours of sunshine in a day (the Campbell–Stokes recorder uses a glass sphere that burns a trace on a card).
- **Evaporimeter (e.g. Piche evaporimeter):** measures the rate of evaporation.
- **Water sampling bottle:** collects water from a chosen depth for testing (oxygen, pH, salinity, temperature).
- **Quadrats, transects, sweep nets, pitfall traps, Tullgren funnels:** these collect and count **organisms** (biotic factors) rather than measuring abiotic factors.

## Simple experiments on soil (edaphic factors)

JAMB sometimes asks about experiments to find the water, air and humus content of soil. Each is described in the standard form.

### Experiment 1: To find the water content of a soil sample

- **Aim:** to find the percentage of water in a fresh soil sample.
- **Materials:** fresh soil sample, evaporating dish, weighing balance, oven (or a hot-air oven or water bath).
- **Method:**
  1. Weigh an empty evaporating dish (W₁).
  2. Add fresh soil and weigh again (W₂). Mass of fresh soil = W₂ − W₁.
  3. Heat the dish in an oven at about 100–105 °C (just above the boiling point of water, but not hot enough to burn the humus).
  4. Cool and weigh. Repeat heating and weighing until the mass stops changing (**constant mass**). Record the final mass (W₃).
- **Calculation:** mass of water lost = W₂ − W₃. Percentage of water = [(W₂ − W₃) ÷ (W₂ − W₁)] × 100.
- **Observation and conclusion:** the soil loses mass because its water evaporates. Clay soil usually loses the most water, sandy soil the least.

### Experiment 2: To find the humus (organic matter) content of a soil sample

- **Aim:** to find the percentage of humus in a dry soil sample.
- **Method:** take the **dry** soil from experiment 1 (constant mass, W₃) in a crucible. Heat it **strongly** over a Bunsen flame for some time, stirring, until all the organic matter burns away. Cool and weigh. Repeat until the mass is constant (W₄).
- **Calculation:** mass of humus = W₃ − W₄. Percentage of humus = [(W₃ − W₄) ÷ (mass of dry soil)] × 100.
- **Conclusion:** loamy and forest soils usually contain the most humus. (The soil must be dried first, otherwise the loss of water would be counted as humus.)

### Experiment 3: To find the air content of a soil sample

- **Aim:** to show that soil contains air and to estimate how much.
- **Method:**
  1. Push an empty tin of known volume (with small holes punched in its base) open end first into the soil, until it is full of undisturbed soil. Dig it out and level the soil.
  2. Pour a measured volume of water (for example 200 cm³) into a measuring cylinder.
  3. Tip the soil into the cylinder of water, stir to release the air bubbles, and allow to settle.
  4. Read the new level of the water.
- **Calculation:** volume of air = (volume of water + volume of soil in the tin) − final volume reading. Percentage of air = (volume of air ÷ volume of soil) × 100.
- **Observation and conclusion:** bubbles of air rise from the soil as water fills the air spaces. Sandy soil contains the most air, clay soil the least.

### Experiment 4: To compare the water retention (drainage) of sandy, loamy and clay soils

- **Method:** place equal masses of dry sandy, loamy and clay soil in three funnels plugged with cotton wool, each over a measuring cylinder. Pour equal volumes of water into each at the same time. After a fixed time (for example 30 minutes), record the volume of water that has drained through.
- **Observation:** most water drains through the sandy soil, least through the clay soil.
- **Conclusion:** sandy soil has the **largest particles and air spaces**, so it drains fastest and **retains least water**; clay soil **retains most water**; loamy soil is in between.

## How the activities of plants and animals (particularly humans) affect distribution

**Objective iii: describe how the activities of plants/animals (particularly human) affect the distribution of organisms.**

### Activities of plants that affect distribution

Plants change their environment, and so change which other organisms can live there.

1. **Shading.** Tall trees form a canopy that shades the ground. This lowers light intensity and temperature and raises humidity under the trees. Only shade-tolerant plants (ferns, mosses, some herbs) and animals that like damp, dark places (snails, millipedes, frogs) live on the forest floor. Farmers make use of this: cocoa seedlings are planted under shade trees.
2. **Adding humus.** Fallen leaves, twigs and dead plants decay into humus, which improves the soil and supports earthworms, millipedes and decomposers.
3. **Providing food and shelter.** Plants are the producers on which all animals depend. Trees provide nesting sites for birds (weaver birds, hornbills), homes for monkeys and squirrels, and microhabitats for insects and epiphytes. Wherever a plant species grows, the animals that feed on it are found.
4. **Holding the soil.** Plant roots bind the soil and protect it from erosion by wind and water. Where vegetation is removed, the soil is washed away and other plants cannot grow.
5. **Changing the soil chemistry.** Legumes add nitrogen to the soil (through their root nodule bacteria). Some plants release chemicals from their roots or leaves that **stop other plants from growing** near them (this is called **allelopathy**). Fallen pine or eucalyptus leaves, for example, can make the ground beneath them poor for other plants.
6. **Competition.** Fast-growing plants and weeds take up light, water and nutrients and may crowd out others. The **water hyacinth** (*Eichhornia crassipes*), an introduced floating plant, has covered large parts of the Lagos Lagoon, creeks and rivers in southern Nigeria. It blocks light and lowers oxygen in the water, killing fish and other aquatic plants, and blocks waterways for boats and fishermen.
7. **Pioneer plants and succession.** Lichens, mosses and grasses colonise bare rock or bare land, form soil and make it possible for bigger plants to grow later (succession, topic C5).

### Activities of animals (other than humans) that affect distribution

1. **Grazing and browsing.** Herbivores such as cattle, goats, sheep and antelopes eat plants. Moderate grazing keeps grasses short and allows many small plants to grow; heavy grazing removes the plant cover. Goats browse on young trees and shrubs and stop forest from regrowing.
2. **Trampling.** Large animals (cattle, elephants) compact the soil, especially near water points and along paths, so fewer plants grow there.
3. **Predation.** Predators limit their prey, and so allow plants eaten by the prey to grow. Removing predators can lead to a population explosion of herbivores such as rats and grasshoppers.
4. **Pollination and seed dispersal.** Bees, butterflies, birds and bats pollinate flowers; birds, monkeys, fruit bats, rodents and elephants disperse seeds, often far from the parent plant. Animals therefore spread plants into new areas.
5. **Burrowing and soil mixing.** Earthworms aerate, drain and mix the soil; termites build mounds and change the soil around them, and plants growing on termite mounds are often different from those on the surrounding soil; rodents and moles dig burrows.
6. **Adding manure.** Animal droppings add nutrients to the soil, so plants grow more vigorously around cattle kraals and bird roosts.
7. **Building structures.** Weaver birds strip trees of their leaves when building nests; elephants push down trees and open up forest, turning it into more grassy vegetation.
8. **Spreading disease.** Vectors such as mosquitoes, tsetse flies and whiteflies carry diseases that limit the distribution of humans, livestock and crops.

### Human activities that affect distribution

Humans change the environment more than any other organism. Some changes are deliberate (clearing land for farms), and others are side effects (pollution). The main human activities are explained below.

#### 1. Deforestation

**Deforestation** is the cutting down or clearing of forests without replacing them, for timber, fuelwood, farmland, roads, settlements and mining.

**Effects on distribution:**

- **Loss of habitat:** forest animals (monkeys, chimpanzees, forest elephants, many birds and insects) lose their homes and food and either move away or die. Some, such as the Cross River gorilla, now survive only in small patches of forest.
- **Loss of species (biodiversity):** many forest plants, including valuable timber trees such as iroko (*Milicia excelsa*) and African mahogany (*Khaya*), become rare.
- **Soil erosion and leaching:** without trees, heavy rain washes away the topsoil, causing gully erosion (serious in Anambra, Imo and Enugu States) and loss of fertility.
- **Change in microclimate:** the cleared land becomes hotter, drier and brighter, so forest-floor organisms (mosses, ferns, frogs, snails) disappear and sun-loving grasses and weeds invade.
- **Climate change:** trees store carbon; cutting and burning them releases carbon (IV) oxide (CO₂), which adds to global warming (topic C2).
- **Desertification:** in the north, cutting trees for firewood removes the protection against wind erosion and helps the desert spread southwards.

#### 2. Bush burning

**Bush burning** is the deliberate or accidental setting of fire to vegetation, common in the dry season in the savanna. People burn the bush to clear land for farming, to hunt animals (driving out grasscutters and rodents), to stimulate fresh grass for cattle, and to control ticks and pests.

**Effects on distribution:**

- **Kills plants and animals**, especially small, slow-moving animals (snails, insects, tortoises, young birds in nests) and seedlings.
- **Favours fire-resistant plants:** only plants with thick, corky bark (such as the shea tree, *Vitellaria paradoxa*, and the locust bean tree, *Parkia biglobosa*), underground stems and deep roots survive regular burning. Over time, burning turns forest edges into savanna and keeps the savanna open. Fire-sensitive trees disappear.
- **Destroys humus and soil organisms**, and leaves the soil bare, so it is easily eroded.
- **Air pollution:** smoke and carbon (IV) oxide are released into the air.
- Some animals benefit: cattle egrets and birds of prey gather at the edge of fires to catch fleeing insects and rodents.

#### 3. Farming (agriculture)

**Effects on distribution:**

- **Clearing of natural vegetation** for farms removes the habitat of wild plants and animals.
- **Monocropping** (growing one crop over a large area, as in large rice, maize or oil palm plantations) reduces the variety of plants and animals, and allows pests of that crop to multiply.
- **Fertilisers** washed into rivers and ponds cause **eutrophication**: algae grow rapidly (algal bloom), then die and decay, using up the oxygen in the water, so fish and other animals die.
- **Pesticides and herbicides** kill not only pests and weeds but also useful organisms such as bees, earthworms, frogs and birds. Some pesticides build up in food chains (topic C2).
- **Irrigation** brings water to dry lands, allowing crops (and water-loving organisms such as mosquitoes and snails that carry schistosomiasis) to spread into dry areas. Poorly managed irrigation can make soils salty.
- **Shifting cultivation and bush fallowing**, when practised with long fallow periods, allow the vegetation to recover; when fallow periods become short because of population pressure, the soil becomes poor and the forest does not return.
- **Introduction of crops and livestock** brings new species into an area (for example, cassava and maize were introduced into Africa from the Americas).

#### 4. Urbanisation and road building

**Urbanisation** is the growth of towns and cities, with houses, roads, industries and markets.

**Effects on distribution:**

- **Natural habitats are destroyed** and replaced by buildings and concrete. Wetlands and mangroves around Lagos, for example, have been filled with sand for housing and roads.
- **Some organisms are favoured:** animals that live close to humans, such as rats, house mice, cockroaches, houseflies, house sparrows, pigeons, *Agama* lizards and some mosquitoes, become common in towns.
- **Roads split habitats** into small pieces (fragmentation), so animals cannot move between them, and many animals are killed by vehicles.
- **Waste and pollution** increase (see below).

#### 5. Dams

Dams are built across rivers to store water for **hydroelectric power**, irrigation and water supply. Nigerian examples: the **Kainji Dam** (completed in 1968) and **Jebba Dam** on the River Niger, and the **Shiroro Dam** on the River Kaduna, all in Niger State.

**Effects on distribution:**

- **Flooding of land behind the dam:** a large lake (reservoir) forms. Land organisms are drowned or driven out, and villages and farms are moved. Kainji Lake, for example, flooded a large area of land, including the old town of Bussa, whose people were resettled in New Bussa.
- **New aquatic habitat:** the lake provides a home for fish, water birds and aquatic plants, and supports a fishing industry. (Kainji Lake National Park lies next to the lake.)
- **Changes downstream:** below the dam, the river carries less water and less silt in some seasons, and floodplains that used to flood naturally may no longer flood each year. This affects floodplain farming and fishing, and the fish that need floods to breed. Release of water from dams during heavy rain can cause sudden floods downstream.
- **Blocking of fish migration:** fish that swim up or down the river to breed cannot pass the dam.
- **Spread of water-related diseases:** still water in reservoirs and irrigation canals favours snails that carry **schistosomiasis** (bilharzia), and mosquitoes.

#### 6. Pollution

**Pollution** is the release of harmful substances or energy into the environment.

| Type | Main sources in Nigeria | Effects on distribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Water pollution** | Oil spills (Niger Delta), sewage, industrial waste, fertilisers, pesticides, refuse dumped in drains and rivers | Kills fish, crabs, plankton and mangroves; oil coats the gills of fish and the feathers of birds; eutrophication reduces oxygen; only pollution-tolerant organisms (such as sludge worms and some mosquito larvae) survive |
| **Land (soil) pollution** | Oil spills, refuse, plastics, mining waste, heavy metals | Kills soil organisms and crops; makes land unfit for farming |
| **Air pollution** | Gas flaring, vehicle exhaust, generators, bush burning, factories | Damages leaves; lichens (which are very sensitive to sulphur (IV) oxide) disappear from polluted areas; acid rain; respiratory diseases |
| **Noise and light pollution** | Industries, traffic, generators | Disturbs animals; some birds and other animals avoid noisy areas |

**Nigerian example:** in the **Niger Delta**, decades of oil spills and gas flaring have damaged mangroves, creeks and farmland. A 2011 report by the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) on **Ogoniland** found serious and widespread oil contamination of land and water there, and recommended a long-term clean-up.

**Note:** lichens are used as **indicators** of air pollution. Where the air is clean, many kinds of lichens grow on tree trunks and walls; where it is polluted with sulphur (IV) oxide, few or none grow.

#### 7. Overgrazing

**Overgrazing** happens when too many animals (cattle, goats, sheep) graze an area for too long, so the plants cannot recover.

**Effects on distribution:**

- Grass cover is destroyed and the soil is left bare.
- Bare soil is **eroded** by wind and rain, and **trampling** compacts it.
- Palatable (tasty) grasses disappear and are replaced by unpalatable, thorny shrubs and weeds.
- In the dry north, overgrazing (together with drought and cutting trees for firewood) contributes to **desertification**: the spread of desert-like conditions southwards. Northern states such as Sokoto, Yobe, Borno, Jigawa and Katsina are among those most affected.
- It forces herders to move to new areas in search of pasture, which can lead to conflict with farmers.

#### 8. Hunting and poaching

**Hunting** is killing wild animals for food (bushmeat), skins, horns, traditional medicine or sport. **Poaching** is illegal hunting.

**Effects on distribution:**

- **Reduces or wipes out populations** of hunted species. Elephants, lions, leopards, chimpanzees, gorillas, pangolins, crocodiles and many antelopes have become rare or have disappeared from large parts of Nigeria. Pangolins are trafficked for their scales.
- **Upsets food chains:** removing predators can lead to an increase in their prey; removing herbivores and seed-dispersing animals (such as monkeys and hornbills) affects the plants they feed on or spread.
- **Hunting with fire** (bush burning) and poison (in rivers) kills many other organisms as well.

Conservation measures (national parks, game reserves, laws against poaching) are covered in topic C7.

#### 9. Other human activities

- **Mining and quarrying:** remove vegetation and soil. Tin mining on the Jos Plateau left large areas of bare spoil heaps and mining ponds.
- **Introduction of new species:** humans have brought in plants and animals that spread and harm local species, such as the water hyacinth in southern waterways.
- **Conservation:** humans can also **improve** the distribution of organisms by planting trees (afforestation and reforestation, such as shelterbelts in the north), protecting habitats in national parks (Yankari Game Reserve, Kainji Lake National Park, Cross River National Park, Old Oyo National Park), and controlling pollution.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Habitat vs niche:** the habitat is where an organism lives (its address); the niche is its role or way of life (its profession). Do not mix them up.
- **Population vs community:** a population is **one species**; a community is **many species**. An ecosystem is a community **plus** its abiotic environment.
- **Abiotic vs biotic:** abiotic = non-living (temperature, rainfall, pH, soil, salinity). Biotic = living (predation, parasitism, competition, food, disease). Human activities are biotic factors, though they change abiotic conditions.
- **Secchi disc measures turbidity,** not depth of water, temperature or salinity. The Secchi depth is the **average** of the depth where the disc disappears and the depth where it reappears.
- **Six's thermometer** records the **maximum and minimum** temperatures; the steel indices are reset with a **magnet**.
- **Rain gauge reads in millimetres (mm),** and should be placed in an open space, away from trees and buildings.
- **Wet-and-dry bulb:** the bigger the difference between the readings, the **drier** the air (lower relative humidity). Equal readings mean the air is saturated (100%).
- **Wind vane:** the arrow points to the direction the wind is **coming from**, and winds are named by where they come from.
- **Anemometer = wind speed; hygrometer = humidity; hydrometer = density/salinity; barometer = pressure; altimeter = altitude.** These names are easy to confuse.
- **Temperature falls with altitude,** so plateaus (Jos, Mambilla, Obudu) are cool and support montane vegetation, temperate crops and cattle.
- **Rainfall decreases from the south to the north of Nigeria,** which is why vegetation changes from forest to savanna to semi-desert.
- **Clay holds most water; sand holds least; loam is the best for most crops.**

## Quick summary

1. Ecology is the study of organisms in relation to each other and their environment. Key terms: habitat (home), niche (role), population (one species), community (many species), ecosystem (community + abiotic environment), biosphere (all life on Earth).
2. Ecological factors are abiotic (non-living) and biotic (living). Each organism survives only within a range of tolerance for each factor; a factor in short supply is a limiting factor.
3. Temperature, rainfall, relative humidity, wind, altitude, salinity, turbidity, pH and soil (edaphic) conditions all decide where organisms can live. In Nigeria, rainfall (decreasing northwards) and altitude (cool plateaus) are especially important.
4. Biotic factors include food, predation, competition, parasitism, disease, mutualism and decomposition.
5. Plants and animals change their environment by shading, adding humus, grazing, trampling, burrowing, pollinating and dispersing seeds.
6. Human activities (deforestation, bush burning, farming, urbanisation, dams, pollution, overgrazing and hunting) have the greatest effect on the distribution of organisms, mostly by destroying habitats.
7. Abiotic factors are measured with instruments: the Secchi disc (turbidity), thermometer and Six's maximum–minimum thermometer (temperature), rain gauge (rainfall), hygrometer (humidity), wind vane and anemometer (wind), pH meter (pH), hydrometer or salinometer (salinity), light meter (light) and soil auger (soil samples).

## Practice questions

**1. A community together with its non-living environment is called**

- A. a population
- B. an ecosystem
- C. a habitat
- D. a niche

**Answer: B.** An ecosystem is the community (all the living organisms) plus the abiotic environment, interacting as one unit.

**2. The turbidity of a pond is best measured with**

- A. a hydrometer
- B. a hygrometer
- C. a Secchi disc
- D. an anemometer

**Answer: C.** The Secchi disc is lowered into the water until it disappears; the depth shows how cloudy (turbid) the water is and how far light penetrates.

**3. A Secchi disc disappeared from view at a depth of 85 cm and reappeared at 75 cm when it was raised. What is the Secchi depth?**

- A. 10 cm
- B. 75 cm
- C. 80 cm
- D. 160 cm

**Answer: C.** The Secchi depth is the average of the two readings: (85 + 75) ÷ 2 = 80 cm.

**4. In a Six's thermometer, the steel indices are reset by using**

- A. a magnet
- B. heat
- C. a pin
- D. distilled water

**Answer: A.** A small magnet is used to draw each steel index back down to the surface of the mercury after the maximum and minimum temperatures have been read.

**5. When the readings of the wet and dry bulb thermometers are the same, the relative humidity of the air is**

- A. 0%
- B. 25%
- C. 50%
- D. 100%

**Answer: D.** Equal readings mean no net evaporation is taking place from the wet bulb, so the air is saturated: 100% relative humidity.

**6. Irish potatoes and temperate vegetables grow well on the Jos Plateau mainly because of its**

- A. high salinity
- B. low temperature due to high altitude
- C. high turbidity
- D. sandy soil

**Answer: B.** Temperature falls with altitude, so the high Jos Plateau is cool enough for temperate crops.

**7. A rain gauge should be placed**

- A. under a tree, to protect it from wind
- B. close to a wall, to keep it steady
- C. in an open space, away from trees and buildings
- D. inside a Stevenson screen

**Answer: C.** Trees and buildings can shelter the gauge from rain or drip extra water into it, giving wrong readings. (The Stevenson screen is for thermometers, not rain gauges.)

**8. Mangrove trees can grow in the brackish creeks of the Niger Delta because they are adapted to high**

- A. altitude
- B. salinity
- C. turbidity only
- D. wind speed

**Answer: B.** Brackish water and the soils of mangrove swamps are salty; mangroves are halophytes, with special adaptations for coping with salt.

**9. Which of the following soils has the greatest water-holding capacity?**

- A. Sandy soil
- B. Gravel
- C. Loamy soil
- D. Clay soil

**Answer: D.** Clay soil has the smallest particles and tiny pore spaces, so it holds the most water (and is easily waterlogged).

**10. Which of the following human activities is most directly responsible for the spread of desert-like conditions in northern Nigeria?**

- A. Building of dams
- B. Overgrazing and cutting of trees for firewood
- C. Planting of shelterbelts
- D. Creation of game reserves

**Answer: B.** Overgrazing and tree-cutting remove plant cover, so the soil is exposed to wind erosion and desertification spreads. Shelterbelts and game reserves help to prevent it.
