## What is a drug?

A **drug** is any substance (other than ordinary food and water) which, when taken into the body, **changes the way the body or mind works**. Drugs can be swallowed, smoked, sniffed, chewed, injected or absorbed through the skin.

- **Medicinal drugs (medicines)** are used to **prevent, diagnose, treat or cure disease** or relieve pain, e.g. paracetamol, antibiotics, antimalarials and insulin.
- **Psychoactive (mind-altering) drugs** act on the **brain and nervous system**, changing mood, feelings, perception, thinking or behaviour. Most abused drugs are psychoactive. Some of them are also useful medicines when used correctly (e.g. morphine and codeine for pain, diazepam for anxiety and seizures).

Drugs can be:

- **Legal and freely available:** caffeine (coffee, tea, cola drinks), kolanut.
- **Legal but controlled by age or law:** alcohol and tobacco (sale to people under 18 is banned in Nigeria).
- **Prescription-only medicines:** legal only when prescribed and supplied by a pharmacist, e.g. tramadol, codeine-containing medicines, diazepam and other sedatives.
- **Illegal (illicit) drugs:** banned by law, e.g. cannabis, cocaine, heroin and methamphetamine.

## Defining drug abuse and addiction

**Objective iii: define drug abuse and addiction.**

### Drug use, drug misuse and drug abuse

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Drug use** | Taking a drug correctly for its proper purpose | Taking a full course of an antimalarial prescribed by a doctor |
| **Drug misuse** | Using a **medicine** wrongly, usually without the aim of getting "high": wrong dose, wrong time, wrong purpose, someone else's medicine, or without a prescription | Taking antibiotics left over from a friend; not completing a malaria treatment; taking extra painkillers |
| **Drug abuse** | **Deliberate** use of a drug for a **non-medical purpose**, or in amounts or ways that **harm** the user's health, mind or social life; includes any use of illegal drugs | Taking codeine cough syrup or tramadol to get "high"; smoking cannabis; heavy drinking |

**Definition: drug abuse** is the use of a drug in excess, without medical advice or for non-medical purposes (such as to get "high", stay awake or feel bold), in a way that damages the user's physical or mental health or social and economic well-being.

**Textbook variation:** many textbooks use "drug abuse" and "drug misuse" to mean the same thing. Others make the distinction above. The World Health Organization now prefers terms such as "harmful use" and "substance use disorder" rather than "drug abuse". For JAMB, learn the definition of drug abuse given above.

### Drug addiction (drug dependence)

**Definition: drug addiction** is a state in which a person has a **strong, uncontrollable craving** (compulsion) to keep taking a drug, and finds it very difficult to stop, **even though they know it is harming them**. The person's life becomes centred on getting and using the drug.

Addiction usually develops step by step:

1. **Experimental use:** a young person tries a drug out of curiosity or peer pressure.
2. **Occasional (social or recreational) use:** the drug is used at parties or with friends.
3. **Regular use:** the person begins to use the drug often, to cope with stress or boredom or to feel "normal".
4. **Tolerance develops:** larger doses are needed to get the same effect.
5. **Dependence and addiction:** the person feels unable to cope without the drug; stopping causes withdrawal symptoms; the drug takes priority over school, work, family and health.

**How addiction happens in the brain (simplified):** most abused drugs increase the release of the chemical messenger **dopamine** in the brain's **reward system**, the same system that makes eating and other normal pleasures feel good. The drug produces a much stronger "reward" than normal activities. With repeated use, the brain adapts: it becomes less sensitive to dopamine, normal pleasures feel dull, and the person needs the drug to feel normal. The parts of the brain that control judgement and self-control are also weakened. This is why addiction is regarded as a **disease of the brain**, not simply a lack of willpower, and why people need help to recover.

### Related terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Dependence** | A state in which the body or mind needs the drug to function normally. Often used to mean the same as addiction |
| **Physical (physiological) dependence** | The body has adapted to the drug, so **withdrawal symptoms** appear when it is stopped (common with alcohol, opioids such as heroin, codeine and tramadol, and barbiturates and other sedatives) |
| **Psychological dependence** | A strong emotional or mental need for the drug, to feel good, relaxed or confident (common with cannabis, cocaine and methamphetamine, but present with all addictive drugs) |
| **Tolerance** | The body becomes less responsive to the drug after repeated use, so **larger and larger doses** are needed to produce the same effect. This raises the risk of overdose |
| **Withdrawal (withdrawal syndrome)** | The unpleasant, sometimes dangerous, physical and mental symptoms that occur when a dependent person suddenly stops or reduces the drug, e.g. shaking, sweating, anxiety, vomiting, pains, sleeplessness, fits (convulsions) and hallucinations |
| **Overdose** | Taking so much of a drug that it causes serious poisoning, unconsciousness or death |
| **Psychoactive substance** | A substance that affects the mind (mood, thinking, perception, behaviour) |
| **Relapse** | Returning to drug use after a period of stopping |
| **Rehabilitation** | Treatment and support to help a person stop using drugs and return to a normal, productive life |

## Why people abuse drugs

Understanding the causes helps in prevention:

1. **Peer pressure:** wanting to be accepted by friends.
2. **Curiosity** and the desire to experiment.
3. **Escaping problems:** stress, poverty, unemployment, family conflict, trauma, depression or loneliness.
4. **Wrong beliefs:** that drugs improve performance, give courage or energy, help reading for exams, increase sexual performance or allow long hours of hard work (for example among some drivers, conductors, labourers and farm workers).
5. **Easy availability** of drugs and medicines sold without prescription by unlicensed patent medicine shops and street sellers.
6. **Poor parental care** and lack of supervision; parents or older siblings who abuse drugs themselves.
7. **Media influence**: music, films and social media that make drugs look exciting.
8. **Self-medication** that turns into dependence (for example, using painkillers for long periods).
9. **Idleness and lack of recreational facilities**.
10. **Mental health problems** that are not treated.

## Commonly abused drugs

**Objective iv: list the commonly abused drugs.**

### Classes of abused drugs

Psychoactive drugs are usually grouped by their main effect on the **central nervous system (CNS)**:

| Class | Main effect on the CNS | General effects | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Stimulants** | **Speed up** the activity of the nervous system | Alertness, wakefulness, energy, excitement, reduced appetite, fast heartbeat, raised blood pressure | Caffeine, nicotine, kolanut, cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamine |
| **Depressants (sedatives and hypnotics)** | **Slow down** the activity of the nervous system | Relaxation, sleepiness, reduced anxiety, slowed reactions, poor coordination; large doses slow breathing | Alcohol, barbiturates, benzodiazepines (e.g. diazepam), most inhalants |
| **Narcotics (opioids)** | Act on opioid receptors; **relieve pain** and depress the CNS | Pain relief, drowsiness, a dreamy feeling of well-being (euphoria), constipation, slowed breathing | Heroin, morphine, opium, codeine, tramadol, pentazocine |
| **Hallucinogens (psychedelics)** | **Distort perception** | Hallucinations (seeing or hearing things that are not there), altered sense of time and space, confused thinking | LSD, "magic mushrooms" (psilocybin), mescaline; cannabis has some hallucinogenic effects |

**Textbook variation:** classifications differ. **Cannabis** is placed by different textbooks among hallucinogens, depressants or in a class of its own, because it has mixed effects (relaxing, mood-changing and, in high doses, hallucinogenic). The word **narcotic** is used in medicine for opioids, but in law and everyday speech (e.g. "narcotic drugs" in the name of the NDLEA) it is used for all illegal drugs. Some textbooks list **inhalants** and **opioids** as separate classes. In the exam, follow the categories given in the question.

### Each commonly abused drug explained

#### Alcohol

- **What it is:** **ethanol** (ethyl alcohol, C₂H₅OH), produced when **yeast** ferments sugars (see respiration and fermentation in Section B). Beer, palm wine and burukutu contain a few per cent of alcohol; spirits such as gin, whisky and ogogoro contain much more (often around 40% or more).
- **How it acts:** a **depressant**. It slows the brain and spinal cord. Small amounts make a person feel relaxed and talkative (because it reduces self-control); larger amounts cause poor judgement, slurred speech, staggering, slow reactions, vomiting, unconsciousness and, in very large amounts, death from stopped breathing or choking on vomit (alcohol poisoning).
- **Why it is dangerous:** it is legal, cheap and socially accepted, so it is the most widely abused drug. Locally distilled spirits may contain **methanol**, which can cause blindness and death; Nigeria has had outbreaks of methanol poisoning from contaminated local gin.
- **Long-term effects:** liver damage (fatty liver, **alcoholic hepatitis** and **cirrhosis**), stomach ulcers and inflammation (gastritis), inflammation of the pancreas, high blood pressure, heart disease and stroke, brain damage and memory loss, several cancers (mouth, throat, oesophagus, liver, breast), malnutrition, weakened immunity, depression, and **alcohol dependence (alcoholism)**. Drinking in pregnancy can harm the baby (**fetal alcohol syndrome**, with brain damage and abnormal facial features).
- **Withdrawal:** shaking ("the shakes"), sweating, anxiety, sleeplessness and, in severe cases, fits and a dangerous state of confusion and hallucinations (delirium tremens).
- **Social effects:** drink-driving and road accidents, violence, domestic abuse, unprotected sex, crime, loss of jobs and money.

#### Tobacco and nicotine

- **What it is:** the dried leaves of the tobacco plant. Tobacco smoke contains thousands of chemicals, including **nicotine** (the addictive drug), **tar** (a mixture of chemicals, many of which cause cancer), **carbon monoxide** and irritant gases.
- **How it acts:** **nicotine** is a **stimulant**. It reaches the brain within seconds of inhaling, increases alertness, raises heart rate and blood pressure, and narrows blood vessels. It is **highly addictive**.
- **Effects of each component:**
  - **Nicotine:** addiction; raises heart rate and blood pressure; narrows blood vessels.
  - **Tar:** collects in the lungs; contains **carcinogens** (cancer-causing chemicals); paralyses and destroys the **cilia** in the airways, so mucus and dirt are not cleared.
  - **Carbon monoxide:** combines with **haemoglobin** in red blood cells, reducing the blood's ability to carry oxygen (see `C7c-pollution-and-sanitation.md`).
  - **Irritants:** cause coughing and inflammation of the airways.
- **Long-term effects:** **lung cancer** (most cases are caused by smoking), cancers of the mouth, throat, larynx, oesophagus, bladder and other organs; **chronic bronchitis** ("smoker's cough") and **emphysema** (destruction of the alveoli, causing breathlessness) together known as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); **heart attacks**, strokes and poor circulation; stained teeth and fingers, bad breath, gum disease, and reduced sense of taste and smell. Snuff and chewing tobacco cause mouth cancer and gum disease.
- **Smoking in pregnancy:** low birth weight, premature birth and miscarriage.
- **Passive (second-hand) smoking:** people who breathe in other people's smoke (especially children) suffer more chest infections, asthma, lung cancer and heart disease.
- **Shisha (hookah)** and **e-cigarettes (vapes)** are not safe alternatives: shisha smoke contains nicotine, carbon monoxide and tar, and one session can expose a person to a large amount of smoke; e-cigarettes deliver addictive nicotine.
- **Withdrawal:** irritability, craving, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, increased appetite.
- **Law in Nigeria:** the National Tobacco Control Act of 2015 bans smoking in many public places, the sale of tobacco to people under 18, and most tobacco advertising.

#### Cannabis (Indian hemp, igbo)

- **What it is:** the dried leaves, flowering tops and resin of the plant *Cannabis sativa* (Indian hemp). The main psychoactive chemical is **THC** (tetrahydrocannabinol). Cannabis resin is called hashish. In Nigeria it is called **igbo** (in Yoruba) and **wee-wee**, among other names, and is the **most commonly used illegal drug** in the country. Its cultivation, sale and possession are illegal in Nigeria.
- **How it acts:** mixed effects: relaxation, euphoria ("high"), talkativeness or silliness, increased appetite, altered sense of time, sight and sound, red eyes and dry mouth. High doses (especially of strong modern varieties) can cause hallucinations, anxiety, panic and paranoia.
- **Short-term effects:** poor memory, poor concentration and slow reaction time (dangerous when driving or operating machines), lack of coordination, fast heartbeat, anxiety and panic attacks.
- **Long-term effects:**
  - **Mental illness:** cannabis use, especially heavy use starting in the teenage years, increases the risk of **psychosis** (losing touch with reality, hearing voices, paranoia) and **schizophrenia** in vulnerable people. In Nigerian psychiatric hospitals, cannabis is one of the drugs most often linked with drug-related psychosis.
  - **Poor learning and school performance**, memory problems, and loss of motivation.
  - **Lung damage** from smoking (bronchitis), as cannabis smoke contains tar.
  - **Dependence** in a proportion of regular users.
  - Use with tobacco or alcohol adds their harms.
- **Social effects:** dropping out of school, crime, violence and road accidents. Mixtures sold in some places (such as cannabis soaked in local gin) combine its harms with those of alcohol.

#### Cocaine

- **What it is:** a white powder made from the leaves of the **coca** plant, which grows in South America. "**Crack**" is a form of cocaine that can be smoked. Nigeria is mainly a transit country for cocaine trafficking, but local use also occurs.
- **How it acts:** a powerful **stimulant**. It blocks the reabsorption of dopamine in the brain, producing intense but short-lived euphoria, energy, confidence and alertness, followed by a "crash" of tiredness and depression.
- **Effects:** very fast heartbeat, high blood pressure, raised body temperature, dilated pupils, loss of appetite and sleeplessness. It can cause **heart attacks**, irregular heartbeat, **strokes** and fits, even in young people and on first use. Sniffing damages the lining of the nose (and can make a hole in the septum). Long-term: anxiety, paranoia, aggression and psychosis, weight loss, and strong **psychological dependence**. Injecting spreads **HIV** and **hepatitis**.
- **Drug trafficking:** people who swallow packets of cocaine or heroin to smuggle them ("drug mules") can die if a packet bursts inside them.

#### Heroin

- **What it is:** a powerful **opioid** made from **morphine**, which is extracted from **opium**, the dried milky juice of the unripe seed pods of the **opium poppy**. It is a white or brown powder.
- **How it acts:** a **narcotic depressant**. It acts on the brain's **opioid receptors**, producing a rush of pleasure, then drowsiness, warmth and freedom from pain and worry.
- **Effects:** drowsiness, slowed breathing, constricted (pinpoint) pupils, nausea, constipation and itching. It is **extremely addictive**, with rapid **tolerance** and severe **physical dependence**.
- **Dangers:** **overdose** causes breathing to slow and stop, leading to coma and death. Injecting with shared needles spreads **HIV**, **hepatitis B and C** and causes abscesses, collapsed veins and blood infections. Users neglect food and health, and often turn to crime to pay for the drug.
- **Withdrawal:** craving, restlessness, muscle and bone pain, sweating, runny nose and eyes, goose pimples, diarrhoea and vomiting, sleeplessness. Opioid withdrawal is very unpleasant, although rarely life-threatening in otherwise healthy adults.
- **Treatment:** the drug **naloxone** can reverse an opioid overdose if given quickly.

#### Tramadol

- **What it is:** a synthetic **opioid painkiller**, prescribed for moderate to severe pain. Legitimate tablets and capsules are usually 50 mg or 100 mg.
- **Abuse in Nigeria:** tramadol has been one of the most widely abused drugs in Nigeria, especially in the north and among young people, commercial drivers, labourers and students. High-strength tablets (for example 200 mg, 225 mg and higher), which are not approved for medical use in Nigeria, have been smuggled into the country and sold without prescription. People take it believing it gives energy, courage, the ability to work long hours or better sexual performance. NAFDAC has restricted the strength of tramadol that may be imported and sold, and the NDLEA and NAFDAC regularly seize large quantities.
- **Effects of abuse:** drowsiness, dizziness, nausea, constipation; **fits (seizures)**, even in people without epilepsy, especially at high doses; slowed breathing and death in overdose (especially when mixed with alcohol or other depressants); **dependence** and withdrawal (anxiety, sweating, sleeplessness, pain, diarrhoea); and serious interactions with some other medicines.
- **Category:** narcotic (opioid).

#### Codeine cough syrup

- **What it is:** **codeine** is an opioid found naturally in opium (it is weaker than morphine). It is used in small doses in some cough medicines and painkillers. In the body, some codeine is converted into morphine.
- **Abuse in Nigeria:** codeine-containing cough syrups were abused on a very large scale in Nigeria, especially among young people in the north, where people drank them straight from the bottle or mixed with soft drinks. After public concern (including a widely viewed 2018 investigative documentary), the **Federal Government banned the importation of codeine for use in cough syrups in 2018**, and NAFDAC and the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria withdrew the registration of codeine cough syrups. Illegal trade still continues.
- **Effects of abuse:** drowsiness, slurred speech, constipation, slowed breathing; **dependence**; withdrawal symptoms; damage to **kidneys and liver** with long use (also from other ingredients); and mental problems including **psychosis**. Some syrups also contain **promethazine** (an antihistamine that causes heavy sedation), so mixing them with alcohol or other depressants can stop breathing.
- **Category:** narcotic (opioid).

#### Methamphetamine ("mkpuru mmiri")

- **What it is:** a powerful synthetic **stimulant**, related to amphetamine, often in the form of clear crystals (crystal meth, "ice"). In Nigeria it is widely known by its Igbo name **"mkpuru mmiri"**, and its abuse became a serious problem in the south-east and other parts of the country from around 2021. The NDLEA has discovered and destroyed illegal laboratories making it in Nigeria. Local communities, traditional rulers and church groups in the south-east have campaigned against it.
- **How it acts:** it causes a very large release of dopamine in the brain, producing intense euphoria, energy, confidence and wakefulness that can last many hours. It is **very addictive**.
- **Effects:** sleeplessness for days, loss of appetite and severe **weight loss**, fast heartbeat, high blood pressure and raised body temperature; **aggression and violence**; **paranoia, hallucinations and psychosis**, sometimes long-lasting (users have been brought to psychiatric hospitals with severe mental illness); severe tooth decay ("meth mouth"); skin sores from picking at the skin; brain damage; heart attack and stroke. Users may commit crimes, including violence against family members.
- **Category:** stimulant.

#### Amphetamines

- **What they are:** synthetic **stimulant** drugs. Some are legitimate medicines prescribed under strict control for specific conditions (such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and narcolepsy). Abused amphetamines are called "speed", "pep pills" or "uppers".
- **Why they are abused:** to stay awake (some students take them to read through the night before exams, and some drivers to drive long distances), to feel energetic and confident, or to lose weight.
- **Effects:** sleeplessness, restlessness, loss of appetite, fast heartbeat, high blood pressure, dilated pupils, irritability and aggression; after the effect wears off, a "crash" with tiredness and depression. Heavy use causes **paranoia and psychosis**, heart problems and dependence. Using stimulants to read for exams harms memory and judgement and does **not** improve results.
- **Category:** stimulant.

#### Barbiturates and other sedatives

- **What they are:** **depressant** drugs that calm the nervous system and cause sleep. **Barbiturates** (e.g. phenobarbitone) were once widely used as sleeping pills and are still used to treat epilepsy. They have mostly been replaced as sedatives by **benzodiazepines** (e.g. diazepam), which are safer but still addictive. Both are prescription-only medicines but are sometimes sold illegally.
- **Why they are abused:** to calm down, to sleep, to "come down" after stimulants, or mixed with alcohol or cannabis for a stronger effect.
- **Effects:** drowsiness, slurred speech, poor coordination, confusion and memory loss. **Overdose** (especially of barbiturates, or any sedative mixed with **alcohol** or opioids) slows breathing and can cause coma and death. Tolerance and **physical dependence** develop; suddenly stopping after heavy use can cause dangerous **withdrawal fits**, so stopping must be medically supervised.
- **Category:** depressant.

#### Inhalants and solvents

- **What they are:** household and industrial products that give off vapours: glue, petrol, kerosene, paint thinners, correction fluid, nail-polish remover, lighter gas and aerosol sprays. They are cheap and easy to obtain, so they are often abused by **children and street children**. They are sniffed from a container, a soaked cloth or a bag.
- **How they act:** mostly **depressants**. They are absorbed quickly through the lungs and reach the brain within seconds, producing a short "high" like drunkenness, with dizziness, giddiness, and sometimes hallucinations.
- **Effects:** headache, nausea, slurred speech, loss of coordination, unconsciousness. They can cause **sudden death** (even on the first use) by making the heart beat irregularly or by suffocation. Long-term: damage to the **brain** (memory loss, poor concentration), **nerves**, **liver**, **kidneys**, bone marrow and lungs; sores around the nose and mouth.
- **Category:** mostly depressant.

#### Caffeine

- **What it is:** a mild **stimulant** found naturally in **coffee**, **tea**, **cocoa** (in small amounts) and **kola nuts**, and added to cola drinks, **energy drinks** and some painkiller tablets.
- **How it acts:** it blocks a brain chemical (adenosine) that causes tiredness, so it increases **alertness** and reduces sleepiness. It also increases urine production (a diuretic effect).
- **Effects of moderate use:** generally safe for most healthy adults.
- **Effects of excessive use (abuse):** sleeplessness (insomnia), restlessness, anxiety and nervousness, headache, **fast or irregular heartbeat**, stomach upset, frequent urination, and shaky hands. Students who use large amounts of coffee, energy drinks or caffeine tablets to stay awake for exams may become exhausted and perform worse. Mixing energy drinks with alcohol is dangerous, because the person feels less drunk than they are.
- **Dependence:** regular heavy users can develop mild dependence, with **withdrawal headaches**, tiredness and irritability when they stop.
- **Category:** stimulant (mild).

#### Kolanut

- **What it is:** the seeds of the kola trees *Cola nitida* and *Cola acuminata*, grown widely in southern Nigeria and traded and chewed throughout the country. Kolanut is culturally very important: it is presented to welcome guests and at ceremonies such as weddings and naming ceremonies (for example, among the Igbo, the breaking of kola is a respected custom). It is chewed heavily in the north.
- **Active ingredients:** **caffeine** and **theobromine** (another mild stimulant, also found in cocoa).
- **Why it is abused:** to stay awake (by students, drivers, night guards and long-distance travellers), to reduce hunger and tiredness during hard work.
- **Effects of excessive use:** sleeplessness, restlessness, fast heartbeat, raised blood pressure, stomach irritation, stained teeth, and mild dependence.
- **Category:** stimulant (mild).

**Note:** kolanut and caffeine are legal and are normally used in small amounts without harm. They are included in the syllabus list because **excessive** use for their stimulant effect is a form of drug abuse.

## Effects of drug abuse on human health

**Objective v: state the effects of drug abuse on human health.**

### Effects on the body systems

| Body system or organ | Effects of drug abuse | Main drugs responsible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Brain and nervous system** | Addiction; damaged memory and learning; poor judgement and coordination; fits (seizures); brain damage; stroke | All psychoactive drugs; alcohol, inhalants, tramadol, methamphetamine, cocaine |
| **Mental health** | Anxiety, depression, paranoia, hallucinations, **psychosis** and schizophrenia; suicidal thoughts | Cannabis, methamphetamine, cocaine, amphetamines, alcohol, codeine syrups |
| **Breathing system** | **Lung cancer**, chronic bronchitis, emphysema; **slowed or stopped breathing** in overdose | Tobacco, cannabis (smoking); opioids, sedatives, alcohol (overdose); inhalants |
| **Heart and blood vessels** | Fast or irregular heartbeat, high blood pressure, **heart attack**, stroke | Cocaine, methamphetamine, amphetamines, nicotine, alcohol, caffeine in excess |
| **Liver** | Fatty liver, hepatitis, **cirrhosis**, liver cancer | Alcohol; also some cough syrups and solvents; hepatitis viruses from shared needles |
| **Kidneys** | Kidney damage and failure | Inhalants, codeine syrups and other drugs in heavy use; dehydration from stimulants |
| **Digestive system** | Stomach ulcers, gastritis, loss of appetite, malnutrition, constipation | Alcohol, opioids, stimulants |
| **Mouth and teeth** | Mouth cancer, gum disease, tooth decay | Tobacco, methamphetamine, kolanut in excess |
| **Immune system** | Weakened immunity; infections such as **HIV** and **hepatitis B and C** from shared needles and risky sex | Injected drugs; all drugs that impair judgement |
| **Reproductive system and pregnancy** | Reduced fertility; miscarriage, low birth weight, birth defects; babies born dependent on drugs | Alcohol (fetal alcohol syndrome), tobacco, opioids, cocaine |
| **Whole body** | **Overdose** and death; accidents and injuries; weight loss | All |

### General health effects

1. **Addiction (dependence)** and withdrawal symptoms.
2. **Overdose and death**, especially with opioids, sedatives, alcohol and inhalants, and when drugs are mixed.
3. **Mental illness**: drug-induced psychosis is one of the common reasons young people are admitted to psychiatric hospitals in Nigeria.
4. **Damage to vital organs**: brain, lungs, heart, liver and kidneys.
5. **Infectious diseases**: HIV, hepatitis and other STIs from sharing needles and from risky sexual behaviour (see `C7a-diseases.md`).
6. **Malnutrition** and weight loss, as drugs reduce appetite and money is spent on drugs instead of food.
7. **Injuries** from accidents, falls and violence.
8. **Harm to unborn babies**.

## Effects of drug abuse on the family and society

### Effects on the individual and family

- **Poor academic performance**, truancy, expulsion and **dropping out of school**.
- Loss of jobs and income; **poverty**.
- **Family conflict**, domestic violence, neglect and abuse of children, divorce and broken homes.
- Loss of respect and trust; stigma.
- Money and property stolen or sold by the user to buy drugs.
- Children of drug users may grow up neglected and copy the habit.

### Effects on society

1. **Crime**: theft, armed robbery, kidnapping, cultism, and violence, often committed under the influence of drugs or to get money for drugs.
2. **Road accidents**: drivers under the influence of alcohol, cannabis, tramadol or other drugs cause many crashes.
3. **Insecurity**: drug trafficking finances criminal gangs, and drug use has been linked with violent crime and with the activities of armed groups.
4. **Spread of HIV and other diseases**.
5. **Loss of productivity**: a large proportion of drug users are young people who should be studying or working; this harms the economy.
6. **Burden on health services**, rehabilitation centres and families who must care for people with drug-related illness.
7. **Drug trafficking** damages Nigeria's international image, and Nigerians are often subjected to extra checks at foreign airports.
8. **Corruption**, as traffickers bribe officials.
9. **Breakdown of moral and social values**.

**The scale of the problem in Nigeria:** the first large national survey of drug use (2018, carried out by the National Bureau of Statistics and the Centre for Research and Information on Substance Abuse, with the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime) found that about **14.4%** of Nigerians aged 15 to 64, roughly **14 million people**, had used at least one psychoactive drug (not counting alcohol and tobacco) in the previous year. This is much higher than the global average. **Cannabis** was the most commonly used drug, followed by the **non-medical use of opioid medicines** such as **tramadol**, **codeine** and morphine. About one in every five users was estimated to suffer from drug use disorders (dependence), and many could not get treatment.

## Prevention of drug abuse

**Content item b.iii: prevention of drug abuse.**

Preventing drug abuse needs action by **individuals, families, schools, communities, religious bodies, the government and international organisations**. It involves **reducing the supply** of drugs (stopping production and trafficking), **reducing the demand** for drugs (education and support), and **treating and rehabilitating** people who are already dependent.

### The role of the individual

1. **Say no** to drugs, and learn how to resist peer pressure: give a clear refusal, suggest a different activity, or walk away.
2. Choose **good friends** and positive role models.
3. Get **accurate information** about drugs and their effects.
4. Take medicines **only as prescribed**; never use other people's prescription medicines; buy medicines only from registered pharmacies.
5. Find healthy ways to cope with stress: sports, music, hobbies, religious activities, talking to trusted adults.
6. **Seek help early** if you are using drugs, from a parent, teacher, counsellor, doctor or religious leader.

### The role of the family

1. Parents should **teach children** about the dangers of drugs early, and set a **good example** (not abusing alcohol, tobacco or other drugs themselves).
2. Provide **love, care, supervision** and open communication, so children can discuss problems.
3. Know their children's **friends** and activities.
4. Store medicines safely and avoid giving children unprescribed medicines.
5. Watch for **warning signs** (sudden change in behaviour or friends, falling grades, red eyes, missing money, secrecy, loss of interest in activities) and seek help early, without violence or rejection.

### The role of schools

1. **Drug education** in the curriculum (for example, in biology, health education, civic education and religious studies).
2. **Guidance and counselling** services.
3. Anti-drug clubs, debates, drama and talks by NDLEA officers and health workers; observing the **International Day against Drug Abuse and Illicit Trafficking** on **26 June** every year.
4. **Sports, games and clubs** that keep students busy and give them a sense of achievement.
5. Clear school rules against drugs, applied fairly, with support for students who need help.

### The role of religious and community organisations and the media

1. Religious bodies, traditional rulers and community leaders teach moral values and discourage drug use; some run rehabilitation programmes.
2. Community vigilance against drug dealers and illegal laboratories, and reporting them to the authorities.
3. Youth programmes, skills acquisition and job creation, which reduce idleness and poverty.
4. Responsible **media**: anti-drug campaigns on radio, television and social media; not glamorising drugs.

### The role of government and its agencies

#### National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA)

The **NDLEA** was established in **1989** (by Decree No. 48 of 1989, now the NDLEA Act) as Nigeria's main agency for fighting illegal drugs.

**Functions:**

1. **Enforcing drug laws**: arresting and prosecuting people who cultivate, produce, traffic, sell or possess illegal drugs.
2. **Seizing and destroying** illegal drugs, destroying cannabis farms, and dismantling illegal drug laboratories (such as those making methamphetamine).
3. **Controlling drug trafficking** at airports, seaports and land borders, working with international agencies such as the UNODC, Interpol and foreign drug agencies.
4. **Tracing and confiscating** money and property gained from drug trafficking.
5. **Drug demand reduction**: public enlightenment campaigns in schools and communities, and the "War Against Drug Abuse" (WADA) advocacy campaign.
6. **Counselling, treatment and rehabilitation** of drug users through its own centres and by referral.
7. Cooperating with NAFDAC to control the illegal sale of pharmaceutical drugs such as tramadol and codeine.

#### National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC)

**NAFDAC** was established in **1993** (by Decree No. 15 of 1993, now the NAFDAC Act) to regulate and control food, drugs, cosmetics, medical devices, chemicals and packaged water.

**Functions related to drug abuse:**

1. **Registering** and approving drugs before they can be manufactured, imported, sold or advertised in Nigeria (the NAFDAC registration number on a product shows it has been approved).
2. **Controlling the import and distribution** of **controlled medicines** such as opioids (codeine, tramadol, morphine) and sedatives, and limiting their strength (e.g. restrictions on high-dose tramadol and the withdrawal of codeine cough syrups).
3. **Fighting fake, substandard and smuggled drugs**: inspecting factories, markets and pharmacies; seizing and destroying illegal products.
4. **Regulating advertisements** of drugs, alcohol and tobacco.
5. **Public enlightenment** on safe use of medicines and the dangers of drug abuse.
6. Working with the **NDLEA**, the **Pharmacists Council of Nigeria** (which licenses pharmacies and patent medicine stores) and the police.

#### Other government actions

1. **Laws** that control or ban drugs and prescribe penalties for trafficking; laws on the sale of alcohol and tobacco to minors (e.g. the National Tobacco Control Act, 2015).
2. **Ministry of Health** programmes on drug demand reduction, treatment and mental health; **psychiatric (neuropsychiatric) hospitals** that treat drug dependence.
3. **Creating jobs** and opportunities for young people.
4. **Taxes** on alcohol and tobacco to discourage use.
5. **International cooperation** through the **United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC)**, the WHO and international drug control treaties.

### Treatment and rehabilitation

People who are already dependent on drugs need **treatment**, not just punishment. **Rehabilitation** helps them to stop using drugs, recover their health and return to a normal life.

**Steps in treatment and rehabilitation:**

1. **Recognition and willingness**: the person (often helped by family) accepts that there is a problem and seeks help.
2. **Assessment** by doctors and counsellors to find out which drugs are used, how much, and what physical and mental health problems exist.
3. **Detoxification**: supervised withdrawal from the drug in a hospital or treatment centre, with medicines to ease withdrawal symptoms and prevent dangerous complications (e.g. fits from alcohol or sedative withdrawal).
4. **Treatment of other illnesses**: mental illness, infections, malnutrition.
5. **Counselling and therapy**: individual and group counselling and behavioural therapy to understand why the person used drugs, how to handle cravings, triggers and peer pressure, and how to cope with stress. Family therapy helps repair relationships.
6. **Skills acquisition and education**: learning a trade or returning to school, so the person has a purpose and income.
7. **Reintegration** into the family and community without stigma.
8. **After-care and support groups**: regular follow-up and support groups to prevent **relapse**.

In Nigeria, treatment is offered by federal **neuropsychiatric hospitals** (for example at Yaba in Lagos and Aro in Abeokuta, among others), psychiatric departments of teaching hospitals, NDLEA counselling and rehabilitation centres, and some state, religious and private centres.

**Note:** people who are dependent on drugs should not be chained, beaten or locked up, which is still done in some unregulated "prayer camps" and "rehabilitation homes". Drug dependence is a health condition that needs proper, humane treatment.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Drug | Any substance, other than food and water, that changes the way the body or mind works |
| Psychoactive drug | A drug that affects the brain, changing mood, perception or behaviour |
| Drug misuse | Using a medicine wrongly (wrong dose, purpose or person) |
| Drug abuse | Use of a drug in excess, without medical advice or for non-medical purposes, in a way that harms the user |
| Drug addiction | A compulsive craving for a drug and inability to stop, despite harm |
| Dependence | A state in which the body or mind needs a drug to function normally |
| Physical dependence | Dependence in which stopping the drug causes physical withdrawal symptoms |
| Psychological dependence | An emotional or mental need for a drug |
| Tolerance | Reduced response to a drug, so larger doses are needed for the same effect |
| Withdrawal symptoms | Unpleasant physical and mental symptoms when a dependent person stops a drug |
| Overdose | Taking a dangerous amount of a drug |
| Stimulant | A drug that speeds up the nervous system, e.g. caffeine, cocaine, methamphetamine |
| Depressant | A drug that slows down the nervous system, e.g. alcohol, barbiturates |
| Narcotic (opioid) | A pain-relieving drug that acts on opioid receptors and depresses the nervous system, e.g. heroin, codeine, tramadol |
| Hallucinogen | A drug that distorts perception and causes hallucinations, e.g. LSD; cannabis in high doses |
| Hallucination | Seeing, hearing or feeling things that are not really there |
| Psychosis | A serious mental state in which a person loses touch with reality |
| Mkpuru mmiri | A Nigerian (Igbo) name for crystal methamphetamine |
| Detoxification | Supervised removal of a drug from the body during withdrawal |
| Rehabilitation | Treatment and support to help a person recover from drug dependence and return to normal life |
| Relapse | Returning to drug use after stopping |
| NDLEA | National Drug Law Enforcement Agency: enforces drug laws and reduces drug demand |
| NAFDAC | National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control: regulates food, drugs and chemicals |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Drug abuse vs addiction:** abuse is the **harmful, non-medical use** of a drug; addiction is the **state of compulsive dependence** that can result from abuse.
- **Tolerance is not the same as withdrawal:** tolerance means needing **more** of the drug for the same effect; withdrawal is the illness felt when the drug is **stopped**.
- **Alcohol is a depressant**, even though small amounts make people lively; it slows the nervous system and reduces self-control.
- **Nicotine, caffeine, kolanut, cocaine, amphetamines and methamphetamine are stimulants.**
- **Heroin, morphine, codeine and tramadol are narcotics (opioids)**; their overdose kills by **stopping breathing**.
- **Cannabis** is the most commonly abused illegal drug in Nigeria; it is usually classed as a **hallucinogen** in JAMB-type questions, but some books call it a depressant.
- **Carbon monoxide** in cigarette smoke reduces oxygen transport by combining with haemoglobin; **tar** causes cancer and damages cilia; **nicotine** causes addiction.
- **NDLEA** fights illegal drugs and trafficking; **NAFDAC** regulates and registers foods and medicines (including controlling tramadol and codeine). Do not mix them up.
- **26 June** is the International Day against Drug Abuse and Illicit Trafficking.
- **Rehabilitation**, not just punishment, is needed for people who are dependent on drugs.

## Quick summary

1. A drug is any substance that changes the way the body or mind works; drug abuse is the use of a drug in excess, without medical advice or for non-medical purposes, in a way that causes harm.
2. Drug addiction (dependence) is a compulsive need for a drug despite harm; it involves tolerance and, on stopping, withdrawal symptoms.
3. Commonly abused drugs include alcohol, tobacco, cannabis (Indian hemp), cocaine, heroin, tramadol, codeine cough syrup, methamphetamine ("mkpuru mmiri"), amphetamines, sedatives, inhalants, and caffeine and kolanut in excess.
4. Drugs are grouped as stimulants (e.g. cocaine, nicotine, caffeine), depressants (e.g. alcohol, barbiturates), narcotics or opioids (e.g. heroin, codeine, tramadol) and hallucinogens (e.g. LSD, cannabis).
5. Drug abuse damages the brain, mental health, lungs, heart, liver and kidneys, spreads HIV and hepatitis, and can cause death by overdose.
6. It also causes school dropout, family breakdown, crime, road accidents, insecurity and loss of productivity.
7. Drug abuse is prevented by education, good parenting, positive peer groups, law enforcement by the NDLEA, regulation of medicines by NAFDAC, and treatment and rehabilitation of people who are dependent.

## Practice questions

**1. Drug abuse can best be defined as**

- A. the use of drugs prescribed by a doctor
- B. the use of a drug without medical advice or for non-medical purposes, in a harmful way
- C. the production of drugs in a factory
- D. the use of any herbal medicine

**Answer: B.** Drug abuse is the non-medical, excessive or harmful use of a drug, without proper medical advice.

**2. A person who needs larger and larger doses of a drug to obtain the same effect has developed**

- A. withdrawal
- B. immunity
- C. tolerance
- D. hallucination

**Answer: C.** Tolerance means the body has become less responsive to the drug, so higher doses are needed for the same effect.

**3. Which of the following is a stimulant?**

- A. Cocaine
- B. Heroin
- C. Alcohol
- D. Barbiturate

**Answer: A.** Cocaine speeds up the nervous system. Heroin is an opioid narcotic; alcohol and barbiturates are depressants.

**4. The substance in cigarette smoke that combines with haemoglobin and reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood is**

- A. nicotine
- B. tar
- C. carbon monoxide
- D. caffeine

**Answer: C.** Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin, forming carboxyhaemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried. Nicotine causes addiction; tar causes cancer.

**5. Tramadol and codeine are both commonly abused in Nigeria. They belong to the group of drugs known as**

- A. hallucinogens
- B. stimulants
- C. inhalants
- D. narcotics (opioids)

**Answer: D.** Tramadol and codeine are opioid painkillers (narcotics); overdose can depress breathing.

**6. "Mkpuru mmiri", a drug widely abused in parts of Nigeria, is a local name for**

- A. methamphetamine
- B. cannabis
- C. codeine syrup
- D. local gin

**Answer: A.** Mkpuru mmiri is crystal methamphetamine, a powerful stimulant that can cause psychosis and violent behaviour.

**7. Which of the following organs is most damaged by long-term heavy drinking of alcohol?**

- A. Lungs
- B. Liver
- C. Eyes
- D. Skin

**Answer: B.** Alcohol is broken down in the liver; heavy drinking causes fatty liver, alcoholic hepatitis and cirrhosis.

**8. The unpleasant symptoms such as shaking, sweating and anxiety that occur when a dependent person suddenly stops taking a drug are called**

- A. side effects
- B. tolerance
- C. withdrawal symptoms
- D. allergy

**Answer: C.** Withdrawal symptoms occur when the body, which has adapted to the drug, no longer receives it.

**9. The agency in Nigeria mainly responsible for arresting drug traffickers and destroying illegal drugs is the**

- A. NAFDAC
- B. NDLEA
- C. NCDC
- D. NESREA

**Answer: B.** The National Drug Law Enforcement Agency enforces Nigeria's drug laws. NAFDAC regulates foods and medicines; NCDC controls diseases; NESREA enforces environmental standards.

**10. Which of the following is NOT a good way to prevent drug abuse among young people?**

- A. Drug education in schools
- B. Good parental care and supervision
- C. Buying medicines without a prescription from street sellers
- D. Engaging in sports and youth activities

**Answer: C.** Buying unprescribed medicines from unlicensed sellers encourages misuse and abuse; the other options help prevent drug abuse.
