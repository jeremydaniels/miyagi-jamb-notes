## Elements, compounds and mixtures

All matter in the universe can be classified into elements, compounds, and mixtures.

### Distinguishing between elements, compounds and mixtures

**1. Elements**
An **element** is a substance that cannot be split into simpler substances by any known chemical reaction. It consists of only one type of atom. 
- **Examples:** Iron ($\text{Fe}$), Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$), Gold ($\text{Au}$), Carbon ($\text{C}$). 
- In Nigeria, elements are mined in various regions, such as gold in Zamfara State and lead in Ebonyi State.

**2. Compounds**
A **compound** is a substance containing two or more elements that are **chemically combined** in a fixed ratio by mass. The properties of a compound are entirely different from the properties of the elements that make it up. Compounds can only be separated into their constituent elements by chemical methods (like electrolysis), not by physical methods.
- **Examples:** Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), Sodium chloride/table salt ($\text{NaCl}$), Calcium trioxocarbonate(IV)/limestone ($\text{CaCO}_3$). 
- Limestone, mined in Ogun State to make cement, is a common chemical compound.

**3. Mixtures**
A **mixture** contains two or more substances (elements or compounds) that are **physically combined**, not chemically combined. The components of a mixture keep their own individual properties and can be mixed in any proportion. Mixtures can be easily separated by physical methods.
- **Examples:** Air (a mixture of gases like nitrogen, oxygen, and carbon(IV) oxide), seawater (water and dissolved salts), soil, crude oil, and brass (an alloy of copper and zinc).

| Feature | Elements | Compounds | Mixtures |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Composition** | Made of one type of atom. | Made of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio. | Made of two or more substances physically mixed in any ratio. |
| **Properties** | Retains its fundamental atomic properties. | Entirely different from the properties of its constituent elements. | Retains the properties of its individual components. |
| **Separation** | Cannot be broken down further chemically. | Can only be separated by chemical methods (e.g., electrolysis). | Can be separated easily by physical methods (e.g., filtration). |
| **Energy Change** | N/A | Heat or light is usually given out or absorbed during formation. | No significant heat or light is given out or absorbed during mixing. |

**Common mistakes in classification:**
- *Wrong working/thinking:* Assuming air is a compound because it has a relatively constant composition.
- *Corrected version:* Air is a mixture. The gases $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$ are physically mixed, not chemically bonded, and can be separated by fractional distillation of liquid air.

## Chemical and physical changes

Changes in matter are classified into two types based on whether a new substance is formed.

### Differentiating between chemical and physical changes

**1. Physical Changes**
A **physical change** is a change in which **no new chemical substance is formed**. It is usually a change in the physical state, shape, or size of the substance, and it is easily reversible. Mass is strictly conserved without forming any new molecules.
- **Examples:** 
  - Melting of ice into water: $\text{H}_2\text{O(s)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$.
  - Dissolving table salt ($\text{NaCl}$) in water.
  - Sublimation of camphor.
  - Grating cassava (the physical shape changes, but it is still cassava).

**2. Chemical Changes**
A **chemical change** is a change in which **one or more entirely new substances are formed**. It involves a chemical reaction and is usually permanent (irreversible) under normal conditions. Heat is often strongly absorbed or released.
- **Examples:** 
  - Rusting of iron roofs (iron reacts with oxygen and moisture to form hydrated iron(III) oxide).
  - Burning of firewood.
  - Souring of palm wine (bacteria oxidise the ethanol in the wine to ethanoic (acetic) acid, a new substance).
  - Digestion of food.

| Feature | Physical Change | Chemical Change |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **New Substance** | No new substance is formed. | One or more new substances are formed. |
| **Reversibility** | Usually easily reversible (e.g., by freezing or heating). | Usually irreversible (cannot be easily reversed). |
| **Mass Change** | Mass of the specific substance remains exactly the same. | Mass of individual reacting substances changes as they convert. |
| **Energy Change** | Very little heat is absorbed or given off. | A large amount of heat or light is usually absorbed or given off. |

## Pure and impure substances

In chemistry, the word "pure" has a very strict meaning, different from its everyday use.

### Distinguishing between pure and impure substances

- **Pure Substances:** A pure substance is one that consists of only one kind of matter—either a single element or a single compound. It has exactly the same composition throughout (homogeneous) and has fixed, constant physical properties (such as a specific boiling point and melting point). Examples include pure distilled water, pure oxygen gas, and pure sodium chloride crystals.
- **Impure Substances:** An impure substance is a **mixture**. It contains a main substance mixed with one or more other substances (impurities). Because the composition can vary, an impure substance does not have fixed physical properties. 

| Feature | Pure Substance | Impure Substance (Mixture) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Composition** | Consists of only one type of element or compound. | Consists of two or more substances. |
| **Properties** | Sharp, specific physical properties (e.g., melts exactly at one temperature). | Variable properties depending on the amount of impurity. |
| **Chromatogram**| Gives only one spot on a paper chromatogram. | Gives multiple spots on a paper chromatogram. |

## Boiling, density, freezing and melting points

Chemists cannot always tell if a substance is pure just by looking at it. They must measure its physical properties quantitatively.

### Using boiling and melting points as criteria for purity of chemical substances

The most reliable criteria for testing the purity of a substance are its melting point, boiling point, and density.

**1. Melting and Freezing Points**
- **Pure solid:** Melts sharply at one specific temperature. For example, pure ice melts exactly at $0.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
- **Impure solid (Effect of Impurities):** The presence of an impurity **lowers the melting point** (melting point depression) and causes the solid to melt slowly over a **broad range of temperatures**. 

**2. Boiling Point**
- **Pure liquid:** Boils steadily at one specific temperature. For example, pure water boils exactly at $100.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at $1\text{ atm}$ pressure.
- **Impure liquid (Effect of Impurities):** The presence of dissolved non-volatile impurities **raises the boiling point** (boiling point elevation) and causes the liquid to boil over a range of temperatures. 

**3. Density**
- **Pure substance:** Has a fixed, constant density ($\text{Density} = \text{Mass} / \text{Volume}$).
- **Impure substance:** The density will deviate from the standard value depending on whether the impurity is denser or lighter than the pure substance.

**Common Mistake Alert:** Students frequently mix up the effects of impurities.
- *Wrong working:* Assuming an impurity raises the melting point. "Pure ice melts at $0.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so impure ice melts at $2.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$."
- *Corrected version:* Impurities **LOWER** the melting point (e.g., $-2.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$) but **RAISE** the boiling point (e.g., $102.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

#### Worked Examples on Purity Criteria

**1. Interpreting Melting Point Data**
**Question:** A student tests two samples of solid sulfur. Sample X melts sharply at exactly $115.2\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Sample Y melts between $110.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $114.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Which sample is pure and why?
**Working:**
1. State the rule for purity: A pure solid has a sharp, constant melting point. An impure solid melts over a broad temperature range and at a lower temperature.
2. Calculate the temperature range for Sample X: $\Delta T = 115.2\ ^\circ\text{C} - 115.2\ ^\circ\text{C} = 0.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ (Sharp).
3. Calculate the temperature range for Sample Y: $\Delta T = 114.5\ ^\circ\text{C} - 110.0\ ^\circ\text{C} = 4.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$ (Broad range).
4. **Conclusion:** Sample X is pure. Sample Y is an impure mixture.

**2. Density as a Check for Purity**
**Question:** A local miner in Jos finds a shiny yellow stone. It has a mass of $38.6\text{ g}$ and a volume of $2.0\text{ cm}^3$. Given that the density of pure gold is $19.3\text{ g cm}^{-3}$, determine if the stone is pure gold.
**Working:**
1. State the formula for density: $\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$
2. Substitute the given values: $\text{Mass} = 38.6\text{ g}$, $\text{Volume} = 2.0\text{ cm}^3$.
3. Calculate the density: $\frac{38.6\text{ g}}{2.0\text{ cm}^3} = 19.3\text{ g cm}^{-3}$.
4. **Conclusion:** The calculated density ($19.3\text{ g cm}^{-3}$) exactly matches the known density of pure gold. The stone is likely pure gold.

**3. Interpreting Boiling Point Elevation**
**Question:** Pure water boils at $100.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at $1\text{ atm}$. A sample of well water from a village boils at a temperature of $102.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at the same pressure. What is the boiling point elevation?
**Working:**
1. Note the standard property: $T_{\text{pure}} = 100.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Identify the deviation: $T_{\text{impure}} = 102.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
3. Calculate the elevation: $\Delta T_b = T_{\text{impure}} - T_{\text{pure}} = 102.5\ ^\circ\text{C} - 100.0\ ^\circ\text{C} = 2.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
4. **Conclusion:** The well water is an impure mixture containing dissolved solids that elevated the boiling point by $2.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

## Identifying the properties of the components of a mixture

Before choosing a method to separate a mixture, a chemist must identify the physical properties of its components. The separation method always exploits a specific difference in these properties.

- If one component is a **large solid** and the other is liquid $\rightarrow$ use difference in **particle size** (Filtration).
- If one component evaporates easily and the other does not $\rightarrow$ use difference in **volatility** (Evaporation).
- If two liquids mix completely but boil at different temperatures $\rightarrow$ use difference in **boiling points** (Distillation).
- If two solids dissolve differently in a solvent at different temperatures $\rightarrow$ use difference in **solubility** (Crystallization).
- If one solid changes directly to a gas $\rightarrow$ use difference in **sublimation** ability.
- If one component is magnetic (like iron) $\rightarrow$ use **magnetic properties** (Magnetization).

## Separation processes

### Specifying the principle involved in each separation method

Every separation technique is based on a core scientific principle, relying on the physical properties identified above. 

| Separation Method | Core Principle Involved |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Evaporation** | Difference in volatility; the solvent turns to gas, leaving the non-volatile solid behind. |
| **Simple Distillation** | Large difference in boiling points between a volatile liquid and a dissolved solid or another liquid. |
| **Fractional Distillation** | Very close boiling points of miscible liquids; separated using a fractionating column. |
| **Sublimation** | One solid component transitions directly from solid to gas on heating, leaving others behind. |
| **Crystallization** | Difference in solubility at different temperatures; solubility decreases as the solution cools, forming crystals. |
| **Chromatography** | Different rates of movement of solutes over a stationary phase based on their relative solubility in a mobile solvent. |
| **Filtration** | Difference in particle size; a porous barrier traps large solid particles but lets the liquid pass. |
| **Centrifugation** | Difference in density; rapid spinning forces the denser particles to the bottom of a container. |
| **Decantation** | Difference in density of immiscible substances; heavier insoluble solids settle, allowing the top liquid to be poured off. |
| **Magnetization** | Difference in magnetic properties; a magnet attracts magnetic metals ($\text{Fe}$, $\text{Co}$, $\text{Ni}$). |

### Applying the basic principle of separation processes in everyday life

Below is a detailed step-by-step breakdown of the 13 separation processes named in the syllabus, including how they are applied in everyday life and Nigerian industries.

#### 1. Evaporation
- **How it works:** A solution (a solid dissolved in a liquid) is heated. The volatile liquid solvent turns into a vapor and escapes into the air, leaving the non-volatile solid residue behind in the evaporating dish.
- **Everyday application:** **Extracting salt from seawater.** In coastal areas of Nigeria (like Lagos), shallow ponds of seawater are left under the hot sun. The water evaporates, leaving crude salt ($\text{NaCl}$) crystals behind. 
- **Limitation:** Evaporation cannot be used if the solid easily decomposes on heating (e.g., sugar chars on strong heating), and the liquid solvent is lost to the atmosphere.

#### 2. Simple Distillation
- **How it works:** Similar to evaporation, but the vapor is captured, cooled, and turned back into a liquid (condensed) so that both the solvent and solute are recovered. The mixture is heated in a round-bottom flask. The liquid boils to a vapor, travels into a **Liebig condenser** (cooled by running water), and condenses back into a pure liquid called the **distillate**.
- **Everyday application:** Producing **pure distilled water** for use in car batteries and hospitals. In the Niger Delta, local brewers use simple distillation to separate ethanol from fermented palm wine to produce the local gin, **ogogoro**.

#### 3. Fractional Distillation
- **How it works:** Used to separate two or more **miscible liquids** (liquids that mix completely) that have **close boiling points** (usually less than $10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ difference). The apparatus is similar to simple distillation but includes a **fractionating column** (often packed with glass beads) placed vertically above the boiling flask. The beads provide a large surface area for repeated evaporation and condensation. The liquid with the lowest boiling point reaches the top of the column first and distills over, while higher-boiling liquids condense back into the flask until the temperature is raised.
- **Everyday application:** **Refining of crude oil.** At the Port Harcourt, Warri, and Kaduna refineries, crude oil is separated into useful fractions like petrol, kerosene, diesel, and asphalt based on their boiling points. It is also used to separate liquid air into pure oxygen and nitrogen.

#### 4. Sublimation
- **How it works:** A mixture containing a sublimable solid (one that changes directly from solid to gas) and a non-sublimable solid is heated. The sublimable solid vaporizes and then condenses on a cool surface placed above it (forming the sublimate), leaving the impurities behind.
- **Common sublimable substances:** Iodine ($\text{I}_2$), ammonium chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$), and camphor.
- **Everyday application:** Purifying sublimable materials like camphor (used as mothballs in wardrobes to repel insects in Nigerian homes) when they are mixed with dirt or sand.

#### 5. Simple Crystallization
- **How it works:** Used to recover a solid that decomposes easily on heating. An impure solution is heated gently to evaporate some water, making it a **saturated solution**. It is then allowed to cool slowly. Because solids are less soluble at lower temperatures, the pure solid separates out as highly ordered crystals.
- **Everyday application:** **Sugar production.** Industries like Dangote Sugar use crystallization to obtain pure white sugar crystals from concentrated sugarcane syrup.

#### 6. Fractional Crystallization
- **How it works:** Used to separate a mixture of two or more dissolved solids that have **different solubilities** at different temperatures. When a hot, concentrated solution of the mixture is cooled, the substance that is least soluble at that lower temperature forms crystals first and is filtered out.
- **Everyday application:** Mining companies use this to separate complex mixtures of natural salt deposits extracted from the earth. 

#### 7. Re-crystallization
- **How it works:** This is a technique strictly for **purifying an already formed but impure solid**. The impure solid is dissolved in the minimum amount of a suitable hot solvent. The hot solution is filtered to remove insoluble impurities. As the filtrate cools, the desired pure solid crystallizes out, leaving soluble impurities dissolved in the remaining liquid (called the mother liquor). The pure crystals are then filtered, washed, and dried.
- **Everyday application:** Pharmaceutical manufacturing. Drug manufacturing companies in Nigeria use re-crystallization to ensure that medicines (like paracetamol crystals) are 100% pure before pressing them into tablets.

#### 8. Paper Chromatography
- **How it works:** Separates complex mixtures of coloured substances. A spot of the mixture (like ink) is placed near the bottom of a strip of filter paper (the **stationary phase**). The paper is dipped into a solvent (the **mobile phase**). As the solvent travels up the paper by capillary action, it dissolves the dyes. Components that are highly soluble in the solvent and weakly attracted to the paper move faster and travel higher, separating the mixture into distinct coloured spots. The ratio of the distance moved by the spot to the distance moved by the solvent is the retardation factor ($R_f$).
- **Everyday application:** Separating dyes in forensic science (investigating forged documents); analyzing the complex dye mixtures used in the local tie-and-dye (**adire**) industry in Abeokuta.

#### 9. Column Chromatography
- **How it works:** Similar principle to paper chromatography but used for larger quantities. The stationary phase (usually silica gel or alumina powder) is packed into a tall glass column. The mixture is poured in at the top, and solvent flows down through the column. The components move down at different rates and are collected in separate flasks at the bottom.
- **Everyday application:** Used extensively by scientists at institutions like the National Institute for Pharmaceutical Research and Development (NIPRD) in Abuja to isolate active medicinal compounds from local medicinal plants (e.g., extracting anti-malarial compounds from neem leaves).

#### 10. Magnetization (Magnetic Separation)
- **How it works:** A magnet is passed over a mixture. Magnetic metals are attracted to the magnet, leaving non-magnetic substances behind. The magnetic metals are iron ($\text{Fe}$), cobalt ($\text{Co}$), and nickel ($\text{Ni}$).
- **Everyday application:** Sorting scrap metals. In recycling facilities in Aba and Lagos, large electromagnets are used to pick up iron and steel scraps from mixed rubbish containing plastics, glass, and aluminium.

#### 11. Decantation
- **How it works:** A crude method of separating a dense, insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is allowed to stand so the heavy solid settles to the bottom. The clear liquid on top (the supernatant) is then carefully poured off (decanted) into another container.
- **Everyday application:** In the kitchen, pouring water off washed local rice or beans before cooking; separating red palm oil from the heavy, watery sludge during traditional palm oil processing.

#### 12. Filtration
- **How it works:** Used to completely separate an insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is poured through a porous material (like filter paper in a funnel). The liquid passes through the tiny pores and is collected as the **filtrate**. The solid particles are too large to pass through and remain on the paper as the **residue**.
- **Everyday application:** Using a fine cloth or sieve to separate chaff from milled maize pap (ogi); state water corporations (like the Lagos Water Corporation) pass river water through huge sand beds to filter out solid dirt during water treatment.

[IMAGE: Clean flat textbook illustration showing the separation of an insoluble solid from a liquid by filtration. The filter paper traps the solid, and clear liquid drips into the flask below. Labels to add later: "Mixture", "Glass rod", "Filter paper", "Filter funnel", "Residue (trapped solid)", "Filtrate (clear liquid)", "Conical flask". White background.]

#### 13. Centrifugation
- **How it works:** Used to separate fine, lightweight solid particles suspended in a liquid, which are too small to be easily filtered. The mixture is placed in test tubes inside a machine called a centrifuge, which spins at incredibly high speeds. The strong centrifugal force pushes the denser solid particles to the bottom of the tube in a solid lump, leaving clear liquid at the top.
- **Everyday application:** Medical laboratories use it to separate heavy red blood cells from the lighter, watery blood plasma; dairies use it to separate heavy cream from milk.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Boiling Point and Melting Point Mix-ups:** This is the most common student error. Remember that impurities **raise** the boiling point but **lower** the melting/freezing point. An impure substance also melts and boils over a *range* rather than at a sharp point.
- **Evaporation vs. Crystallization:** Both recover solids, but if the solid easily decomposes on heating (like sugar), or if you need highly pure crystals, crystallization must be used instead of evaporation.
- **Simple vs. Fractional Distillation:** Use *simple* distillation when separating a liquid from a solid (water from salt) or liquids with vastly different boiling points. Use *fractional* distillation for miscible liquids with close boiling points (like ethanol and water, or crude oil fractions).
- **Sublimable Substances:** Memorize the common examples tested by JAMB: Iodine ($\text{I}_2$), ammonium chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$), and camphor. Sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) does *not* sublime.
- **Chromatography Principle:** Chromatography separates based on differences in *rates of movement* (driven by differences in solubility in a mobile phase), not by density or boiling point.

## Quick summary

1. Matter is classified into elements (one type of atom), compounds (elements chemically bonded), and mixtures (substances physically mixed).
2. Physical changes form no new substances and are easily reversible, while chemical changes form new substances and are irreversible.
3. A pure substance has sharp, fixed melting and boiling points, while an impure substance melts over a lower range and boils over a higher range.
4. Density can be calculated ($\text{Density} = \text{Mass} / \text{Volume}$) and compared to standard values to confirm purity.
5. The choice of separation technique depends entirely on differences in physical properties (e.g., boiling points for distillation, particle size for filtration).
6. Fractional distillation uses a fractionating column to separate miscible liquids with close boiling points, heavily applied in crude oil refining.
7. Re-crystallization is the standard method for purifying an impure solid, relying on dissolving the substance in a hot solvent and allowing it to cool.

## Practice questions

**1. A mixture of iron filings and sulfur powder has a total mass of $40.0\text{ g}$. A magnet is used to completely separate the iron, leaving $15.0\text{ g}$ of sulfur. What is the percentage by mass of iron in the original mixture?**

- A. $37.5\%$
- B. $55.0\%$
- C. $62.5\%$
- D. $15.0\%$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Note the total mass of the mixture is $40.0\text{ g}$.
2. Note the mass of sulfur remaining is $15.0\text{ g}$.
3. Calculate the mass of iron extracted: $40.0\text{ g} - 15.0\text{ g} = 25.0\text{ g}$.
4. Calculate the percentage of iron: $\frac{25.0\text{ g}}{40.0\text{ g}} \times 100\% = 62.5\%$.
The most tempting wrong option is A. This mistake happens if you incorrectly calculate the percentage of the remaining sulfur ($15.0\text{ g}$) instead of the extracted iron.

**2. The melting of ice into water is a physical change where total mass is conserved. If a block of ice melts completely into liquid water and the liquid water formed has a mass of $25.0\text{ g}$, what was the original mass of the ice block?**

- A. $27.2\text{ g}$
- B. $25.0\text{ g}$
- C. $250.0\text{ g}$
- D. $1.00\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Recall the law of conservation of mass for a physical change: mass of reactants = mass of products.
2. The mass of the liquid water product formed is directly given as $25.0\text{ g}$.
3. Therefore, the original mass of the solid ice block must exactly equal the mass of the liquid water: $25.0\text{ g}$.
The most tempting wrong option is A. This mistake happens if you divide the mass of the water ($25.0\text{ g}$) by the density of ice (approximately $0.92\text{ g cm}^{-3}$), which gives $27.2$, instead of recognising that mass is conserved in a physical change.

**3. A student tests a $10.0\text{ g}$ sample of a solid substance to check its purity. The substance begins to melt at $112.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and completely melts at $117.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. What is the melting temperature range ($\Delta T$) that indicates this is an impure mixture?**

- A. $11.2\ ^\circ\text{C}$
- B. $229.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$
- C. $112.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$
- D. $5.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Note the initial melting temperature: $T_1 = 112.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Note the final melting temperature: $T_2 = 117.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
3. Calculate the melting temperature range difference: $\Delta T = T_2 - T_1 = 117.0\ ^\circ\text{C} - 112.0\ ^\circ\text{C} = 5.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
The most tempting wrong option is B. This mistake happens if you incorrectly add the two temperatures together ($112.0 + 117.0$) instead of subtracting to find the range.

**4. Pure water has a boiling point of $100.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. A $50.0\text{ cm}^3$ sample of well water containing dissolved salts boils at $102.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$. What is the boiling point elevation ($\Delta T_b$) caused by the impurities?**

- A. $2.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$
- B. $202.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$
- C. $50.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$
- D. $102.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Note the boiling point of the pure solvent (water): $T_{\text{pure}} = 100.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Note the boiling point of the impure liquid (well water): $T_{\text{impure}} = 102.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
3. Calculate the boiling point elevation: $\Delta T_b = T_{\text{impure}} - T_{\text{pure}} = 102.5\ ^\circ\text{C} - 100.0\ ^\circ\text{C} = 2.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
The most tempting wrong option is C. This mistake happens if you mistakenly use the numerical volume of the sample ($50.0\text{ cm}^3$) as the answer for the temperature difference.

**5. A local miner finds a shiny block of metal. It has a mass of $38.6\text{ g}$ and a volume of $2.0\text{ cm}^3$. What is the density of the metal, which can be used to identify if it is pure gold?**

- A. $77.2\text{ g cm}^{-3}$
- B. $19.3\text{ g cm}^{-3}$
- C. $0.05\text{ g cm}^{-3}$
- D. $40.6\text{ g cm}^{-3}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. State the formula for density: $\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$.
2. Substitute the given values: $\text{Mass} = 38.6\text{ g}$, $\text{Volume} = 2.0\text{ cm}^3$.

**6. To obtain pure salt from seawater, a student evaporates $200.0\text{ g}$ of seawater. If the seawater contains exactly $3.5\%$ salt by mass, what mass of solid salt residue is left in the evaporating dish?**

- A. $196.5\text{ g}$
- B. $7.0\text{ g}$
- C. $57.1\text{ g}$
- D. $3.5\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Identify the total mass of the seawater solution: $200.0\text{ g}$.
2. Identify the percentage composition of salt in the water: $3.5\%$.
3. Calculate the mass of the salt: $\frac{3.5}{100} \times 200.0\text{ g} = 7.0\text{ g}$.
The most tempting wrong option is A. This mistake happens if you simply subtract the percentage value directly from the total mass ($200.0 - 3.5 = 196.5$) to find the remaining mass, which incorrectly gives the mass of the evaporated water.

**7. In a paper chromatography experiment to separate dye, a particular dye spot moves $4.5\text{ cm}$ from the baseline, while the solvent front moves $9.0\text{ cm}$. What is the retardation factor ($R_f$) of the dye? (Note: $R_f$ has no unit)**

- A. $2.0$
- B. $13.5$
- C. $40.5$
- D. $0.5$

**Answer: D.** 
1. State the formula for the retardation factor: $R_f = \frac{\text{Distance moved by spot}}{\text{Distance moved by solvent}}$.
2. Substitute the given values: Distance of spot = $4.5\text{ cm}$, Distance of solvent = $9.0\text{ cm}$.
3. Calculate the $R_f$ value: $\frac{4.5\text{ cm}}{9.0\text{ cm}} = 0.5$.
The most tempting wrong option is A. This mistake happens if you invert the formula and divide the solvent distance by the spot distance ($9.0 / 4.5 = 2.0$) instead of the correct ratio.

**8. A local brewer in the Niger Delta distils $250.0\text{ cm}^3$ of fermented palm wine and collects exactly $25.0\text{ cm}^3$ of pure ethanol distillate. If the density of pure ethanol is $0.80\text{ g cm}^{-3}$, what is the mass of the ethanol collected?**

- A. $20.0\text{ g}$
- B. $200.0\text{ g}$
- C. $31.3\text{ g}$
- D. $25.0\text{ g}$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Note the volume of the collected ethanol distillate: $V = 25.0\text{ cm}^3$.
2. Note the density of the ethanol: $d = 0.80\text{ g cm}^{-3}$.
3. Rearrange the density formula to find mass: $\text{Mass} = \text{Density} \times \text{Volume}$.
4. Calculate the mass: $0.80\text{ g cm}^{-3} \times 25.0\text{ cm}^3 = 20.0\text{ g}$.
The most tempting wrong option is B. This mistake happens if you incorrectly use the starting volume of the entire palm wine mixture ($250.0\text{ cm}^3$) in the calculation instead of the volume of the collected pure ethanol distillate.

**9. During municipal water treatment, $500.0\text{ g}$ of muddy river water is filtered. The dried solid mud residue left on the filter bed weighs $15.0\text{ g}$. Assuming no water is lost to evaporation, what is the mass of the clear filtrate (water) collected?**

- A. $515.0\text{ g}$
- B. $15.0\text{ g}$
- C. $485.0\text{ g}$
- D. $500.0\text{ g}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Identify the total mass of the mixture (muddy water): $500.0\text{ g}$.
2. Identify the mass of the insoluble residue (mud): $15.0\text{ g}$.
3. State the conservation of mass principle: $\text{Mass of filtrate} = \text{Total mass} - \text{Mass of residue}$.
4. Calculate the mass of the filtrate: $500.0\text{ g} - 15.0\text{ g} = 485.0\text{ g}$.
The most tempting wrong option is A. This mistake happens if you incorrectly add the mass of the residue to the starting mass of the mixture ($500.0 + 15.0$).

**10. A $20.0\text{ g}$ mixture of iodine and sodium chloride is heated gently in an evaporating dish. The iodine completely sublimes away as a gas. If exactly $12.0\text{ g}$ of non-sublimable solid remains in the dish, what mass of iodine sublimed?**

- A. $12.0\text{ g}$
- B. $8.0\text{ g}$
- C. $32.0\text{ g}$
- D. $1.7\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Note the starting total mass of the mixture: $20.0\text{ g}$.
2. Note the mass of the non-sublimable residue (sodium chloride) remaining: $12.0\text{ g}$.
3. Calculate the mass of the sublimed substance (iodine) by difference: $20.0\text{ g} - 12.0\text{ g} = 8.0\text{ g}$.
The most tempting wrong option is A. This mistake happens if you just read the remaining mass ($12.0\text{ g}$) in the question and select it as the answer, without calculating the difference to find what was actually lost.
