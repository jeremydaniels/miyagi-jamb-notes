## Electrolytes, non-electrolytes and Faraday's laws

### Distinguishing between electrolytes and non-electrolytes

Substances behave differently when an electric current passes through them. We classify substances into conductors, non-conductors, electrolytes, and non-electrolytes.

- **Conductors** are usually solid metals (like copper or aluminium) or graphite that allow electric current to pass through them without being chemically decomposed. They conduct electricity because they have a "sea" of free, mobile **electrons**. 
- **Non-conductors (Insulators)** are solids like dry wood or plastic that do not allow electric current to pass through them.

In chemistry, we are more interested in liquids and solutions:

- **Electrolytes** are compounds in a liquid state (either molten or dissolved in water) that conduct electricity and are **chemically decomposed** in the process. They conduct electricity because they contain **free, mobile ions** (cations and anions), not electrons. 
  - **Strong electrolytes:** These ionise (dissociate) completely in water, producing a high concentration of mobile ions. They are excellent conductors. Examples include strong acids (dilute $\text{HCl}$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), strong alkalis ($\text{NaOH}$, $\text{KOH}$), and most soluble salts (aqueous $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{CuSO}_4$).
  - **Weak electrolytes:** These ionise only partially in water, producing a low concentration of mobile ions. They are poor conductors. Examples include weak acids (ethanoic acid, citric acid), weak alkalis (aqueous ammonia), and tap water.
- **Non-electrolytes** are liquids or solutions that do not conduct electricity at all because they contain **no mobile ions**. They exist entirely as neutral molecules. Examples include pure (distilled) water, ethanol, sugar solution, benzene, trichloromethane, and kerosene (widely used in Nigerian stoves).

| Feature | Electrolyte | Non-electrolyte |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conductivity** | Conducts electricity in molten or aqueous state | Does not conduct electricity |
| **Particles present** | Mobile ions ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{H}^+$, etc.) | Neutral molecules only |
| **Chemical change** | Decomposed by the current (electrolysis) | No chemical decomposition |
| **Examples** | Aqueous $\text{NaCl}$, dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, molten $\text{KCl}$ | Sugar solution, pure water, ethanol, kerosene |

### Performing calculations based on faraday as mole of electrons

Electrolysis follows two fundamental laws discovered by the English scientist Michael Faraday in the 1830s.

**Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis** states that the mass ($m$) of an element discharged during electrolysis is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed through the electrolyte. 
Mathematically, $m \propto Q$. Since $Q = I \times t$ (where $I$ is current in Amperes, $\text{A}$, and $t$ is time in seconds, $\text{s}$), we can write $m \propto I t$.

**Faraday's Second Law of Electrolysis** states that when the same quantity of electricity is passed through different electrolytes, the relative number of moles of the elements discharged are inversely proportional to the charges on their ions.

**The Faraday Constant ($F$)**
To perform calculations in JAMB, you must understand the relationship between electricity and moles. 
- An electron has a tiny electrical charge. The total charge of **one mole of electrons** ($6.02 \times 10^{23}$ electrons) is called **One Faraday ($1\text{ F}$)**.
- The standard value is **$1\text{ F} = 96,500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$** (Coulombs per mole).

The number of Faradays required to discharge one mole of a substance depends on the charge of the ion in the half-equation:
- To deposit $1\text{ mole}$ of Silver ($\text{Ag}$), the equation is $\text{Ag}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Ag}$. This requires **$1\text{ mole}$ of electrons ($1\text{ F}$)**.
- To deposit $1\text{ mole}$ of Copper ($\text{Cu}$), the equation is $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$. This requires **$2\text{ moles}$ of electrons ($2\text{ F}$)**.
- To deposit $1\text{ mole}$ of Aluminium ($\text{Al}$), the equation is $\text{Al}^{3+} + 3\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Al}$. This requires **$3\text{ moles}$ of electrons ($3\text{ F}$)**.
- To produce $1\text{ mole}$ of Oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$), the equation is $4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2 + 4\text{e}^-$. This requires **$4\text{ moles}$ of electrons ($4\text{ F}$)**.

Let's look at three worked examples showing the standard step-by-step method you should use.

**Example 1: Calculating the mass of a metal deposited**
**Problem:** Calculate the mass of copper deposited when a current of $0.5\text{ A}$ is passed through aqueous copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) for $1930\text{ s}$. [$\text{Cu} = 64$, $1\text{ F} = 96,500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$]

1. **Write the half-equation for copper:**
   $\text{Cu}^{2+}_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}_{\text{(s)}}$
   This shows that $2\text{ moles}$ of electrons ($2 \times 96,500\text{ C}$) deposit $1\text{ mole}$ of copper ($64\text{ g}$).
2. **Calculate the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed:**
   $Q = I \times t$
   $Q = 0.5\text{ A} \times 1930\text{ s} = 965\text{ C}$
3. **Use the mole ratio to find the mass deposited:**
   $2 \times 96,500\text{ C} = 193,000\text{ C}$ deposits $64\text{ g}$ of $\text{Cu}$.
   Therefore, $965\text{ C}$ will deposit:
   $m = \frac{64\text{ g} \times 965\text{ C}}{193000\text{ C}}$
   $m = \frac{64 \times 1}{200}\text{ g} = \frac{64}{200}\text{ g} = 0.32\text{ g}$. 
   **Answer:** $0.32\text{ g}$ of copper.

**Example 2: Calculating the volume of a gas produced**
**Problem:** Calculate the volume of oxygen gas produced at s.t.p. when a current of $2.0\text{ A}$ is passed through dilute tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid for $9650\text{ s}$. [Molar volume at s.t.p. = $22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$, $1\text{ F} = 96,500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$]

1. **Write the half-equation for oxygen:**
   $4\text{OH}^-_{\text{(aq)}} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}} + \text{O}_{2\text{(g)}} + 4\text{e}^-$
   This shows that $4\text{ moles}$ of electrons ($4 \times 96,500\text{ C}$) produce $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{O}_2$ gas ($22.4\text{ dm}^3$).
2. **Calculate the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed:**
   $Q = I \times t$
   $Q = 2.0\text{ A} \times 9650\text{ s} = 19,300\text{ C}$
3. **Use the mole ratio to find the volume:**
   $4 \times 96,500\text{ C} = 386,000\text{ C}$ produces $22.4\text{ dm}^3$.
   Therefore, $19,300\text{ C}$ produces:
   $V = \frac{22.4\text{ dm}^3 \times 19300\text{ C}}{386000\text{ C}}$
   $V = \frac{22.4 \times 1}{20}\text{ dm}^3 = 1.12\text{ dm}^3$.
   **Answer:** $1.12\text{ dm}^3$ of oxygen.

**Example 3: Calculating time**
**Problem:** How long will it take to deposit $2.16\text{ g}$ of silver from an aqueous solution of a silver salt using a current of $2.0\text{ A}$? [$\text{Ag} = 108$, $1\text{ F} = 96,500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$]

1. **Write the half-equation:**
   $\text{Ag}^+_{\text{(aq)}} + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Ag}_{\text{(s)}}$
   $1\text{ mole}$ of electrons ($96,500\text{ C}$) deposits $1\text{ mole}$ of Ag ($108\text{ g}$).
2. **Calculate the charge needed to deposit $2.16\text{ g}$ of Ag:**
   $108\text{ g}$ requires $96,500\text{ C}$.
   $2.16\text{ g}$ requires $Q = \frac{96500\text{ C} \times 2.16\text{ g}}{108\text{ g}} = 96500 \times 0.02\text{ C} = 1930\text{ C}$.
3. **Calculate the time using $Q = I \times t$:**
   $t = \frac{Q}{I} = \frac{1930\text{ C}}{2.0\text{ A}} = 965\text{ s}$.
   **Answer:** $965\text{ s}$ (or 16 minutes and 5 seconds).

**Common mistakes in calculations:**
- Forgetting to convert time from minutes or hours into **seconds**. Always use seconds.
- Using the wrong mole ratio. Remember hydrogen gas is $\text{H}_2$ (so $2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2$ requires $2\text{ F}$), and oxygen gas is $\text{O}_2$ (requires $4\text{ F}$).
- Using the wrong formula. Some students blindly memorize $m = \frac{ZIt}{F}$; using the mole-ratio method shown above is much safer and easier to understand.

## Electrolysis of specific electrolytes and discharge of ions

Electrolysis is the chemical decomposition of an electrolyte by passing a direct current (d.c.) through it. It takes place in an **electrolytic cell** which consists of a container, the electrolyte, a direct current power supply (like a battery), and two solid conductors called **electrodes**.
- **Anode:** The positive electrode. Anions (negative ions) migrate here and lose electrons (Oxidation).
- **Cathode:** The negative electrode. Cations (positive ions) migrate here and gain electrons (Reduction).

### Identifying suitable electrodes for different electrolytes

The choice of electrode is very important because it can affect the products of electrolysis. Electrodes are broadly classified into two types:

1. **Inert electrodes:** These do not take part in the chemical reaction. Their only job is to carry current into and out of the electrolyte.
   - **Platinum (Pt):** Excellent inert electrode, highly unreactive, but very expensive. Suitable for dilute acids and almost all solutions.
   - **Carbon (Graphite):** Cheap and widely used. It is inert in most cases, but it can be slowly eaten away at the anode if oxygen gas is produced (the carbon reacts with hot oxygen to form $\text{CO}_2$). 
2. **Active (Reactive) electrodes:** These participate in the chemical reaction. Examples include copper, silver, and zinc. If copper is used as the anode during the electrolysis of copper salts, the anode itself will dissolve into the solution instead of producing a gas.

| Electrolyte being electrolysed | Suitable Electrodes | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dilute acids (e.g. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) | Platinum | Inert, doesn't react with the acid or the oxygen produced. |
| Fused (molten) $\text{NaCl}$ | Carbon anode, Iron cathode | Carbon is cheap; Iron does not alloy with the sodium produced. |
| Aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$ (to extract pure Cu) | Impure Copper anode, Pure Copper cathode | Active electrodes allow the anode to dissolve and transfer copper to the cathode. |

### Specifying the chemical reactions at the electrodes

Here are the standard reactions for the specific electrolytes named in the JAMB syllabus. 

**1. Dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ (using Platinum electrodes)**
- **Ions present:** $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ (from acid), $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$ (from water).
- **Cathode (-):** Only $\text{H}^+$ ions migrate here. They gain electrons to form hydrogen gas.
  $2\text{H}^+_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_{2\text{(g)}}$
- **Anode (+):** Both $\text{OH}^-$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ migrate here. $\text{OH}^-$ is discharged because it is lower in the electrochemical series.
  $4\text{OH}^-_{\text{(aq)}} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}} + \text{O}_{2\text{(g)}} + 4\text{e}^-$
- **Overall effect:** Water is essentially being electrolysed. The concentration of the $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ increases as water is used up.

**2. Aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$ (using Platinum/Carbon electrodes)**
- **Ions present:** $\text{Cu}^{2+}$, $\text{H}^+$, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{OH}^-$.
- **Cathode (-):** $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ and $\text{H}^+$ migrate here. $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is discharged because it is lower in the series. Pink/brown copper metal is deposited.
  $\text{Cu}^{2+}_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}_{\text{(s)}}$
- **Anode (+):** $\text{OH}^-$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ migrate here. $\text{OH}^-$ is discharged. Oxygen gas is given off.
  $4\text{OH}^-_{\text{(aq)}} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}} + \text{O}_{2\text{(g)}} + 4\text{e}^-$
- **Overall effect:** The blue colour of the solution fades as $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions are removed. The solution becomes acidic because $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ are left behind, forming dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.

**3. Aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$ (using active Copper electrodes)**
- **Cathode (-):** Copper is deposited, same as above.
  $\text{Cu}^{2+}_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}_{\text{(s)}}$
- **Anode (+):** Instead of discharging $\text{OH}^-$, the copper anode dissolves (oxidises) because it requires less energy than discharging $\text{OH}^-$.
  $\text{Cu}_{\text{(s)}} \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^-$
- **Overall effect:** The concentration of the solution remains unchanged. The blue colour remains strong. The anode gets thinner, and the cathode gets thicker.

**4. Aqueous $\text{CuCl}_2$ solution (using Carbon electrodes)**
- **Ions present:** $\text{Cu}^{2+}$, $\text{H}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{OH}^-$.
- **Cathode (-):** $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is discharged over $\text{H}^+$.
  $\text{Cu}^{2+}_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}_{\text{(s)}}$
- **Anode (+):** Although $\text{OH}^-$ is lower than $\text{Cl}^-$ in the series, halogen ions ($\text{Cl}^-$) are usually discharged if they are in a reasonable concentration. Chlorine gas is produced.
  $2\text{Cl}^-_{\text{(aq)}} \rightarrow \text{Cl}_{2\text{(g)}} + 2\text{e}^-$

**5. Dilute $\text{NaCl}$ solution (using Platinum electrodes)**
- **Ions present:** $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{H}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{OH}^-$.
- **Cathode (-):** $\text{H}^+$ is discharged because it is lower than $\text{Na}^+$ in the series.
  $2\text{H}^+_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_{2\text{(g)}}$
- **Anode (+):** Because the solution is dilute, $\text{OH}^-$ is discharged instead of $\text{Cl}^-$.
  $4\text{OH}^-_{\text{(aq)}} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}} + \text{O}_{2\text{(g)}} + 4\text{e}^-$

**6. Concentrated $\text{NaCl}$ solution (Brine) (using Carbon/Titanium electrodes)**
- **Cathode (-):** $\text{H}^+$ is discharged. (Hydrogen gas is produced).
  $2\text{H}^+_{\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_{2\text{(g)}}$
- **Anode (+):** Because the $\text{Cl}^-$ concentration is very high, it overcomes the normal electrochemical series order. $\text{Cl}^-$ is discharged instead of $\text{OH}^-$.
  $2\text{Cl}^-_{\text{(aq)}} \rightarrow \text{Cl}_{2\text{(g)}} + 2\text{e}^-$
- **Overall effect:** $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ are removed, leaving $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$ behind. The solution becomes concentrated Sodium Hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$).

**7. Fused (molten) $\text{NaCl}$**
- **Ions present:** Only $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ (no water, so no $\text{H}^+$ or $\text{OH}^-$).
- **Cathode (-):** Sodium ions must be discharged.
  $\text{Na}^+_{\text{(l)}} + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Na}_{\text{(l)}}$
- **Anode (+):** Chloride ions are discharged.
  $2\text{Cl}^-_{\text{(l)}} \rightarrow \text{Cl}_{2\text{(g)}} + 2\text{e}^-$

### Identifying the factors that affect the products of electrolysis

During electrolysis of aqueous solutions, there is usually a "competition" between the ions from the salt and the ions from water ($\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$). Only one type of cation and one type of anion will be discharged. 

Three main factors decide which ion wins the competition:

**1. Position of the ions in the Electrochemical Series**
If all other factors are equal, the ion that is **lower** in the electrochemical series will be discharged preferentially because it requires less energy to do so. 
- At the cathode: If $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ and $\text{H}^+$ are present, $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is discharged because it is lower than $\text{H}^+$.
- At the anode: If $\text{OH}^-$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ are present, $\text{OH}^-$ is discharged because it is lower than $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$.

**2. Concentration of the ions in the electrolyte**
A higher concentration can override the rule of the electrochemical series, provided the two competing ions are close to each other in the series. 
- In dilute $\text{NaCl}$, the anode produces Oxygen (because $\text{OH}^-$ is discharged normally).
- In concentrated $\text{NaCl}$, the anode produces Chlorine (because the massive amount of $\text{Cl}^-$ forces it to be discharged instead of $\text{OH}^-$).
- *Note:* Concentration cannot override the series if the ions are far apart. For example, even in very concentrated $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{Na}^+$ is never discharged at the cathode in an aqueous solution because it is too far above $\text{H}^+$.

**3. Nature of the electrodes**
As discussed earlier, using an active (reactive) electrode changes the reaction at the anode. If you use a copper anode in a copper solution, the anode dissolves ($\text{Cu} \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$) rather than any anion being discharged.

## Uses of electrolysis

### Specifying the different areas of application of electrolysis

Electrolysis is not just a laboratory experiment; it is a massive industrial process. It is used in four main areas:

1. **Extraction of metals:** Metals high up in the electrochemical series (like Potassium, Sodium, Calcium, Magnesium, and Aluminium) are highly reactive and form very stable ores. They cannot be extracted by heating with carbon. They are extracted by the electrolysis of their **molten (fused) ores**. 
2. **Production of chemical compounds and non-metals:** Electrolysis of concentrated brine ($\text{NaCl}$) is the primary method for manufacturing Sodium Hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$), Chlorine gas ($\text{Cl}_2$), and Hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$).
3. **Purification (Refining) of metals:** Metals extracted from ores are often impure. Electrolysis is used to make them up to 99.9% pure, which is essential for metals like copper used in electrical wiring.
4. **Electroplating:** Coating a cheap or easily rusted metal object with a thin layer of a more precious or unreactive metal (like silver, gold, chromium, or tin).

### Determining the different areas of application of electrolytic processes

How do we decide which electrolytic process to apply? 

- **If you need to extract Aluminium:** You cannot use an aqueous solution because hydrogen would be produced instead of aluminium. You must melt the bauxite (aluminium ore) and dissolve it in molten cryolite, then electrolyse it in a high-temperature cell (the Hall-Héroult process).
- **If you need to refine (purify) a metal:** You apply the active electrode principle. Set up the cell with the **impure metal as the anode** and a small piece of **pure metal as the cathode**. Use a soluble salt of the metal as the electrolyte. For copper refining, the impure copper anode dissolves, and pure copper plates onto the pure cathode. The impurities fall to the bottom as "anode slime".
- **If you want to electroplate a Nigerian local iron bangle with silver:** 
  1. The bangle to be plated MUST be made the **cathode (-)**.
  2. The plating metal (pure Silver) MUST be the **anode (+)**.
  3. The electrolyte MUST be a solution of the plating metal (e.g. aqueous Silver trioxonitrate(V), $\text{AgNO}_3$).
  When current flows, silver dissolves from the anode and plates beautifully onto the iron bangle at the cathode.

## Electrochemical cells

### Stating the significance of the electrochemical series

The electrochemical series arranges elements (mostly metals, plus hydrogen) in order of their standard electrode potentials. For metals, it is arranged from the most electropositive (most reactive, loses electrons easiest) at the top, to the least electropositive at the bottom.

**The Electrochemical Series (Metals):**
$\text{K}, \text{Ca}, \text{Na}, \text{Mg}, \text{Al}, \text{Zn}, \text{Fe}, \text{Sn}, \text{Pb}, \text{H}, \text{Cu}, \text{Hg}, \text{Ag}, \text{Au}$

*A useful mnemonic for Nigerian students:*
**K**ing **Ca**n **Na**ot **Mg**ake **Al** **Zn**ebra **Fe**ed **Sn**ails **Pb**roperly, **H**e **Cu**ould **Hg**ave **Ag** **Au**oat.
*(King Can Not Make A Zebra Feed Snails Properly, He Could Have A Goat).*

**Significance of the series:**
1. **Predicting displacement:** A metal higher in the series will displace a metal lower down from an aqueous solution of its salt. (e.g. Zinc will displace Copper from $\text{CuSO}_4$, but Silver will not).
2. **Predicting the ease of discharge:** In electrolysis, ions of metals lower in the series require less energy to be discharged at the cathode.
3. **Reducing power:** Metals at the top (like $\text{K}$, $\text{Na}$) are strong reducing agents because they easily give away electrons.
4. **Predicting e.m.f of cells:** The further apart two metals are in the series, the greater the voltage (e.m.f) they will produce when connected to form an electrochemical cell.

### Identifying the various electrochemical cells

There are two main types of electrochemical cells:
1. **Electrolytic cells:** Use electrical energy to force a non-spontaneous chemical reaction to happen. (Anode is positive, Cathode is negative).
2. **Galvanic (Voltaic) cells:** Use a spontaneous chemical reaction to generate electrical energy. (Anode is negative, Cathode is positive).

Types of Galvanic cells include:
- **Primary cells:** Once the chemicals are used up, the cell is dead and must be thrown away. 
  - *Simple cell (Daniell cell):* Uses Zinc and Copper electrodes.
  - *Leclanché cell (Dry cell):* The standard AA battery used in flashlights and radios in Nigeria. Uses a zinc case (anode) and a carbon rod (cathode) with ammonium chloride paste.
- **Secondary cells:** Can be recharged by passing a direct current backward through them. 
  - *Lead-acid accumulator:* Used as car batteries. It uses lead, lead(IV) oxide, and concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.

### Calculating electrode potentials using half-cell reaction equations

Every metal dipping into a solution of its ions forms a **half-cell** with a Standard Electrode Potential ($E^\circ$), measured in Volts, $\text{V}$. By international convention, these are given as **reduction potentials**. 
- Metals high in the series (like $\text{Zn}$) have negative $E^\circ$ values (e.g. $-0.76\text{ V}$), meaning they prefer to be oxidised.
- Metals low in the series (like $\text{Cu}$) have positive $E^\circ$ values (e.g. $+0.34\text{ V}$), meaning they prefer to be reduced.

When two half-cells are connected (usually by a wire and a salt bridge), the standard electromotive force (e.m.f.) of the cell is calculated as:
$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$$
(Also remembered as $E^\circ_{\text{right}} - E^\circ_{\text{left}}$).
- The electrode with the **more negative** (or less positive) potential becomes the **anode** (where oxidation happens).
- The electrode with the **more positive** potential becomes the **cathode** (where reduction happens).

**Example 1: Zinc/Copper Cell**
**Problem:** Given the standard reduction potentials $E^\circ$ for $\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn} = -0.76\text{ V}$ and $\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu} = +0.34\text{ V}$. Calculate the standard e.m.f. of the cell.

1. **Identify anode and cathode:** Zinc is more negative, so it is the anode. Copper is the cathode.
   Half reactions: 
   Anode: $\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$
   Cathode: $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$
2. **Apply the formula:**
   $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$
   $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +0.34\text{ V} - (-0.76\text{ V}) = 0.34\text{ V} + 0.76\text{ V} = +1.10\text{ V}$.
   **Answer:** $+1.10\text{ V}$.

**Example 2: Iron/Silver Cell**
**Problem:** Given $E^\circ$ for $\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe} = -0.44\text{ V}$ and $\text{Ag}^+/\text{Ag} = +0.80\text{ V}$. Find the e.m.f. of the cell represented by the notation: $\text{Fe(s)} | \text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)} || \text{Ag}^+\text{(aq)} | \text{Ag(s)}$.

1. **Identify electrodes from cell notation:** In standard IUPAC cell notation, the left side is always the anode ($\text{Fe}$) and the right side is the cathode ($\text{Ag}$). (Confirm: $\text{Fe}$ is more negative, so this matches).
2. **Apply the formula:**
   $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$
   $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +0.80\text{ V} - (-0.44\text{ V}) = 0.80\text{ V} + 0.44\text{ V} = +1.24\text{ V}$.
   **Answer:** $+1.24\text{ V}$.

**Example 3: Magnesium/Copper Cell**
**Problem:** Calculate the standard e.m.f. of a cell composed of $\text{Mg}^{2+}/\text{Mg}$ ($E^\circ = -2.37\text{ V}$) and $\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}$ ($E^\circ = +0.34\text{ V}$).

1. **Identify anode and cathode:** Magnesium is more negative, so it is the anode. Copper is more positive, so it is the cathode.
2. **Apply the formula:**
   $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$
   $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +0.34\text{ V} - (-2.37\text{ V}) = 0.34\text{ V} + 2.37\text{ V} = +2.71\text{ V}$.
   **Answer:** $+2.71\text{ V}$.

*(Note: The e.m.f. of a spontaneous cell is always positive).*

## Corrosion of metals and its prevention

### Identifying methods used in protecting metals

Corrosion (specifically called **rusting** when it refers to iron) is an electrolytic process. For iron to rust, both **oxygen** and **water** must be present.
- **The mechanism:** A droplet of water on iron acts as a miniature electrolytic cell. The iron acts as the anode, losing electrons and dissolving: $\text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$. Oxygen and water act as the cathode, gaining those electrons to form hydroxide ions: $\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$. These ions react to form hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).

Rusting causes immense damage to cars, bridges, and roofs. It can be prevented using several methods:

1. **Cathodic Protection (Sacrificial Anode):** The iron object (like an underground pipeline or a ship's hull) is connected by a wire to a block of a **more reactive metal** (usually Magnesium or Zinc). Because the Mg is higher in the electrochemical series, it acts as the anode and corrodes (sacrifices itself), forcing the iron to act as the cathode (which cannot dissolve). 
2. **Galvanizing:** Coating iron with a thin layer of Zinc by dipping it in molten zinc (used for roofing sheets in Nigeria). Even if the zinc is scratched, it still protects the iron by sacrificial cathodic protection.
3. **Electroplating:** Using electrolysis to coat iron with a layer of an unreactive metal like Tin (used for food cans) or Chromium (used for shiny car bumpers). If a tin coating is heavily scratched, the iron will rust rapidly because iron is more reactive than tin.
4. **Painting:** Creates a physical barrier preventing air and moisture from reaching the iron. Used for cars, gates, and ships.
5. **Coating with Grease or Oil:** Another physical barrier, mainly used for moving parts of machinery, bicycle chains, and engine parts, where paint would quickly wear off.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Electrolysis | The chemical decomposition of a compound (in molten or aqueous state) by passing a direct current through it. |
| Electrolyte | A substance that conducts electricity in molten or aqueous state and is decomposed in the process. |
| Non-electrolyte | A substance that does not conduct electricity because it lacks free mobile ions. |
| Electrode | A solid conductor through which current enters and leaves the electrolyte. |
| Anode | The positive electrode; the site of oxidation (loss of electrons). |
| Cathode | The negative electrode; the site of reduction (gain of electrons). |
| Faraday ($F$) | The total electrical charge of one mole of electrons ($96,500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$). |
| Active electrode | An electrode that takes part in the chemical reaction (e.g. Copper in $\text{CuSO}_4$). |
| Electrochemical series | A list of elements in decreasing order of electropositivity and reactivity. |
| Galvanic cell | A cell that uses a spontaneous chemical reaction to produce electricity (e.g. a battery). |
| Cathodic protection | Protecting iron from rusting by connecting it to a more reactive metal that corrodes in its place. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Common mistake in definitions:** Students often say electrolytes conduct using "free electrons". This is wrong. Electrolytes conduct using **mobile ions**. Only metals/graphite conduct using free electrons.
- **Common mistake with Galvanic vs Electrolytic cells:** Remember the signs! In an **electrolytic** cell, Anode is (+), Cathode is (-). In a **galvanic/voltaic** cell (battery), Anode is (-), Cathode is (+). However, in **both** cells, Oxidation always occurs at the Anode, and Reduction at the Cathode (remember **AN OX, RED CAT**).
- **Brine vs Dilute $\text{NaCl}$:** In JAMB, always pay attention to the concentration of $\text{NaCl}$. Dilute $\text{NaCl}$ gives Oxygen at the anode. Concentrated $\text{NaCl}$ (Brine) gives Chlorine at the anode.
- **Unit slips in calculations:** Always convert time to seconds before calculating $Q = I \times t$.
- **Electroplating set-up:** Always remember the object to be plated MUST be the cathode (-).
- **Cell potential formula:** Be careful with signs. Subtraction of a negative number becomes addition (e.g. $0.80 - (-0.44) = 1.24$).

## Quick summary

1. Electrolytes are liquid or aqueous substances that conduct electricity via mobile ions and are chemically decomposed; non-electrolytes have no mobile ions and do not conduct.
2. Faraday's First Law relates mass to the quantity of electricity ($m \propto Q$). One Faraday ($96,500\text{ C}$) is the charge of one mole of electrons.
3. During electrolysis, cations migrate to the cathode (where they are reduced), and anions migrate to the anode (where they are oxidised).
4. The product of electrolysis depends on the ions' position in the electrochemical series, their concentration, and the nature of the electrodes.
5. Electrolysis is widely used for extracting highly reactive metals ($\text{Al}$, $\text{Na}$), purifying metals ($\text{Cu}$), electroplating, and producing important chemicals ($\text{NaOH}$, $\text{Cl}_2$).
6. Electrochemical cells generate an electromotive force (e.m.f) calculated by $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$. The anode is the metal with the more negative potential.
7. Rusting of iron is an electrolytic process that can be prevented by barrier methods (paint, grease) or sacrificial methods (cathodic protection using magnesium or zinc).

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following liquids is a non-electrolyte?**

- A. Dilute hydrochloric acid
- B. Aqueous sodium chloride
- C. Ethanol
- D. Molten potassium chloride

**Answer: C.** Ethanol is a covalent liquid that does not ionise in solution, meaning it has no mobile ions and cannot conduct electricity. The mistake behind option B is confusing a strong electrolyte in an aqueous solution with a non-electrolyte, perhaps thinking only molten salts conduct.

**2. What volume of hydrogen gas at s.t.p. is produced at the cathode when $0.1\text{ F}$ of electricity is passed through dilute tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid? (Molar volume at s.t.p. = $22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$)**

- A. $1.12\text{ dm}^3$
- B. $2.24\text{ dm}^3$
- C. $11.2\text{ dm}^3$
- D. $22.4\text{ dm}^3$

**Answer: A.** 1. Write the balanced half-equation: $2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2$. 2. This shows $2\text{ F}$ of electricity produce $1\text{ mole}$ ($22.4\text{ dm}^3$) of $\text{H}_2$ gas. 3. Set up the ratio: if $2\text{ F}$ produce $22.4\text{ dm}^3$, then $0.1\text{ F}$ produces $V = \frac{0.1\text{ F} \times 22.4\text{ dm}^3}{2\text{ F}} = 1.12\text{ dm}^3$. The mistake behind option B is assuming $1\text{ F}$ produces $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{H}_2$ gas, which would result in $0.1\text{ F} \times 22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1} = 2.24\text{ dm}^3$.

**3. In the electrolysis of aqueous copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) for the purpose of purifying copper, which electrodes are used?**

- A. Platinum anode and platinum cathode
- B. Impure copper anode and pure copper cathode
- C. Pure copper anode and impure copper cathode
- D. Graphite anode and pure copper cathode

**Answer: B.** To purify a metal via electrolysis, the anode must be the impure metal, which dissolves, and the pure metal is the cathode, where pure copper deposits. The mistake behind option C is reversing the roles; making the impure metal the cathode would plate more impurities over it or fail to refine it.

**4. During the electrolysis of dilute sodium chloride solution using platinum electrodes, the reaction at the anode is:**

- A. $\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl} + \text{e}^-$
- B. $4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2 + 4\text{e}^-$
- C. $\text{Na}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Na}$
- D. $2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2$

**Answer: B.** In dilute $\text{NaCl}$, both $\text{Cl}^-$ and $\text{OH}^-$ migrate to the anode, but $\text{OH}^-$ is discharged because it is lower in the electrochemical series and the solution is not concentrated enough to override this. The mistake behind option A is assuming that $\text{Cl}^-$ is discharged just because it is the anion from the salt, which only happens in concentrated solutions (brine).

**5. When fused (molten) sodium chloride is electrolysed using graphite electrodes, the products are:**

- A. Sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the anode
- B. Hydrogen at the cathode and chlorine at the anode
- C. Sodium at the anode and chlorine at the cathode
- D. Hydrogen at the anode and oxygen at the cathode

**Answer: A.** Fused $\text{NaCl}$ contains only $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, with no water present. $\text{Na}^+$ ions are reduced to sodium metal at the cathode, and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions are oxidised to chlorine gas at the anode. The mistake behind option B is assuming it is an aqueous solution, where water provides $\text{H}^+$ ions that would be discharged instead of $\text{Na}^+$.

**6. If concentrated $\text{NaCl}$ (brine) is electrolysed instead of dilute $\text{NaCl}$, the product at the anode changes from oxygen to chlorine. This illustrates the effect of:**

- A. Nature of electrodes
- B. Concentration of ions
- C. Position in the electrochemical series
- D. Temperature of the electrolyte

**Answer: B.** Normally, $\text{OH}^-$ is discharged because it is lower in the electrochemical series. However, the much higher concentration of $\text{Cl}^-$ ions in brine overrides the normal order. The mistake behind option C is attributing the change to the electrochemical series itself, rather than the concentration overriding it.

**7. Which of the following metals will NOT displace copper from an aqueous solution of copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI)?**

- A. Zinc
- B. Iron
- C. Magnesium
- D. Silver

**Answer: D.** A metal can only displace another metal from its salt solution if it is higher than the other metal in the electrochemical series. Silver is located below copper, so it is less reactive and cannot displace it. The mistake behind option A is thinking that zinc is lower than copper in the series, which is incorrect.

**8. Given the standard electrode potentials for $\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn} = -0.76\text{ V}$ and $\text{Ag}^+/\text{Ag} = +0.80\text{ V}$. What is the standard e.m.f. of the cell: $\text{Zn(s)} | \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} || \text{Ag}^+\text{(aq)} | \text{Ag(s)}$?**

- A. $+1.56\text{ V}$
- B. $+0.04\text{ V}$
- C. $-1.56\text{ V}$
- D. $-0.04\text{ V}$

**Answer: A.** 1. Identify the anode (left side, $\text{Zn}$) and cathode (right side, $\text{Ag}$) from the cell notation. 2. Use the formula $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$. 3. Substitute the values: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +0.80\text{ V} - (-0.76\text{ V})$. 4. Calculate the result: $0.80\text{ V} + 0.76\text{ V} = +1.56\text{ V}$. The mistake behind option B is adding the two potentials algebraically without subtracting the anode's potential (i.e. $0.80\text{ V} + (-0.76\text{ V}) = +0.04\text{ V}$).

**9. How many Faradays of electricity are required to deposit $0.2\text{ moles}$ of Aluminium?**

- A. $0.2\text{ F}$
- B. $0.6\text{ F}$
- C. $1.0\text{ F}$
- D. $3.0\text{ F}$

**Answer: B.** 1. Write the half-equation for the discharge of Aluminium: $\text{Al}^{3+} + 3\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Al}$. 2. This shows that $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{Al}$ requires $3\text{ moles}$ of electrons, which is $3\text{ F}$. 3. To find the amount for $0.2\text{ moles}$, multiply the ratio: $0.2 \times 3\text{ F} = 0.6\text{ F}$. The mistake behind option A is assuming a $1:1$ ratio ($0.2 \times 1\text{ F} = 0.2\text{ F}$), ignoring the $3+$ charge of the Aluminium ion.

**10. What mass of copper is deposited from a copper(II) salt solution when a current of $1.5\text{ A}$ flows for $1930\text{ s}$? [$\text{Cu} = 64$, $1\text{ F} = 96,500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$]**

- A. $0.48\text{ g}$
- B. $0.96\text{ g}$
- C. $1.92\text{ g}$
- D. $3.84\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 1. Write the half-equation for copper: $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$. 2. This shows $2\text{ F}$ ($2 \times 96,500\text{ C} = 193,000\text{ C}$) deposit $1\text{ mole}$ ($64\text{ g}$) of $\text{Cu}$. 3. Calculate charge $Q = I \times t = 1.5\text{ A} \times 1930\text{ s} = 2895\text{ C}$. 4. Use the mole ratio to find mass $m = \frac{64\text{ g} \times 2895\text{ C}}{193000\text{ C}} = 0.96\text{ g}$. The mistake behind option C is assuming copper requires only $1\text{ F}$ instead of $2\text{ F}$, giving $m = \frac{64 \times 2895}{96500} = 1.92\text{ g}$.
