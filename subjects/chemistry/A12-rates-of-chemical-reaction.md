## Relating Reaction Rate to the Kinetic Theory of Matter

The **rate of a chemical reaction** is the speed at which reactants are consumed or products are formed over a given period of time. Some chemical reactions, like explosions or the neutralization of a strong acid by a strong base, happen in a fraction of a second. Others, like the rusting of an iron gate in coastal areas like Lagos, take months or years.

Mathematically, the rate of reaction is defined as:
$$\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Change in amount (or concentration, mass, volume)}}{\text{Time taken}}$$

Depending on what is being measured, the units of reaction rate can be $\text{g s}^{-1}$, $\text{cm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$, or $\text{mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$.

**Objective ix: relate the rate of reaction to the kinetic theory of matter.**

The **Kinetic Theory of Matter** states that all matter is made up of tiny particles (atoms, molecules, or ions) that are in constant, random motion. Because they are moving, they possess kinetic energy. 

For a chemical reaction to occur, these moving particles must collide with one another. This is the foundation of the **Collision Theory**. However, not every collision results in a chemical reaction. A collision that successfully produces a product is called an **effective collision**.

According to the collision theory, for a collision to be effective, two strict conditions must be met:
1. **Correct Orientation:** The particles must bump into each other facing the right way so that the appropriate chemical bonds can break and form.
2. **Sufficient Energy:** The particles must collide with a minimum amount of kinetic energy. If they collide too gently, they will simply bounce off each other without reacting. 

Therefore, the **rate of a chemical reaction** directly depends on the **frequency of effective collisions**. Anything that increases the number of collisions per second, or increases the energy of the particles, will increase the rate of reaction.

## Activation Energy

**Content c: Activation energy (Qualitative treatment of Arrhenius’ law and the collision theory, effect of light).**

The minimum extra amount of energy that reacting particles must possess in order to undergo an effective collision is called the **Activation Energy ($E_a$)**. 

### Significance of Activation Energy to Chemical Reactions

**Objective x: examine the significance of activation energy to chemical reactions.**

Activation energy acts as an **energy barrier** that reactants must overcome to turn into products. 
- If a reaction has a **high activation energy**, only a few particles will have enough energy to react when they collide. The reaction will be very **slow** at room temperature.
- If a reaction has a **low activation energy**, a large fraction of the particles will have enough energy to react upon collision. The reaction will be **fast**.

### The Arrhenius Law

The relationship between temperature, activation energy, and the rate of reaction is given mathematically by the **Arrhenius equation**:
$$k = A e^{-\frac{E_a}{RT}}$$
Where:
- $k$ = Rate constant (a measure of how fast the reaction is)
- $A$ = Arrhenius constant (related to collision frequency)
- $E_a$ = Activation energy
- $R$ = Molar gas constant
- $T$ = Absolute temperature in Kelvin

**Qualitative treatment:** You do not need to perform complex calculations with this formula for JAMB. You only need to understand the relationship it shows:
1. **If Temperature ($T$) increases**, the negative exponent becomes smaller (closer to zero), making $k$ larger. Therefore, the rate of reaction increases.
2. **If Activation Energy ($E_a$) decreases** (e.g., by adding a catalyst), the negative exponent also becomes smaller, making $k$ larger. Therefore, the rate of reaction increases.

### Effect of Light on Photochemical Reactions

Some chemical reactions require light energy to overcome the activation energy barrier. These are called **photochemical reactions**. The light provides the necessary energy to break chemical bonds and initiate the reaction.

A classic example is the **halogenation of alkanes**. If a mixture of methane and chlorine gas is kept in the dark, no reaction occurs. However, in the presence of bright sunlight or ultraviolet (UV) light, the light energy splits the chlorine molecules ($\text{Cl}_2$) into highly reactive free radicals, and the reaction proceeds explosively:
$$\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \xrightarrow{\text{UV light}} \text{CH}_3\text{Cl(g)} + \text{HCl(g)}$$

## Factors Changing the Rate of a Chemical Reaction

**Content a: Elementary treatment of the following factors which can change the rate of a chemical reaction.**

### Identifying the Factors that Affect the Rates of a Chemical Reaction

**Objective i: identify the factors that affect the rates of a chemical reaction.**

Based on the kinetic and collision theories, the rate of a reaction can be changed by altering the conditions of the system. The five major factors are:
1. Temperature
2. Concentration of reactants (or Pressure for gases)
3. Surface area of solid reactants
4. Presence of a catalyst
5. Light (for photochemical reactions)

### Effect of Temperature on the Rate of Reactions

**Objective ii: determine the effects of temperature on the rate of reactions.**

An increase in temperature generally increases the rate of a chemical reaction. In many reactions, raising the temperature by just $10 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ can double the reaction rate.

**Why does this happen?**
When a substance is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. This causes them to collide more frequently. However, the most important reason is that **a much greater fraction of the particles now possesses energy equal to or greater than the activation energy ($E_a$)**. Therefore, the number of effective collisions per second drastically increases.

Two curves start at the origin. The first curve, labelled 'Lower Temperature (T_1)', goes up to a high peak, then tails off to the right. The second curve, labelled 'Higher Temperature (T_2)', has a lower and wider peak, shifted to the right, and crosses the first curve, remaining higher on the far right. A vertical dashed line on the right side of the x-axis is labelled 'Activation Energy (E_a)'. The area under both curves to the right of E_a is shaded, showing that the higher temperature curve has a much larger shaded area. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

**Examples:**
- **$\text{HCl}$ and $\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3$ (The Disappearing Cross Experiment):** 
  When dilute hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium trioxothiosulphate(II), a yellow precipitate of solid sulphur is formed, which slowly makes the solution opaque.
  $$\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3\text{(aq)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{S(s)}$$
  **Step-by-step:** A glass flask containing the $\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3$ is placed over a paper with a black cross "X". $\text{HCl}$ is added, and a stopwatch is started. As the sulphur forms, the liquid turns cloudy until the cross can no longer be seen. If the experiment is repeated with the solutions heated to a higher temperature, the cross disappears much faster.
- **$\text{Mg}$ and $\text{HCl}$:** Magnesium ribbon reacts with dilute $\text{HCl}$ to produce hydrogen gas.
  $$\text{Mg(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{MgCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$
  At higher temperatures, the rate at which bubbles of $\text{H}_2$ gas are formed increases.

### Effect of Concentration and Pressure on the Rate of a Chemical Reaction

**Objective iii: examine the effect of concentration/pressure on the rate of a chemical reaction.**

**Concentration:** Increasing the concentration of a reactant in a solution increases the reaction rate. A higher concentration means there are more reactant particles crowded into the same volume. Because they are closer together, the chance of them colliding is much higher, leading to an increased frequency of effective collisions.
- **The Iodine Clock Reaction:** This is a famous experiment where two colourless solutions are mixed. After a specific time delay, the mixture suddenly turns dark blue-black due to the formation of a starch-iodine complex. By using a higher concentration of one reactant, the time it takes for the blue-black colour to appear becomes much shorter.

**Pressure:** For gaseous systems, pressure acts just like concentration. Increasing the pressure of a gas forces its molecules closer together into a smaller volume. This crowding increases the collision frequency and speeds up the reaction. Pressure has little to no effect on liquids and solids.

### Effect of Surface Area on the Rate of a Chemical Reaction

**Objective iv: describe how the rate of a chemical is affected by surface area.**

When a solid reacts with a liquid or a gas, the reaction can only happen at the boundary where the two states meet (the surface of the solid). If the solid is in one large lump, most of its particles are hidden safely inside the lump and cannot react until the outer layers are eaten away. 

By crushing or grinding the lump into a **powder**, the hidden internal particles are exposed. This vastly increases the **surface area**, providing many more contact points for collisions, and thereby increasing the reaction rate.

**Example: Marble ($\text{CaCO}_3$) and $\text{HCl}$**
$$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$
If equal masses of marble are reacted with the same volume and concentration of dilute $\text{HCl}$:
1. **Lumps of the same mass:** The reaction fizzes steadily but slowly because only the outside of the lumps is exposed.
2. **Powdered form:** The reaction fizzes violently and finishes very quickly because a massive surface area is exposed to the acid.

### Types of Catalysts and Their Effects

**Objective v: determine the types of catalysts suitable for different reactions and their effects.**

A **catalyst** is a substance that alters the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed or chemically changed at the end of the reaction. While a few catalysts slow down reactions, the word is almost always used to mean a substance that speeds up a reaction (a positive catalyst).

**How it works:** A catalyst speeds up a reaction by providing an **alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy**. Because the energy barrier is lowered, a much larger fraction of the colliding particles will have enough energy to react. 

**Important facts about catalysts:**
- A catalyst does **not** change the overall enthalpy change ($\Delta H$) of the reaction.
- A catalyst does **not** increase the amount (yield) of product formed; it only makes the product form faster.
- Catalysts are highly specific (a catalyst for one reaction might not work for another).

**Examples from the syllabus:**
- **Decomposition of Hydrogen Peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$):** At room temperature, $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ decomposes into water and oxygen very slowly. If a pinch of black **manganese(IV) oxide ($\text{MnO}_2$)** powder is added, the decomposition becomes explosive and oxygen bubbles rapidly. 
  $$2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\text{(aq)} \xrightarrow{\text{MnO}_2} 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$$
- **Decomposition of Potassium trioxochlorate(V) ($\text{KClO}_3$):** Heating $\text{KClO}_3$ produces oxygen gas slowly, but adding **$\text{MnO}_2$** as a catalyst speeds up the reaction significantly, even at a lower temperature.
  $$2\text{KClO}_3\text{(s)} \xrightarrow{\text{MnO}_2, \Delta} 2\text{KCl(s)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)}$$

## Moderating Effects in Chemical Reactions

**Objective vi: determine ways of moderating these effects in chemical reactions.**

To "moderate" a reaction means to slow it down or keep it under control when it is happening too fast or when it is unwanted. This is done by reversing the factors that increase reaction rates.

1. **Lowering the temperature (Cooling/Refrigeration):** 
   In many Nigerian homes, cooked foods like soups or stews will spoil quickly if left out in the hot afternoon heat. The high temperatures speed up the bacterial and chemical reactions that cause decay. We moderate this by storing the food in a refrigerator. The low temperature removes heat energy, meaning fewer particles have the required activation energy, and the rate of spoilage drops drastically.
2. **Adding Inhibitors (Negative Catalysts):** 
   An inhibitor is a substance added to slow down a chemical reaction. It works by increasing the activation energy or by tying up reactants. For example, acetanilide or phosphoric acid is often added to bottles of hydrogen peroxide as a stabilizer to stop it from decomposing on the shelf.
3. **Reducing Surface Area (Coating/Painting):** 
   To stop an iron gate from rusting (a chemical reaction with oxygen and water), it is painted. The paint acts as a barrier, reducing the exposed surface area to zero and moderating the reaction.
4. **Storage in Dark Bottles:** 
   Light-sensitive chemicals (like silver trioxonitrate(V) or hydrogen peroxide) will decompose rapidly if exposed to sunlight. We moderate the effect of light by storing them in dark amber bottles, which block UV rays.

## Reaction Rate Curves

**Content b: Reaction rate curves.**

A **reaction rate curve** is a graph that shows how the amount (or concentration, mass, or volume) of a reactant or product changes as time passes. 

### Interpreting Reaction Rate Curves

**Objective vii: interpret reaction rate curves.**

[IMAGE: A typical reaction rate curve plotting concentration against time. A 2D graph with 'Concentration' on the y-axis and 'Time' on the x-axis. Two curves are shown. One curve starts high on the y-axis and curves downwards, becoming less steep and eventually horizontal; it is labelled 'Reactants'. The other curve starts at the origin (0,0) and curves upwards, becoming less steep and eventually horizontal; it is labelled 'Products'. Tangent lines are drawn at the beginning of the 'Products' curve to show a steep initial rate, and later to show a decreasing rate. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

When you look at a reaction rate curve for a **product** being formed:
1. **The start (Initial Rate):** The curve is very steep. The gradient (slope) of the curve at any point gives the rate of reaction. The rate is highest at the beginning because the concentration of reactants is at its maximum.
2. **The middle:** As reactants are used up, their concentration drops, and the collision frequency decreases. The curve becomes less steep, showing that the rate of reaction is slowing down.
3. **The end:** Eventually, the curve flattens out into a horizontal line (gradient = 0). This means the reaction has stopped completely, usually because the limiting reactant has been completely consumed.

### Deducing Activation Energy from Reaction Rate Curves

**Objective xi: deduce the value of activation energy ($E_a$) from reaction rate curves.**

Activation energy ($E_a$) can be deduced from graphical plots in two main ways:
1. **From an Energy Profile Diagram:** The activation energy is deduced by measuring the vertical distance (height) from the starting energy plateau of the reactants to the very top of the energy peak (the activated complex).
2. **From an Arrhenius Plot:** According to the Arrhenius law, if you perform the same reaction at several different temperatures, you can plot a specific type of rate curve. By plotting the natural logarithm of the rate constant ($\ln k$) on the y-axis against the inverse of the temperature ($1/T$) on the x-axis, you will get a straight line. The slope (gradient) of this straight line is equal to $-E_a / R$. Therefore, $E_a$ can be calculated directly by multiplying the slope by $-R$.

## Solving Simple Problems on the Rate of Reactions

**Objective viii: solve simple problems on the rate of reactions.**

Problems on reaction rates usually ask you to calculate the rate from given data, convert units, or use stoichiometric mole ratios to relate the rate of one substance to another.

**Example 1: Rate of mass loss**
A student reacts $5.0 \text{ g}$ of marble lumps ($\text{CaCO}_3$) with excess dilute $\text{HCl}$. After $60 \text{ s}$, the mass of unreacted marble is $3.2 \text{ g}$. What is the average rate of reaction in $\text{g s}^{-1}$?
1. Write the formula for the rate: $\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Change in mass}}{\text{Time taken}}$.
2. Find the mass of marble that reacted: $5.0 \text{ g} - 3.2 \text{ g} = 1.8 \text{ g}$.
3. State the time taken: $t = 60 \text{ s}$.
4. Substitute the values into the formula: $\text{Rate} = \frac{1.8 \text{ g}}{60 \text{ s}}$.
5. Calculate the final answer: $0.03 \text{ g s}^{-1}$.

**Example 2: Rate from volume of gas**
In the reaction between magnesium and hydrochloric acid, $120 \text{ cm}^3$ of hydrogen gas is collected in $2$ minutes. Calculate the rate of formation of hydrogen gas in $\text{cm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$.
1. Note the volume of gas produced: $V = 120 \text{ cm}^3$.
2. Convert the time to seconds: $2 \text{ minutes} = 2 \times 60 \text{ s} = 120 \text{ s}$.
3. Write the rate formula: $\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Volume produced}}{\text{Time taken}}$.
4. Substitute the values: $\text{Rate} = \frac{120 \text{ cm}^3}{120 \text{ s}}$.
5. Calculate the final answer: $1.0 \text{ cm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$.

**Example 3: Stoichiometric relations**
Consider the Haber process for producing ammonia:
$$\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)}$$
If hydrogen gas is consumed at a rate of $0.060 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$, what is the rate of production of ammonia?
1. Write the balanced equation and find the mole ratio of $\text{H}_2$ to $\text{NH}_3$. The stoichiometric ratio is $3:2$.
2. This means that for every $3$ moles of $\text{H}_2$ consumed, $2$ moles of $\text{NH}_3$ are produced.
3. Write the mathematical relationship: $\frac{\text{Rate of NH}_3}{2} = \frac{\text{Rate of H}_2}{3}$, so $\text{Rate of NH}_3 = \frac{2}{3} \times \text{Rate of H}_2$.
4. Substitute the given rate: $\text{Rate of NH}_3 = \frac{2}{3} \times 0.060 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$.
5. Calculate the final answer: $2 \times 0.020 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1} = 0.040 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Rate of reaction** | The speed at which reactants are consumed or products are formed over time. |
| **Collision theory** | The theory that particles must collide with the correct orientation and sufficient energy to react. |
| **Effective collision** | A collision between particles that successfully results in a chemical reaction. |
| **Activation energy ($E_a$)** | The minimum energy that colliding particles must possess for a reaction to occur. |
| **Catalyst** | A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, without being consumed. |
| **Inhibitor** | A substance added to a reaction to slow it down (sometimes called a negative catalyst). |
| **Photochemical reaction** | A chemical reaction that is initiated or accelerated by the absorption of light energy. |
| **Reaction rate curve** | A graph showing how the amount of a reactant or product changes with time. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Catalysts do not increase yield:** A common JAMB trick is to ask what a catalyst does to the amount of product formed. It **does not** increase the final amount of product; it only helps the reaction reach the end faster.
- **Surface area applies to reactants, not products:** Only the surface area of a solid *reactant* affects the rate. Grinding a product into powder after it is formed does nothing to the speed of the reaction.
- **Don't confuse activation energy with enthalpy change ($\Delta H$):** Activation energy ($E_a$) is the peak energy barrier you must cross to start the reaction. Enthalpy change is the overall difference in energy between the start (reactants) and the end (products).
- **Unit conversions matter:** Always check if a rate calculation question asks for the answer in $\text{s}^{-1}$. If the time given is in minutes, forgetting to multiply by 60 is the most common reason for picking the wrong option. 

## Quick summary

1. The rate of a chemical reaction is measured as the change in amount, concentration, mass, or volume of a substance per unit of time.
2. According to the collision theory, particles must collide with sufficient energy (Activation Energy) and correct orientation for a reaction to occur.
3. Increasing the temperature speeds up a reaction because it increases the kinetic energy of particles, causing a greater fraction of them to exceed the activation energy barrier.
4. Increasing the concentration or pressure crowds particles together, increasing the frequency of collisions.
5. Grinding a solid reactant into a powder increases its surface area, providing more contact points for collisions and vastly speeding up the reaction.
6. A catalyst speeds up a reaction by lowering the activation energy barrier. Manganese(IV) oxide ($\text{MnO}_2$) is used for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide and potassium trioxochlorate(V).
7. Reactions can be moderated (slowed down) by refrigeration, reducing surface area (like painting), adding inhibitors, or storing chemicals in dark bottles.
8. A reaction rate curve is steepest at the start, becomes less steep as reactants are used up, and flattens horizontally when the reaction stops.

## Practice questions

**1. Calculate the rate of reaction if $2.4 \text{ g}$ of magnesium completely reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid in $2 \text{ minutes}$.**

- A. $0.02 \text{ g s}^{-1}$
- B. $1.20 \text{ g s}^{-1}$
- C. $0.20 \text{ g s}^{-1}$
- D. $4.80 \text{ g s}^{-1}$

**Answer: A.**
1. Identify the given mass of magnesium that reacted: $2.4 \text{ g}$.
2. Convert the time to seconds: $2 \text{ minutes} = 2 \times 60 \text{ s} = 120 \text{ s}$.
3. Use the rate formula: $\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Mass reacted}}{\text{Time}}$.
4. Substitute the values: $\text{Rate} = \frac{2.4 \text{ g}}{120 \text{ s}}$.
5. Calculate the result: $0.02 \text{ g s}^{-1}$.
Option B ($1.20 \text{ g s}^{-1}$) is a common mistake from failing to convert the $2 \text{ minutes}$ into seconds and mistakenly calculating $2.4 / 2$.

**2. In an experiment, $60 \text{ cm}^3$ of carbon(IV) oxide gas is produced by the reaction of marble and hydrochloric acid in $30 \text{ s}$. What is the average rate of formation of the gas in $\text{dm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$?**

- A. $2.0 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$
- B. $0.002 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$
- C. $0.5 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$
- D. $2000 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Identify the given volume of gas: $60 \text{ cm}^3$.
2. Convert the volume to $\text{dm}^3$ by dividing by $1000$: $60 \text{ cm}^3 / 1000 = 0.060 \text{ dm}^3$.
3. Note the time taken: $30 \text{ s}$.
4. Use the rate formula: $\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Volume}}{\text{Time}}$.
5. Substitute the values: $\text{Rate} = \frac{0.060 \text{ dm}^3}{30 \text{ s}}$.
6. Calculate the result: $0.002 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$.
Option A ($2.0 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ s}^{-1}$) is a common mistake from forgetting to convert the volume from $\text{cm}^3$ to $\text{dm}^3$ and simply dividing $60 / 30$.

**3. Consider the reaction: $2\text{NO}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{NO(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$. If the rate of decomposition of $\text{NO}_2$ is $4.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$, what is the rate of formation of $\text{O}_2$?**

- A. $2.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$
- B. $4.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$
- C. $8.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$
- D. $1.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$

**Answer: A.**
1. Write the molar ratio from the equation: $2 \text{ moles}$ of $\text{NO}_2$ produce $1 \text{ mole}$ of $\text{O}_2$.
2. State the relationship between their rates: $\text{Rate of O}_2 = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Rate of NO}_2$.
3. Substitute the given rate: $\text{Rate of O}_2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$.
4. Calculate the final answer: $2.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$.
Option C ($8.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$) is a common mistake from multiplying the rate by $2$ instead of dividing by $2$.

**4. Which of the following factors does not affect the rate of a reaction between a solid and a liquid?**

- A. Concentration of the liquid
- B. Temperature of the system
- C. Surface area of the products
- D. Presence of a catalyst

**Answer: C.**
1. Identify the states in the question: The reactants are a solid and a liquid (e.g., $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)}$ and aqueous $\text{HCl}$).
2. Recall the factors affecting reaction rate: Surface area only applies to the solid reactant.
3. Understand product surface area: The surface area of the products (e.g., $1 \text{ g}$ of a product solid) does not influence how fast the reactants are colliding.
4. Conclude: Therefore, option C is correct.
Option A is a common mistake because students forget that concentration (in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$) of the liquid does affect the rate.

**5. According to the collision theory, an increase in temperature increases the rate of reaction mainly because it:**

- A. decreases the activation energy of the reaction
- B. increases the number of effective collisions by increasing the kinetic energy of reacting particles
- C. lowers the enthalpy change of the reaction
- D. increases the concentration of the reactants

**Answer: B.**
1. Consider the collision theory: Reactant particles must collide with energy $\ge E_a$.
2. State the effect of temperature: An increase in temperature (e.g., by $10 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) increases the kinetic energy of particles in Joules ($\text{J}$).
3. Identify the main effect: A much greater fraction of particles now has energy $> E_a$.
4. Conclude: This drastically increases the number of effective collisions per second ($1 \text{ s}$).
Option A is a mistake from confusing the effect of a catalyst (which lowers $E_a$) with temperature.

**6. Equal masses of powdered marble and marble lumps are reacted with the same volume and concentration of dilute hydrochloric acid. The powdered marble reacts faster because it has:**

- A. a lower activation energy
- B. a larger exposed surface area for effective collisions
- C. a higher concentration
- D. higher kinetic energy

**Answer: B.**
1. Identify the solid reactant: Marble ($\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)}$).
2. Compare the forms: Powdered marble vs. lumps of equal mass (e.g., $5.0 \text{ g}$).
3. Relate form to surface area: Grinding a solid into powder greatly increases its exposed surface area in $\text{cm}^2$.
4. Conclude: A larger surface area provides more contact points for effective collisions, making the reaction faster.
Option C is a mistake from confusing solid surface area with the concentration of a solution in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$.

**7. In the reaction between sodium trioxothiosulphate(II) and dilute hydrochloric acid, an increase in temperature speeds up the reaction. This is because:**

- A. it decreases the pressure of the system
- B. it increases the activation energy
- C. a greater fraction of particles possess energy equal to or greater than the activation energy
- D. it decreases the collision frequency

**Answer: C.**
1. Identify the reaction: $\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3\text{(aq)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)}$ forming sulphur.
2. Recall the effect of temperature: Heating increases the kinetic energy of the particles in Joules ($\text{J}$).

**8. Which of the following catalysts is used to speed up the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide?**

- A. Iron
- B. Manganese(IV) oxide
- C. Vanadium(V) oxide
- D. Finely divided nickel

**Answer: B.**
1. Identify the reactant: Hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$).
2. Recall its decomposition: It decomposes into $\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ and $\text{O}_2\text{(g)}$.
3. Identify the standard catalyst: Manganese(IV) oxide ($\text{MnO}_2$) is used in the laboratory to lower $E_a$ for this reaction.
4. Conclude: Option B is the correct catalyst.
Option A is a mistake from confusing this with the Haber process, which uses finely divided iron ($\text{Fe}$) as a catalyst.

**9. The reaction between methane and chlorine in the presence of ultraviolet light is an example of:**

- A. a catalytic reaction
- B. a photochemical reaction
- C. an endothermic reaction
- D. an addition reaction

**Answer: B.**
1. Identify the reactants and conditions: Methane ($\text{CH}_4$) and chlorine ($\text{Cl}_2$) reacting under UV light.
2. Recall the role of light: The UV light provides the energy ($E = h\nu$) needed to break the $\text{Cl}_2$ bonds into free radicals.
3. Classify the reaction: Reactions initiated by light energy are called photochemical reactions.
4. Conclude: It is a photochemical reaction.
Option A is a mistake from thinking the light acts as a physical catalyst rather than an energy source.

**10. In an energy profile diagram for an exothermic reaction, the activation energy is represented by the difference in energy between the:**

- A. reactants and the products
- B. products and the activated complex
- C. reactants and the activated complex
- D. reactants and the origin

**Answer: C.**
1. Visualise the energy profile diagram: Energy $E$ on the y-axis, reaction progress on the x-axis.
2. Identify the starting point: The energy level of the reactants ($H_1$).
3. Identify the peak: The activated complex at the top of the energy barrier.
4. Conclude: The activation energy ($E_a$) is the positive difference from the reactants to the activated complex.
Option A is a mistake from confusing $E_a$ with the overall enthalpy change ($\Delta H$), which is the difference between reactants and products.
