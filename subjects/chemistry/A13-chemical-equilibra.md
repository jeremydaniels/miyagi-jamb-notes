## Reversible reactions

Most of the chemical reactions you learn about in early chemistry go to completion. For example, when magnesium burns in oxygen to form magnesium oxide, it does not spontaneously turn back into magnesium and oxygen. These are called **irreversible reactions**, and they are written with a single, one-way arrow ($\rightarrow$).

However, many reactions can go in both directions. These are called **reversible reactions** and are represented by a double half-arrow ($\rightleftharpoons$). 

In a reversible reaction:
- The reaction reading from left to right is the **forward reaction**.
- The reaction reading from right to left is the **backward (or reverse) reaction**.

For a reversible reaction to reach equilibrium, it must happen in a **closed system**. A closed system means that no reactants or products can escape into the atmosphere, and no new substances can enter. 

### Examples of reversible reactions from the syllabus

1. **Action of steam on iron:**
   When steam is passed over red-hot iron, iron (II, III) oxide and hydrogen gas are formed. If hydrogen gas is passed over the hot iron oxide, it reduces it back to iron and steam. In a closed container, both reactions occur at the same time:
   $$3\text{Fe(s)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4\text{(s)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

2. **Decomposition of dinitrogen tetraoxide:**
   Dinitrogen tetraoxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$) is a colourless gas. When heated, it decomposes into nitrogen (IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$), which is a dark brown gas. If the brown gas is cooled, it combines back into the colourless gas.
   $$\text{N}_2\text{O}_4\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NO}_2\text{(g)}$$
   (Colourless) $\rightleftharpoons$ (Brown)

### What is dynamic equilibrium?

When a reversible reaction starts in a closed container, the reactants are at a high concentration, so the rate of the forward reaction is very fast. As products are formed, they start to react together to reform the reactants, starting the backward reaction. 

Over time, the forward reaction slows down (because the concentration of reactants drops) and the backward reaction speeds up (because the concentration of products builds up). Eventually, a point is reached where the **rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction**. This state is called **dynamic equilibrium**.

It is called "dynamic" because the reactions have not stopped. The forward and backward reactions are still actively happening, but they are perfectly balancing each other out. At this point, the **macroscopic properties** (measurable things like concentration, pressure, and colour intensity) remain completely constant.

## Factors affecting the position of equilibrium

**Objective i: identify the factors that affect the position of equilibrium of a chemical reaction.**

The "position of equilibrium" refers to the relative amounts of reactants and products at equilibrium. 
- If an equilibrium mixture contains mostly products and very little reactant, we say the equilibrium position lies to the **right**.
- If an equilibrium mixture contains mostly reactants and very little product, we say the equilibrium position lies to the **left**.

The position of equilibrium can be changed (shifted) by altering the conditions of the reaction. The three main factors that affect the equilibrium position are:
1. **Concentration** of the reactants or products.
2. **Temperature** of the reaction mixture.
3. **Pressure** (or volume) of the system, but **only for reactions involving gases**.

**What about catalysts?**
A catalyst **does not** affect the position of equilibrium. It lowers the activation energy of both the forward and backward reactions equally. A catalyst only reduces the *time* it takes for the system to reach dynamic equilibrium, but it does not change the final yield of products.

## Predicting the effects of factors (Le Chatelier's principle)

**Objective ii: predict the effects of each factor on the position of equilibrium.**

To predict exactly how an equilibrium position will shift when we change a factor, we use a rule discovered by the French chemist Henri Le Chatelier in 1884. 

**Le Chatelier's Principle** states that:
*If a change (or stress) is applied to a system in dynamic equilibrium, the system will shift its position to oppose or counteract the change.*

In simple terms: if you try to do something to the system, the system will try to do the exact opposite.

### 1. Effect of Concentration

- **Adding a substance:** If you increase the concentration of a reactant, the system will try to decrease it by using it up. Therefore, the equilibrium shifts to the **right** (favouring the forward reaction) to produce more products.
- **Removing a substance:** If you remove a product as it forms, the system will try to replace it. The equilibrium shifts to the **right** to make more of that product.

**Example:**
$$\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)}$$
If you pump more $\text{N}_2$ into the container, the system uses the extra $\text{N}_2$ by making more $\text{NH}_3$. The position of equilibrium shifts to the right. 

### 2. Effect of Temperature

To predict the effect of temperature, you must know whether the forward reaction is **exothermic** (gives out heat to the surroundings) or **endothermic** (takes in heat from the surroundings).
- If the forward reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H$ is negative), the backward reaction must be endothermic.
- If the forward reaction is endothermic ($\Delta H$ is positive), the backward reaction must be exothermic.

According to Le Chatelier's principle:
- **Increasing the temperature:** The system tries to cool down by absorbing heat. It favours the **endothermic** direction.
- **Decreasing the temperature:** The system tries to heat up by releasing heat. It favours the **exothermic** direction.

**Example:**
$$\text{N}_2\text{O}_4\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NO}_2\text{(g)} \quad \Delta H = +58 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$
The forward reaction is endothermic (positive $\Delta H$).
- If we **heat** the mixture, the system shifts in the endothermic direction (the forward reaction) to absorb the extra heat. The equilibrium shifts to the **right**, and the mixture turns a darker brown because more $\text{NO}_2$ is formed.
- If we **cool** the mixture, the system shifts in the exothermic direction (the backward reaction) to replace the lost heat. The equilibrium shifts to the **left**, and the mixture turns colourless as $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$ reforms.

### 3. Effect of Pressure

Pressure only affects systems involving **gases**. To predict the effect, you must count the **number of moles of gas** on each side of the balanced equation.

According to Le Chatelier's principle:
- **Increasing pressure (or decreasing volume):** The system tries to reduce the pressure. It does this by shifting to the side with **fewer moles of gas** molecules (because fewer molecules exert less pressure).
- **Decreasing pressure (or increasing volume):** The system tries to increase the pressure. It shifts to the side with **more moles of gas** molecules.
- **If both sides have the same number of moles of gas**, changing the pressure has **no effect** on the position of equilibrium.

**Example:**
$$\text{N}_2\text{O}_4\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NO}_2\text{(g)}$$
- Left side: $1 \text{ mol}$ of gas.
- Right side: $2 \text{ mol}$ of gas.
- If we **increase the pressure**, the system shifts to the left (fewer moles) to reduce the pressure. The mixture becomes paler.
- If we **decrease the pressure**, the system shifts to the right (more moles). The mixture becomes darker brown.

## Industrial applications of Le Chatelier's principle

**Objective iii: specify the industrial processes where Le Chatelier’s principle is required.**

Many important chemicals are manufactured using reversible reactions. Industrial chemists use Le Chatelier’s principle to choose conditions that will maximize the **yield** (amount of product) while keeping the **rate** fast enough to be profitable. Often, the ideal conditions for a perfect yield make the reaction far too slow, so a **compromise** must be made.

The JAMB syllabus expects you to know two major industrial processes: the **Haber Process** and the **Contact Process**.

### 1. The Haber Process (Manufacture of Ammonia)

Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) is an essential chemical used to make fertilizers. It is manufactured by reacting nitrogen gas (from the air) with hydrogen gas (usually from natural gas).

**Equation:**
$$\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} \quad \Delta H = -92 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$

**Applying Le Chatelier's Principle:**
- **Temperature:** The forward reaction is exothermic. A **low temperature** would push the equilibrium to the right, giving a massive yield of ammonia. However, at a low temperature, the reaction rate is far too slow to be useful in a factory. 
- **Pressure:** There are $4 \text{ mol}$ of gas on the left ($1\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2$) and $2 \text{ mol}$ of gas on the right ($2\text{NH}_3$). A **high pressure** pushes the equilibrium to the right (fewer moles) and also speeds up the reaction by pushing molecules closer together. However, extremely high pressures are dangerous and require very expensive pipes and equipment.

**The Compromise Conditions used in industry:**
| Condition | Value | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Temperature** | About $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | A compromise. High enough to give a reasonable rate, but low enough to still give an acceptable yield (about 15%). |
| **Pressure** | $200 \text{ to } 250 \text{ atm}$ | High enough to push equilibrium to the right and speed up the reaction, but safe and economical enough to build. |
| **Catalyst** | Finely divided iron ($\text{Fe}$) | Speeds up the reaction so it reaches equilibrium faster at $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. (It does not increase the final yield). |

*Note: In the factory, the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are constantly recycled, and the ammonia is continually removed by cooling it into a liquid. Removing the product as it forms constantly pulls the equilibrium to the right.*

### 2. The Contact Process (Manufacture of Tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid)

Tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), commonly known as sulfuric acid, is a heavily manufactured chemical. The critical reversible step in its manufacture is the oxidation of sulphur (IV) oxide to sulphur (VI) oxide.

**Equation:**
$$2\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3\text{(g)} \quad \Delta H = -197 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$
(Textbooks quote values from about $-196$ to $-198\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ for the reaction as written, that is, per $2$ mol of $\text{SO}_3$ formed. The key point for JAMB is that the reaction is exothermic.)

**Applying Le Chatelier's Principle:**
- **Temperature:** The forward reaction is highly exothermic. A **low temperature** would favour a high yield of $\text{SO}_3$, but makes the reaction too slow.
- **Pressure:** There are $3 \text{ mol}$ of gas on the left ($2\text{SO}_2 + 1\text{O}_2$) and $2 \text{ mol}$ on the right ($2\text{SO}_3$). A **high pressure** would favour the product. However, the equilibrium already lies very far to the right (yield is over 98%) even at normal pressure.

**The Compromise Conditions used in industry:**
| Condition | Value | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Temperature** | About $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | A compromise temperature for a reasonably fast rate and an excellent yield. |
| **Pressure** | $1 \text{ to } 2 \text{ atm}$ | High pressure is unnecessary because the yield is already $\sim 98\%$ at normal atmospheric pressure. Building high-pressure plants would be a waste of money. |
| **Catalyst** | Vanadium (V) oxide ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$) | Speeds up the reaction without being consumed. |

## Effects of factors on the equilibrium constant

**Objective iv: determine the effects of these factors on equilibrium constant.**

Every reversible reaction at equilibrium has an **equilibrium constant**, usually written as $K$ (or $K_c$ when working with concentrations in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$, and $K_p$ for gas pressures in $\text{atm}$). 

For a general reversible reaction:
$$a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightleftharpoons c\text{C} + d\text{D}$$
The equilibrium constant expression is:
$$K_c = \frac{[\text{C}]^c [\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a [\text{B}]^b}$$
(Where the square brackets $[\,]$ mean "concentration of" in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$). 

*Note: The JAMB syllabus specifically notes that **no calculation is required**, so you only need to know how the value of $K_c$ behaves conceptually when conditions change.*

**The Golden Rule for JAMB:**
**Temperature is the ONLY factor that can change the numerical value of the equilibrium constant ($K_c$).**

Let us see how the different factors affect $K_c$:

| Factor | Does it change the position of equilibrium? | Does it change the value of the Equilibrium Constant ($K_c$)? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Concentration** | Yes | **No**. The system shifts *so that* the new ratio of products to reactants still equals the old $K_c$. |
| **Pressure / Volume** | Yes (for gases with unequal moles) | **No**. The system shifts just enough to keep $K_c$ identical. |
| **Catalyst** | No | **No**. It speeds up forward and backward reactions equally. |
| **Temperature** | Yes | **YES.** This is the only physical factor that permanently alters $K_c$. |

### How does temperature change the equilibrium constant?
The direction the equilibrium constant shifts depends on whether the forward reaction is exothermic or endothermic.

1. **If the forward reaction is EXOTHERMIC ($\Delta H$ is negative):**
   - **Increasing temperature** shifts the equilibrium to the left (towards reactants). The numerator of the $K_c$ fraction decreases and the denominator increases, so $K_c$ **decreases**.
   - **Decreasing temperature** shifts the equilibrium to the right (towards products), so $K_c$ **increases**.

2. **If the forward reaction is ENDOTHERMIC ($\Delta H$ is positive):**
   - **Increasing temperature** shifts the equilibrium to the right (towards products), so $K_c$ **increases**.
   - **Decreasing temperature** shifts the equilibrium to the left (towards reactants), so $K_c$ **decreases**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Reversible reaction** | A chemical reaction that can proceed in both the forward and backward directions, represented by $\rightleftharpoons$. |
| **Dynamic equilibrium** | The point in a reversible reaction where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction, keeping macroscopic properties constant. |
| **Closed system** | A physical setup where no matter can escape to or enter from the surroundings. |
| **Le Chatelier’s Principle** | A rule stating that if a change is made to a system at equilibrium, the system will shift its position to oppose that change. |
| **Exothermic** | A reaction that releases heat to the surroundings ($\Delta H$ is negative). |
| **Endothermic** | A reaction that absorbs heat from the surroundings ($\Delta H$ is positive). |
| **Equilibrium constant ($K_c$)** | A numerical ratio of the concentrations of products to reactants at equilibrium. Its value is altered only by temperature changes. |
| **Haber process** | The industrial manufacture of ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) from nitrogen and hydrogen gases. |
| **Contact process** | The industrial manufacture of tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), involving the reversible conversion of $\text{SO}_2$ to $\text{SO}_3$. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Catalysts trap:** JAMB frequently asks how a catalyst affects the position of equilibrium or the equilibrium constant. A common mistake is thinking it shifts the equilibrium to the right to make more products. **It does not.** It only lowers the activation energy ($E_a$), making the system reach equilibrium faster. It has no effect on the yield or on $K_c$.
- **Pressure trick:** When checking the effect of pressure, **count the moles of GAS only**. Ignore solids or liquids in the equation. For example, in $3\text{Fe(s)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4\text{(s)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$, there are $4 \text{ mol}$ of gas on the left and $4 \text{ mol}$ of gas on the right. Changing the pressure in $\text{atm}$ has **no effect** on this equilibrium position.
- **Equilibrium constant rule:** Only temperature changes the value of $K_c$. Do not let a question trick you into saying a change in concentration or pressure changes the constant. 
- **Compromise conditions:** Remember the temperatures for both the Haber and Contact processes ($\sim 450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) are compromises. Because their forward reactions are exothermic, a lower temperature would theoretically give a better yield, but it is too slow.

## Quick summary

1. A reversible reaction in a closed system eventually reaches dynamic equilibrium, where the forward and backward rates are equal, and concentrations remain constant.
2. The position of equilibrium is affected by changes in concentration, pressure (for gases), and temperature.
3. Le Chatelier's Principle states that a system at equilibrium will shift its position to oppose any applied change in conditions.
4. Increasing pressure shifts a gaseous equilibrium to the side with fewer moles of gas. If gas moles are equal, pressure has no effect.
5. Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction. Decreasing temperature favours the exothermic direction.
6. The Haber process (making $\text{NH}_3$) relies on a compromise temperature of $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, $200 \text{ atm}$ pressure, and an iron catalyst. The Contact process uses $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, $1-2 \text{ atm}$, and a $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ catalyst.
7. The value of the equilibrium constant ($K_c$) is changed **only** by temperature; a catalyst only speeds up the time taken to reach equilibrium.

## Practice questions

**1. In a chemical system at dynamic equilibrium, which of the following is true?**

- A. The reactions stop completely at $0 \text{ s}$.
- B. The concentrations of reactants and products are equal at $1.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
- C. The rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$.
- D. Only the forward reaction occurs at $298 \text{ K}$.

**Answer: C.**
1. Identify the definition of dynamic equilibrium: the rates of the forward and backward reactions are exactly equal. Let the forward rate be $R_f$ and the backward rate be $R_b$.
2. At equilibrium, $R_f = R_b$ (measured in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$).
3. Because the rates are equal, the macroscopic concentrations of the reactants and products remain constant over time $t$.
4. Mistake behind option B: A common mistake is assuming that equilibrium means the amounts or concentrations are strictly equal (e.g., $1.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ of each). They are constant, but rarely equal.

**2. Which of the following factors does NOT affect the position of equilibrium in a reversible reaction?**

- A. Temperature in $\text{K}$
- B. Pressure in $\text{atm}$
- C. Concentration in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$
- D. Catalyst mass in $\text{g}$

**Answer: D.**
1. List the factors that affect equilibrium position according to Le Chatelier's Principle: concentration ($C$), pressure ($P$), and temperature ($T$).
2. Analyze the role of a catalyst: A catalyst lowers the activation energy ($E_a$) of both the forward and backward reactions by the exact same amount in $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
3. Conclude that the rates increase equally, so the system reaches equilibrium faster but the final equilibrium position (yield) is unchanged.
4. Mistake behind option A: A common mistake is thinking temperature only affects the rate. Temperature always shifts the equilibrium position depending on whether $\Delta H$ is positive or negative. Option D is the correct answer because catalysts have no effect on the position.

**3. Consider the Haber process equation: $\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)}$, where $\Delta H = -92 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. Which of the following conditions theoretically produces the highest equilibrium yield of ammonia?**

- A. High temperature ($800 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and low pressure ($1 \text{ atm}$)
- B. Low temperature ($200 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and high pressure ($400 \text{ atm}$)
- C. High temperature ($800 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and high pressure ($400 \text{ atm}$)
- D. Low temperature ($200 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and low pressure ($1 \text{ atm}$)

**Answer: B.**
1. Analyze pressure: There are $1 + 3 = 4 \text{ mol}$ of gas on the left and $2 \text{ mol}$ of gas on the right. High pressure (e.g., $400 \text{ atm}$) shifts equilibrium to the right (towards fewer moles) to increase yield.
2. Analyze temperature: The forward reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H = -92 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$). Lowering the temperature (e.g., $200 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) forces the system to produce heat, shifting equilibrium to the right to increase yield.
3. Combine the ideal conditions for maximum yield: low temperature and high pressure.
4. Mistake behind option C: A common mistake is assuming that because industry heats the reaction to $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, high temperature gives the best yield. High temperature is actually a compromise for reaction speed, not yield.

**4. The value of the equilibrium constant ($K_c$) for a specific reaction can be changed by altering the:**

- A. volume of the container in $\text{dm}^3$
- B. pressure of the system in $\text{atm}$
- C. concentration of reactants in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$
- D. temperature of the system in $\text{K}$

**Answer: D.**
1. Define the equilibrium constant $K_c$. For $a\text{A} \rightleftharpoons b\text{B}$, $K_c = \frac{[\text{B}]^b}{[\text{A}]^a}$.
2. Note that changes in concentration, pressure, or volume cause the equilibrium concentrations to shift in a way that strictly maintains the same numerical value of $K_c$.
3. Identify that changing the temperature ($T$ in $\text{K}$) alters the kinetic energy of particles, affecting the forward and backward reaction rates differently, which establishes a brand new $K_c$ value.
4. Mistake behind option C: A common mistake is seeing concentration in the $K_c$ formula and assuming changing a concentration permanently changes the constant itself. The system shifts to keep $K_c$ identical.

**5. What is the effect of increasing the pressure on the equilibrium position of $\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \text{I}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{HI(g)}$?**

- A. The equilibrium shifts to the right, producing more $\text{HI}$ in $\text{mol}$.
- B. The equilibrium shifts to the left, producing more $\text{H}_2$ in $\text{mol}$.
- C. The position of equilibrium remains unchanged, with constant $\text{mol}$.
- D. The value of $K_c$ increases, favouring $\text{HI}$ in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$.

**Answer: C.**
1. Determine the number of moles of gas on the reactant (left) side: $1 \text{ mol H}_2 + 1 \text{ mol I}_2 = 2 \text{ mol}$ of gas.
2. Determine the number of moles of gas on the product (right) side: $2 \text{ mol HI}$ of gas.
3. Compare the moles: Since the change in moles $\Delta n = 2 \text{ mol} - 2 \text{ mol} = 0 \text{ mol}$, the pressure on both sides is perfectly balanced. Pressure changes have no effect on this system.
4. Mistake behind option A: A common mistake is blindly assuming an increase in pressure always drives the reaction forward to the products. It only does this if the products have fewer moles of gas.

**6. In the Contact process, sulphur (IV) oxide is converted to sulphur (VI) oxide. Which catalyst is used to speed up this reversible reaction?**

- A. Finely divided iron ($\text{Fe}$) mass in $\text{g}$
- B. Vanadium (V) oxide ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$) mass in $\text{g}$
- C. Manganese (IV) oxide ($\text{MnO}_2$) mass in $\text{g}$
- D. Copper (II) oxide ($\text{CuO}$) mass in $\text{g}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Write the reversible reaction for the Contact process: $2\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3\text{(g)}$.
2. Note that the uncatalysed reaction rate is too slow at the compromise temperature of $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ (or $723 \text{ K}$).
3. Identify the industrial catalyst: Vanadium (V) oxide ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$) is used to lower the activation energy $E_a$ without being consumed.
4. Mistake behind option A: A common mistake is confusing the catalysts of different industrial processes. Finely divided iron ($\text{Fe}$) is the catalyst for the Haber process, not the Contact process.

**7. Consider the endothermic reaction: $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NO}_2\text{(g)}$ where $\Delta H = +58 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. If a sealed flask containing this mixture at equilibrium is placed in hot water at $353 \text{ K}$, what is observed?**

- A. The gas mixture turns pale or colourless because $[\text{N}_2\text{O}_4]$ increases.
- B. The gas mixture becomes darker brown because $[\text{NO}_2]$ increases.
- C. The volume of the container shrinks by $2.0 \text{ dm}^3$.
- D. The equilibrium constant $K_c$ drops to $0$.

**Answer: B.**
1. Check the enthalpy change: The forward reaction is endothermic, meaning it absorbs heat ($\Delta H = +58 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).
2. Apply Le Chatelier's Principle for temperature: Increasing the temperature to $353 \text{ K}$ causes the system to shift in the endothermic direction to absorb the excess heat.
3. Conclude the shift direction: The equilibrium shifts to the right, producing more $\text{NO}_2\text{(g)}$, which is a dark brown gas.
4. Mistake behind option A: A common mistake is confusing the signs of $\Delta H$. If one mistakenly thought a positive $\Delta H$ meant exothermic, they would predict a shift to the left (colourless $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$) upon heating.

**8. The Haber process for ammonia production operates at a compromise temperature of about $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ($723 \text{ K}$). Why is room temperature ($298 \text{ K}$) not used, even though it gives a higher yield?**

- A. The equilibrium constant $K_c$ is zero at $298 \text{ K}$.
- B. The reaction is endothermic, so $298 \text{ K}$ gives no yield.
- C. The rate of reaction at $298 \text{ K}$ is uneconomically slow.
- D. The catalyst requires exactly $298 \text{ K}$ to function.

**Answer: C.**
1. Consider the thermodynamics: The reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H = -92 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), so a low temperature like $298 \text{ K}$ theoretically shifts equilibrium to the right, giving a maximum yield of $\text{NH}_3$.
2. Consider the kinetics: At $298 \text{ K}$, the reactant molecules ($\text{N}_2$ and $\text{H}_2$) lack the necessary activation energy ($E_a$) to break their strong bonds.
3. Conclude the industrial reality: The rate of reaction $R$ at $298 \text{ K}$ is so close to $0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$ that producing ammonia would take years. $450 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ is a compromise to get a decent rate.
4. Mistake behind option B: A common mistake is thinking the forward reaction is endothermic just because the factory heats it up. They heat it for speed, not to increase the theoretical yield.

**9. In the equilibrium reaction $3\text{Fe(s)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4\text{(s)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$, what is the effect of continuously removing $\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$ from the system?**

- A. The concentration $[\text{H}_2\text{O}]$ increases.
- B. The equilibrium shifts to the right, increasing the yield of $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$.
- C. The pressure of the system drops permanently to $0 \text{ atm}$.
- D. The equilibrium shifts to the left, increasing the mass of $\text{Fe}$ in $\text{g}$.

**Answer: B.**
1. Identify the stress applied: A product, $\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$, is being removed, meaning its concentration $[\text{H}_2]$ is continually decreased.
2. Apply Le Chatelier's Principle: The system will shift to oppose the change by trying to produce more $\text{H}_2\text{(g)}$.
3. Determine the shift direction: To make more $\text{H}_2$, the equilibrium must shift to the right, consuming $\text{Fe}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and producing more $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$ and $\text{H}_2$.
4. Mistake behind option D: A mistake is misunderstanding Le Chatelier's principle and thinking the system shifts *away* from the side where something is removed, rather than *towards* it to replace the loss.

**10. If the temperature of a system at equilibrium is decreased, and this causes the equilibrium constant ($K_c$) to increase, what must be true about the forward reaction?**

- A. The forward reaction is endothermic, with $\Delta H > 0 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
- B. The forward reaction is exothermic, with $\Delta H < 0 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
- C. The forward reaction produces more moles of gas, $\Delta n > 0 \text{ mol}$.
- D. The forward reaction is irreversible, with $K_c = \infty$.

**Answer: B.**
1. Define the relationship for $K_c$: An increase in $K_c$ means the equilibrium has shifted to the right (more products, fewer reactants).
2. Note the stress applied: The temperature $T$ was decreased.
3. Apply Le Chatelier's principle: A temperature decrease causes the system to try and generate heat. It will shift in the heat-producing (exothermic) direction.
4. Conclude: Since the shift was to the right, the forward reaction must be the exothermic one ($\Delta H < 0 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).
5. Mistake behind option A: A common mistake is memorising that "temperature increases $K_c$" without linking it to the enthalpy sign. If the reaction were endothermic, a temperature decrease would actually lower $K_c$.
