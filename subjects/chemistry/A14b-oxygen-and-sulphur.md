## Oxygen and Sulphur

Oxygen and sulphur are both members of Group 16 in the Periodic Table (often called the chalcogens). While oxygen is a highly reactive gas essential for life and combustion, sulphur is a yellow solid largely known for its role in industrial chemistry, especially in the manufacture of tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$). 

### Laboratory Preparation of Oxygen
Oxygen can be prepared in the laboratory by the decomposition of oxygen-rich compounds. 

**Method 1: Thermal decomposition of potassium trioxochlorate(V)**
When potassium trioxochlorate(V) ($\text{KClO}_3$) is heated in the presence of a catalyst—manganese(IV) oxide ($\text{MnO}_2$)—it breaks down to yield oxygen gas and potassium chloride. The catalyst lowers the required temperature from $600\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ to about $200\,^{\circ}\text{C}$.
$$2\text{KClO}_3\text{(s)} \xrightarrow{\text{MnO}_2, \Delta} 2\text{KCl(s)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)}$$
The gas is collected over water because it is only slightly soluble in water.

**Method 2: Catalytic decomposition of hydrogen peroxide**
Hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) decomposes slowly on its own, but adding manganese(IV) oxide ($\text{MnO}_2$) at room temperature causes it to break down rapidly into water and oxygen. 
$$2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\text{(aq)} \xrightarrow{\text{MnO}_2} 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$$

### Commercial Production of Oxygen from Liquid Air
For industrial purposes, oxygen is produced cheaply by the **fractional distillation of liquid air**.

**Step-by-step process:**
1. **Purification:** Atmospheric air is filtered to remove dust, and passed through sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) solution to absorb carbon(IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$). 
2. **Cooling and Liquefaction:** The purified air is compressed and expanded repeatedly. This extreme cooling causes water vapour to freeze out as ice. The remaining air eventually liquefies at around $-200\,^{\circ}\text{C}$.
3. **Fractional Distillation:** The liquid air is fed into a fractionating column and allowed to warm up slightly. Nitrogen, which has a lower boiling point ($-196\,^{\circ}\text{C}$), boils off first. Oxygen, with a higher boiling point ($-183\,^{\circ}\text{C}$), remains behind as a liquid and is tapped off into steel cylinders.

### Properties and Uses of Oxygen
**Physical Properties:** It is a colourless, odourless, and tasteless gas, slightly heavier than air, and slightly soluble in water.
**Chemical Properties:** Oxygen is highly reactive and combines directly with most metals and non-metals to form oxides. It supports combustion but does not burn itself. A classic test for oxygen is that it **rekindles a glowing splint**.
**Uses:** Resuscitation in hospitals, mixed with ethyne (acetylene) for oxy-acetylene welding torches (producing a flame of about $3000\,^{\circ}\text{C}$), as an oxidant in rocket fuels, and in steel manufacturing to burn off impurities.

### Worked Example: Oxygen Volume Calculation
**Example 1:** Calculate the volume of oxygen gas produced at s.t.p. when $12.25\text{ g}$ of potassium trioxochlorate(V) is completely decomposed by heat. 
(K = 39.0, Cl = 35.5, O = 16.0; Molar volume of a gas at s.t.p. = $22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$)

1. **Write the balanced chemical equation:**
   $2\text{KClO}_3\text{(s)} \xrightarrow{\text{MnO}_2, \Delta} 2\text{KCl(s)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)}$
2. **Find the molar mass of $\text{KClO}_3$:**
   $\text{KClO}_3 = 39.0 + 35.5 + (3 \times 16.0) = 122.5\text{ g mol}^{-1}$
3. **Calculate the number of moles of $\text{KClO}_3$ used:**
   Moles = $12.25\text{ g} / 122.5\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 0.1\text{ mol}$
4. **Use the mole ratio from the equation to find moles of $\text{O}_2$:**
   The equation shows that 2 moles of $\text{KClO}_3$ produce 3 moles of $\text{O}_2$. 
   Therefore, $0.1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{KClO}_3$ produces $(3/2) \times 0.1\text{ mol} = 0.15\text{ mol}$ of $\text{O}_2$.
5. **Convert moles of $\text{O}_2$ to volume at s.t.p.:**
   Volume = $0.15\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1} = 3.36\text{ dm}^3$.

## Allotropes of Oxygen

Allotropy is the existence of an element in two or more different forms in the same physical state. Oxygen has two gaseous allotropes:

| Feature | Diatomic Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) | Trioxygen (Ozone, $\text{O}_3$) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Formula** | $\text{O}_2$ (two oxygen atoms) | $\text{O}_3$ (three oxygen atoms) |
| **Appearance and Smell** | Colourless, odourless | Pale blue gas with a sharp, pungent (garlic-like) smell |
| **Chemical Activity** | Reactive, supports combustion | Extremely reactive, far more powerful oxidizing agent than $\text{O}_2$ |
| **Uses** | Respiration, welding, combustion | Sterilizing water, bleaching delicate fabrics, air purification |

Ozone is prepared in the laboratory by passing a silent electric discharge through pure, dry oxygen. 
$3\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{O}_3\text{(g)}$

---

## Significance of Ozone to Our Environment

Ozone plays two completely different roles depending on where it is found in the atmosphere:

1. **In the Stratosphere (Good Ozone):** About 15 to 30 kilometres above the Earth's surface, ozone forms a protective layer. Its vital significance is that it **absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation** from the sun. If this UV radiation reached the Earth fully, it would cause widespread skin cancer, eye cataracts, and genetic mutations.
2. **In the Troposphere (Bad Ozone):** Near ground level, ozone is formed from exhaust fumes and pollutants reacting in sunlight (photochemical smog). Here, it is a highly toxic pollutant that causes asthma and respiratory damage.

## Classification of the Oxides of Oxygen

When oxygen combines with other elements, it forms oxides. JAMB expects you to classify them into four distinct types:

| Type of Oxide | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Basic Oxides** | Oxides of metals. They react with acids to form salt and water only. Soluble basic oxides dissolve in water to form alkalis. | $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$, $\text{K}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CaO}$, $\text{MgO}$, $\text{CuO}$ |
| **Acidic Oxides** | Oxides of non-metals. They dissolve in water to form acids, and react with alkalis to form salts. Often called acid anhydrides. | $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{SO}_3$, $\text{NO}_2$, $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$ |
| **Amphoteric Oxides** | Oxides of certain metals that behave as **both** acids and bases. They react with both strong acids and strong alkalis to form salts. (Mnemonic: **ZAP** - **Z**inc, **A**luminium, **P**lumbum/Lead). | $\text{ZnO}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $\text{PbO}$, $\text{SnO}$ |
| **Neutral Oxides** | Non-metal oxides that show neither acidic nor basic properties. They do not react with acids or alkalis and are neutral to litmus paper. | Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), Carbon(II) oxide ($\text{CO}$), Dinitrogen(I) oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), Nitrogen(II) oxide ($\text{NO}$) |

## Various Uses of Water

Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is a product of the combustion of hydrogen. By volume, exactly 2 volumes of hydrogen react with 1 volume of oxygen to form 2 volumes of steam, confirming that water's composition by volume is a 2:1 ratio of hydrogen to oxygen:
$2\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$

Water is arguably the most important compound on Earth. Its uses include:
1. **Domestic Use:** Drinking, cooking, washing, and bathing.
2. **Industrial Use:** Widely used as a universal solvent, as a cooling agent in thermal and nuclear power stations, and in the production of chemicals and textiles.
3. **Agricultural Use:** Irrigation of crops and sustenance of livestock.
4. **Biological Use:** It makes up over 70% of the human body, acting as a medium for biochemical reactions, transporting nutrients, and regulating body temperature.

---

## Effects of Dissolved Atmospheric Gases in Water

Natural water dissolves gases from the atmosphere. These dissolved gases have vital biological and chemical effects:

- **Dissolved Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$):** Essential for the survival of aquatic animals (like fish) and plants, which rely on it for respiration. It helps purify water by allowing aerobic bacteria to break down organic waste.
- **Dissolved Carbon(IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$):** Essential for aquatic plants to carry out photosynthesis. Dissolved $\text{CO}_2$ makes rainwater slightly acidic by forming weak trioxocarbonate(IV) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$). This mildly acidic water dissolves limestone rocks over time, contributing to water hardness.
- **Dissolved Nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$):** Mostly inert, but certain aquatic bacteria fix this dissolved nitrogen into nitrates, enriching the aquatic ecosystem.

## Properties of Hard and Soft Water

Soft water readily forms a lather with soap, such as rainwater or distilled water. Hard water does not readily form a lather but instead forms a white, sticky, insoluble solid called **scum**.

| Feature | Hard Water | Soft Water |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reaction with soap** | Wastes soap, forms scum instead of lather initially | Forms a rich lather easily, saves soap |
| **Dissolved ions** | High concentrations of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ | Little or no $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ |
| **Taste** | Often tastes better due to dissolved minerals | Often tastes flat or bland |
| **Health effects** | Provides dietary calcium (good for bones and teeth) | May lack beneficial minerals; if very acidic, can dissolve lead from old pipes (plumbosolvency) |
| **Industrial impact** | Forms "fur" or boiler scale ($\text{CaCO}_3$) inside kettles and industrial boilers, which can block pipes and cause explosions | Safe for boilers; does not leave mineral deposits |

## Causes of Water Hardness

Hardness is caused by the presence of dissolved **calcium ions ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$)** and **magnesium ions ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$)** in the water. 

Soap is typically sodium stearate ($\text{NaSt}$). When soap is added to hard water, the calcium and magnesium ions react with the stearate to precipitate insoluble calcium or magnesium stearate (the scum).
$2\text{NaSt(aq)} + \text{Ca}^{2+}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaSt}_2\text{(s)} + 2\text{Na}^+\text{(aq)}$
Only after all the $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions have been removed by forming scum will the water begin to form a lather.

---

## Methods of Removal of Hardness

Water hardness is divided into **Temporary hardness** and **Permanent hardness**. 

### 1. Temporary Hardness
Caused by dissolved **calcium hydrogen trioxocarbonate(IV)**, $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$, and magnesium hydrogen trioxocarbonate(IV). 

**Methods to remove temporary hardness:**
- **Boiling:** Heating the water decomposes the soluble hydrogen trioxocarbonate(IV) into insoluble normal trioxocarbonate(IV), which precipitates out.
  $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\text{(aq)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$
- **Addition of Slaked Lime ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$):** A carefully calculated amount of slaked lime is added. It reacts with the soluble bicarbonate to form insoluble calcium carbonate. 
  $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\text{(aq)} + \text{Ca(OH)}_2\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$

### 2. Permanent Hardness
Caused by dissolved **calcium or magnesium tetraoxosulphate(VI)** ($\text{CaSO}_4$, $\text{MgSO}_4$) and **chlorides** ($\text{CaCl}_2$, $\text{MgCl}_2$). These do not decompose on heating, so **boiling cannot remove permanent hardness**.

**Methods to remove BOTH permanent and temporary hardness:**
- **Addition of Washing Soda ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$):** Sodium trioxocarbonate(IV) reacts to precipitate the calcium or magnesium ions as insoluble carbonates.
  $\text{CaSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)}$
- **Ion-Exchange Resin (Permutit process):** Hard water is passed through a column filled with a complex sodium aluminium silicate (zeolite). The resin exchanges harmless sodium ($\text{Na}^+$) ions for the troublesome $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions. 
  $\text{Ca}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{Na}_2\text{-Zeolite(s)} \rightarrow \text{Ca-Zeolite(s)} + 2\text{Na}^+\text{(aq)}$
- **Distillation:** Evaporating the water and condensing the steam removes all dissolved solids, leaving pure, soft water.

## Processes in the Treatment of Water for Town Supply

Raw water must be purified before it is pumped to homes. The standard step-by-step process is:

1. **Aeration:** Raw water is sprayed into the air. This adds oxygen, removes foul-smelling gases, and oxidizes soluble iron(II) compounds into insoluble iron(III) compounds.
2. **Coagulation and Flocculation:** A chemical coagulant, usually **potassium alum** (potassium aluminium tetraoxosulphate(VI)), is added. The alum causes fine, suspended particles of clay to stick together into larger, heavier clumps called *flocs*.
3. **Sedimentation:** The water is allowed to sit quietly in a large tank. The heavy flocs sink to the bottom.
4. **Filtration:** The clear water from the top is passed through thick filter beds of sand and gravel to trap any remaining fine matter.
5. **Disinfection (Chlorination):** Chlorine gas or bleaching powder is added to kill harmful bacteria and microorganisms.
6. **pH Adjustment:** Adding alum makes the water slightly acidic. To prevent corrosion of metal pipes, slaked lime ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) or soda ash is added to neutralize the acid.

## Distinguishing Water of Crystallization, Efflorescence, Deliquescence and Hygroscopy

Many salts exist as crystals that have a fixed number of water molecules chemically combined with them. 
- **Water of crystallization** is the definite amount of water chemically bound in a crystalline compound. Compounds containing this water are called **hydrated** salts. If the water is driven off by heat, the compound becomes **anhydrous**.

Certain compounds behave uniquely when exposed to the moisture in the air:
1. **Efflorescence:** The phenomenon where a hydrated salt **loses part or all of its water of crystallization** to the atmosphere when exposed to dry air. The crystal crumbles into an anhydrous powder.
2. **Deliquescence:** The phenomenon where a substance **absorbs so much moisture** from the atmosphere that it eventually **dissolves in it to form a solution**.
3. **Hygroscopy:** The phenomenon where a substance **absorbs moisture** from the atmosphere but **does NOT form a solution** (it merely becomes damp or sticky, or if it is already a liquid, it becomes diluted). These substances make excellent drying agents (desiccants).

| **Deliquescent substances** | Calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$), Magnesium chloride ($\text{MgCl}_2$), Iron(III) chloride ($\text{FeCl}_3$), solid Sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$), solid Potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$) |

### Worked Example: Percentage of Water of Crystallization
**Example 2:** Calculate the percentage by mass of water of crystallization in copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) pentahydrate, $\text{CuSO}_4\cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
(Cu = 64.0, S = 32.0, O = 16.0, H = 1.0)

1. **Find the molar mass of water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$):**
   $\text{H}_2\text{O} = (2 \times 1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
   Mass of 5 moles of water = $5 \times 18.0\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 90.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. **Find the total molar mass of the compound ($\text{CuSO}_4\cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$):**
   $\text{CuSO}_4 = 64.0 + 32.0 + (4 \times 16.0) = 160.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
   Total molar mass = $160.0\text{ g mol}^{-1} + 90.0\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 250.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. **Calculate the percentage of water:**
   $\%\text{ Water} = (90.0\text{ g mol}^{-1} / 250.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}) \times 100\% = 36.0\%$.

## Allotropes of Sulphur and Their Uses

Sulphur exhibits allotropy, existing in both crystalline and non-crystalline forms:
1. **Rhombic (Alpha) Sulphur:** The most stable allotrope at room temperature (below $96\,^{\circ}\text{C}$). It consists of bright yellow, octahedral (eight-sided) crystals. 
2. **Monoclinic (Beta) Sulphur:** Stable between $96\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ and $119\,^{\circ}\text{C}$. It forms pale yellow, needle-like crystals. If kept at room temperature, it slowly turns back into rhombic sulphur.
3. **Amorphous / Plastic Sulphur:** A non-crystalline form prepared by pouring boiling, molten sulphur into cold water. It forms a dark, rubbery ribbon, but hardens and reverts to rhombic sulphur over time.

**Uses of Sulphur:**
- The vast majority of sulphur is used to manufacture **tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$)**.
- Used in the **vulcanization of rubber** (adding sulphur makes rubber harder, stronger, and more elastic, essential for car tyres).
- Used in manufacturing matches, gunpowder, and agricultural fungicides.

## Preparation, Properties and Uses of Sulphur(IV) Oxide and Hydrogen Sulphide

### Sulphur(IV) Oxide ($\text{SO}_2$)

**Laboratory Preparation Reagents:** 
Prepared by heating a solid trioxosulphate(IV) salt with a dilute acid, or by heating copper turnings with concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
$\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$

**Properties:**
- It is a colourless, poisonous gas with a choking smell of burning sulphur.
- It is an **acidic oxide**: it dissolves in water to form the weak acid, trioxosulphate(IV) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$).
- **Reaction with alkalis:** It neutralizes alkalis. With sodium hydroxide: 
  $\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{NaOH(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$
- **Reducing agent:** It readily reduces other substances. It turns purple $\text{KMnO}_4$ colourless, and turns orange $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ green.
- **Bleaching agent:** It bleaches wet materials by **reduction** (removing oxygen from the dye). Because oxygen from the air slowly re-oxidizes the dye over time, **bleaching by $\text{SO}_2$ is temporary**. (Compare this with chlorine, which bleaches permanently by oxidation).

**Uses of $\text{SO}_2$:**
- Chief intermediate in the manufacture of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
- Bleaching delicate materials (silk, wool, wood pulp) that chlorine would damage.
- Used as a preservative in jams and fruit juices because it stops the growth of bacteria.

### Hydrogen Sulphide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$)

**Laboratory Preparation Reagents:**
Prepared without heat using a Kipp's apparatus, reacting solid iron(II) sulphide ($\text{FeS}$) with dilute hydrochloric acid.
$\text{FeS(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{FeCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{S(g)}$

**Properties:**
- A colourless gas with a disgusting smell of rotten eggs.
- **Weak acid:** Dissolves slightly in water to form a weak, dibasic acid.
- **Strong reducing agent:** It is easily oxidized to yellow sulphur by oxidizing agents.
- **Precipitating agent:** Bubbled through solutions of metallic salts, it precipitates insoluble metal sulphides (e.g., Copper(II) sulphide and Lead(II) sulphide are black).

### Worked Example: Mass of Sulphur Precipitated
**Example 3:** Calculate the mass of sulphur precipitated when $3.4\text{ g}$ of hydrogen sulphide is completely oxidized by chlorine gas. (H = 1.0, S = 32.0)

1. **Write the balanced chemical equation:**
   $\text{H}_2\text{S(g)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{HCl(g)} + \text{S(s)}$
2. **Find the molar mass of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$:**
   $\text{H}_2\text{S} = (2 \times 1.0) + 32.0 = 34.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. **Calculate moles of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ reacting:**
   Moles = $3.4\text{ g} / 34.0\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 0.1\text{ mol}$.
4. **Use the mole ratio to find moles of S produced:**
   The equation shows 1 mole of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ produces 1 mole of S.
   So, $0.1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ produces $0.1\text{ mol}$ of S.
5. **Convert moles of S to mass:**
   Mass = $0.1\text{ mol} \times 32.0\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 3.2\text{ g}$.

## Preparations of Tetraoxosulphate(VI) and Trioxosulphate(IV) Acids

### Trioxosulphate(IV) Acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$)
**Preparation:** Formed simply by dissolving $\text{SO}_2$ gas in water.
$\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3\text{(aq)}$
**Properties:** It is a weak, dibasic acid known only in solution. When a stronger acid (like dilute $\text{HCl}$) is added to its salts, it displaces the $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$, which immediately breaks down to release $\text{SO}_2$ gas and water.

### Tetraoxosulphate(VI) Acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$)
Known as the "king of chemicals," its production level is an indicator of a country's industrial strength.

**Commercial Preparation: The Contact Process**
1. **Production of $\text{SO}_2$:** Sulphur is burned in air to yield $\text{SO}_2$.
   $\text{S(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$
2. **Catalytic oxidation of $\text{SO}_2$ to $\text{SO}_3$ (The Contact step):** The gases are purified and passed over a **Vanadium(V) oxide ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$)** catalyst at a temperature of about $450\,^{\circ}\text{C}$. 
   $2\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3\text{(g)}$ (Exothermic)
3. **Absorption of $\text{SO}_3$:** $\text{SO}_3$ is **not** dissolved directly in water because it reacts violently, producing a dangerous fog of acid mist. Instead, it is dissolved in existing concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ to form a thick liquid called **oleum** ($\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7$).
   $\text{SO}_3\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(l)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7\text{(l)}$
4. **Dilution:** Oleum is carefully diluted with calculated amounts of water to produce 98% concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
   $\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7\text{(l)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(l)}$

Block 4: "Absorption Tower" where conc. H2SO4 falls from the top to absorb SO3, forming Oleum. Block 5: "Dilution Tank" where water is added to Oleum to output Conc. H2SO4. **Properties of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$:**
- **As a dilute acid:** It is a strong, dibasic acid. It reacts with active metals (like Zn or Mg) to release hydrogen gas, and neutralizes bases.
- **As a strong Dehydrating Agent (Concentrated):** It has a massive affinity for water. It aggressively removes the elements of water (hydrogen and oxygen in a 2:1 ratio) from compounds. For example, it turns white sugar ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$) into a swollen black mass of carbon.
  $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}\text{(s)} \xrightarrow{\text{Conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4} 12\text{C(s)} + 11\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$
- **As an Oxidizing Agent (Concentrated and hot):** It oxidizes non-metals and metals (like copper), being reduced to $\text{SO}_2$ in the process.
  $\text{Cu(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(conc)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$

**Uses of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$:** Manufacturing fertilizers (like ammonium sulphate), paints, detergents, and as the electrolyte in car batteries (lead-acid accumulators).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Allotropy | The existence of an element in two or more forms in the same physical state. |
| Amphoteric oxide | An oxide that can act as both an acid and a base (e.g., $\text{ZnO}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$). |
| Scum | An insoluble precipitate formed when soap reacts with calcium or magnesium ions in hard water. |
| Coagulation | The clumping together of fine particles into larger flocs, typically aided by adding alum during water treatment. |
| Efflorescence | The loss of part or all water of crystallization from a hydrated salt to the atmosphere. |
| Deliquescence | The absorption of moisture from the air by a solid until it dissolves to form a solution. |
| Hygroscopy | The absorption of moisture from the air without forming a liquid solution. |
| Oleum | Fuming tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7$), formed by dissolving $\text{SO}_3$ in concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Catalysts Mix-ups:** A frequent JAMB trap. Remember: Contact process = **$\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$**; Haber process = **Finely divided Iron**; Lab prep of Oxygen = **$\text{MnO}_2$**. Do not confuse them!
- **Bleaching mechanism:** $\text{SO}_2$ bleaches by **reduction** (temporary), while chlorine bleaches by **oxidation** (permanent).
- **Distinguishing Sulphates and Sulphites:** Both give a white precipitate with $\text{BaCl}_2$. However, the sulphate precipitate ($\text{BaSO}_4$) is **insoluble** in dilute $\text{HCl}$, whereas the sulphite precipitate ($\text{BaSO}_3$) **dissolves** in dilute $\text{HCl}$.
- **Amphoteric oxides shortcut:** Remember **ZAP** (Zinc, Aluminium, Plumbum/Lead). These are the metals whose oxides and hydroxides are amphoteric.
- **Hygroscopic vs Deliquescent:** Remember that a deliquescent substance turns into a liquid (solution), while a hygroscopic one just gets damp or is already a liquid absorbing more water (like conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
- **Unit slips in calculations:** Always check if the question asks for mass (grams) or volume ($\text{dm}^3$). Remember the molar volume at s.t.p. is $22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$.
- **Scum formation:** Only hard water forms scum. The chemical cause is always $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions.

## Quick summary

1. Oxygen is prepared in the lab by decomposing $\text{KClO}_3$ or $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ with an $\text{MnO}_2$ catalyst, and industrially by the fractional distillation of liquid air.
2. Ozone ($\text{O}_3$) is an allotrope of oxygen that forms a protective layer in the stratosphere, shielding the Earth from deadly UV radiation.
3. Oxides are classified into four types: basic (metal oxides), acidic (non-metal oxides), amphoteric ($\text{ZnO}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $\text{PbO}$), and neutral ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NO}$).
4. Hard water contains dissolved $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions, causing soap to waste by forming scum. Temporary hardness is removed by boiling or slaked lime; permanent hardness requires washing soda or ion-exchange resins.
5. Town water treatment follows a specific sequence: Aeration $\rightarrow$ Coagulation (Alum) $\rightarrow$ Sedimentation $\rightarrow$ Filtration $\rightarrow$ Chlorination $\rightarrow$ pH adjustment.
6. Efflorescent substances lose water to the air; deliquescent substances absorb water until they dissolve; hygroscopic substances absorb water but do not form a solution.
7. Sulphur exists as rhombic, monoclinic, and amorphous (plastic) allotropes, and is primarily used to manufacture $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
8. The Contact Process produces $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ using a $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ catalyst. Concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is a powerful dehydrating and oxidizing agent.

## Practice questions

**1. What volume of oxygen gas is produced at s.t.p. when $12.25 \text{ g}$ of potassium trioxochlorate(V) is completely decomposed by heat?** (K = 39.0, Cl = 35.5, O = 16.0, Molar volume at s.t.p. = $22.4 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ mol}^{-1}$)
- A. $1.12 \text{ dm}^3$
- B. $2.24 \text{ dm}^3$
- C. $3.36 \text{ dm}^3$
- D. $6.72 \text{ dm}^3$

**Answer: C.** 1. Write the balanced equation: $2\text{KClO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow 2\text{KCl(s)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)}$. 2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{KClO}_3$: $39.0 + 35.5 + (3 \times 16.0) = 122.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$. 3. Find the moles of $\text{KClO}_3$: $12.25 \text{ g} / 122.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 0.1 \text{ mol}$. 4. Use the mole ratio ($2:3$) to find the moles of $\text{O}_2$: $0.1 \text{ mol} \times (3/2) = 0.15 \text{ mol}$. 5. Convert to volume at s.t.p.: $0.15 \text{ mol} \times 22.4 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ mol}^{-1} = 3.36 \text{ dm}^3$. The most tempting wrong option is B, which comes from the mistake of assuming a $1:1$ mole ratio, leading to $0.1 \text{ mol} \times 22.4 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ mol}^{-1} = 2.24 \text{ dm}^3$.

**2. What is the significance of the ozone layer in the upper atmosphere (stratosphere)?**
- A. It traps heat to keep the Earth warm.
- B. It absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the sun.
- C. It provides extra oxygen for high-altitude airplanes.
- D. It prevents the escape of water vapour into space.

**Answer: B.** 1. Recall that ozone ($\text{O}_3$) is an allotrope of oxygen found in the stratosphere. 2. Note that its main function is to absorb high-energy ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun. 3. Conclude that this prevents harmful UV rays from reaching the Earth's surface. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of confusing ozone's protective role with the heat-trapping role of greenhouse gases like $\text{CO}_2$.

**3. Permanent hardness in water can be removed by adding**
- A. slaked lime ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$)
- B. washing soda ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$)
- C. potassium alum ($\text{KAl(SO}_4)_2 \cdot 12\text{H}_2\text{O}$)
- D. dilute hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$)

**Answer: B.** 1. Note that permanent hardness is caused by dissolved calcium or magnesium tetraoxosulphate(VI) or chlorides. 2. Recognize that boiling or adding slaked lime ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) only removes temporary hardness. 3. Adding washing soda ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) provides $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ ions. 4. These react with $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ to form insoluble carbonates, removing hardness. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of confusing the treatment for permanent hardness with that for temporary hardness.

**4. In the treatment of water for town supply, the primary purpose of adding potassium alum is to**
- A. kill harmful bacteria and microorganisms
- B. neutralize the acidic pH of the water
- C. cause fine suspended particles to clump together
- D. remove foul odours and volatile gases

**Answer: C.** 1. Trace the steps of water treatment: aeration, coagulation, sedimentation, filtration, chlorination. 2. Identify the role of potassium alum ($\text{KAl(SO}_4)_2 \cdot 12\text{H}_2\text{O}$): it acts as a coagulant. 3. It makes fine, lightweight particles of clay stick together (flocculate) into heavier clumps that settle out. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of confusing the coagulation step with the disinfection step, which uses chlorine to kill bacteria.

**5. Which of the following substances undergoes efflorescence when exposed to the atmosphere?**
- A. Calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$)
- B. Sodium trioxocarbonate(IV) decahydrate ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$)
- C. Solid sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$)
- D. Concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$)

**Answer: B.** 1. Define efflorescence as the loss of part or all water of crystallization to the atmosphere. 2. Identify that washing soda ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is a classic efflorescent salt that crumbles to a white powder. 3. Note that $\text{CaCl}_2$ and $\text{NaOH}$ are deliquescent, absorbing water to form a solution. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of confusing efflorescence (losing water) with deliquescence (absorbing water).

**6. Which of the following pairs of oxides are amphoteric?**
- A. $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{SO}_2$
- B. $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CaO}$
- C. $\text{ZnO}$ and $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$
- D. $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CO}$

**Answer: C.** 1. Define an amphoteric oxide as one that reacts with both acids and bases to form a salt and water. 2. Recall the mnemonic "ZAP" (Zinc, Aluminium, Plumbum/Lead) for metals forming amphoteric oxides. 3. Select $\text{ZnO}$ and $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ as the correct pair. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of confusing amphoteric oxides with acidic oxides formed by non-metals like carbon and sulphur (e.g., $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{SO}_2$).

**7. Calculate the percentage by mass of water of crystallization in copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) pentahydrate, $\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$.** (Cu = 64.0, S = 32.0, O = 16.0, H = 1.0)
- A. $7.2\%$
- B. $10.1\%$
- C. $36.0\%$
- D. $64.0\%$

**Answer: C.** 1. Find the molar mass of one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecule: $(2 \times 1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$. 2. Calculate the mass of water in the compound: $5 \times 18.0 = 90.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$. 3. Find the molar mass of $\text{CuSO}_4$: $64.0 + 32.0 + (4 \times 16.0) = 160.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$. 4. Calculate the total molar mass: $160.0 + 90.0 = 250.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$. 5. Calculate the percentage: $(90.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1} / 250.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) \times 100\% = 36.0\%$. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of using the mass of only one water molecule ($18.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$) instead of five, leading to $(18.0 / 250.0) \times 100\% = 7.2\%$.

**8. The bleaching action of sulphur(IV) oxide ($\text{SO}_2$) differs from that of chlorine because it**
- A. bleaches by oxidation and is permanent
- B. bleaches by oxidation and is temporary
- C. bleaches by reduction and is permanent
- D. bleaches by reduction and is temporary

**Answer: D.** 1. Recall that $\text{SO}_2$ bleaches wet materials by removing oxygen from the dye. 2. Recognize this removal of oxygen as reduction. 3. Note that over time, oxygen from the air slowly re-oxidizes the dye, making the bleaching temporary. 4. Contrast this with chlorine, which bleaches permanently by oxidation. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of confusing the bleaching mechanism of $\text{SO}_2$ with the permanent oxidative bleaching of chlorine.

**9. In the Contact process for the manufacture of tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid, the catalyst used is**
- A. finely divided iron
- B. manganese(IV) oxide ($\text{MnO}_2$)
- C. vanadium(V) oxide ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$)
- D. platinum-rhodium gauze

**Answer: C.** 1. Identify the key step in the Contact process: the catalytic conversion of $\text{SO}_2$ to $\text{SO}_3$. 2. Recall the specific catalyst required for this exothermic reaction at $450\,^{\circ}\text{C}$. 3. Select vanadium(V) oxide ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$). The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of confusing the Contact process catalyst with finely divided iron, which is the catalyst used in the Haber process.

**10. What mass of sulphur is precipitated when $3.4 \text{ g}$ of hydrogen sulphide is completely oxidized by chlorine gas?** (H = 1.0, S = 32.0)
- A. $1.6 \text{ g}$
- B. $3.2 \text{ g}$
- C. $6.4 \text{ g}$
- D. $9.6 \text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{H}_2\text{S(g)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{HCl(g)} + \text{S(s)}$. 2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$: $(2 \times 1.0) + 32.0 = 34.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$. 3. Calculate moles of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ used: $3.4 \text{ g} / 34.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 0.1 \text{ mol}$. 4. Use the mole ratio ($1:1$) to find moles of S: $0.1 \text{ mol}$. 5. Convert moles to mass: $0.1 \text{ mol} \times 32.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 3.2 \text{ g}$. The most tempting wrong option is C, which comes from the mistake of doubling the mole ratio for sulphur, leading to $0.2 \text{ mol} \times 32.0 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 6.4 \text{ g}$.
