## Nitrogen

Nitrogen is a non-metallic element in Group 15 of the periodic table. It exists naturally as a diatomic gas, $\text{N}_2$, which makes up about $78\%$ of the Earth's atmosphere by volume. It is a very unreactive gas because of the strong triple bond ($\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$) between its atoms, which requires a large amount of energy to break. 

### Nitrogen and its Production

**Laboratory preparation:**
Nitrogen is prepared in the laboratory by heating a solution of an ammonium salt with a trioxonitrate (III) salt. The most common reagents are **ammonium chloride** ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$) and **sodium trioxonitrate (III)** (also known as sodium nitrite, $\text{NaNO}_2$).
Heating ammonium nitrite directly is dangerous because it can explode. Therefore, it is produced in place (in situ) by heating the mixture of the two stable salts.
1. The salts react to form ammonium nitrite:
   $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl(aq)} + \text{NaNO}_2\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{NO}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{NaCl(aq)}$
2. The ammonium nitrite decomposes smoothly on heating to yield nitrogen gas:
   $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_2\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$

The gas is collected over water because it is only slightly soluble in water.

A delivery tube leads from the flask to a pneumatic trough, where nitrogen gas bubbles up into an inverted gas jar filled with water. Labels to add: "mixture of NH₄Cl and NaNO₂", "heat", "delivery tube", "water", "nitrogen gas". Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

**Industrial production from liquid air:**
On a large scale, nitrogen is obtained by the **fractional distillation of liquid air**. 
1. Air is passed through concentrated sodium hydroxide to remove carbon (IV) oxide.
2. The air is then dried and repeatedly compressed and cooled (expanded) until it liquefies.
3. The liquid air is fractionally distilled. Nitrogen has a lower boiling point ($-196\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) than oxygen ($-183\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), so it boils off first as a gas, leaving liquid oxygen behind.

### Preparation of Ammonia

**Laboratory preparation:**
Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) is prepared by heating any ammonium salt with a strong alkali. In the lab, a mixture of solid **ammonium chloride** and solid **calcium hydroxide** (slaked lime) is normally used.
$$2\text{NH}_4\text{Cl(s)} + \text{Ca(OH)}_2\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2\text{(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)}$$

- **Drying:** Ammonia is an alkaline gas. It cannot be dried using concentrated tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) or fused calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$) because it reacts with them. It is instead dried by passing it through a tower packed with **quicklime (calcium oxide, $\text{CaO}$)**.
- **Collection:** It is collected by **upward delivery (downward displacement of air)** because it is lighter than air and highly soluble in water.

**Industrial preparation (Haber Process):**
The Haber process is the large-scale industrial manufacture of ammonia directly from nitrogen and hydrogen gases. 
$$\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} + \text{heat}$$
This reaction is reversible and exothermic. To get a good yield of ammonia quickly, specific conditions are chosen:
1. **Catalyst:** **Finely divided iron** is used to speed up the reaction. (Sometimes molybdenum or aluminium oxide is added as a promoter).
2. **Temperature:** A moderate temperature of about **$450\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$** is used. If the temperature is too high, the yield of ammonia decreases; if it is too low, the reaction is too slow.
3. **Pressure:** A high pressure of about **$200\text{ atm}$** is used to push the equilibrium to the right (favouring ammonia production).

**Worked Example 1: Volume of ammonia in the Haber Process**
What volume of ammonia is produced when $50\text{ cm}^3$ of nitrogen reacts completely with hydrogen gas at s.t.p.? (Assume $100\%$ yield).
1. Write the balanced chemical equation for the reaction:
   $\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)}$
2. Determine the volume ratio from the stoichiometry of the gases:
   $1\text{ volume}$ of $\text{N}_2$ produces $2\text{ volumes}$ of $\text{NH}_3$.
3. Use the given volume to find the answer:
   If $1\text{ volume} = 50\text{ cm}^3$, then $2\text{ volumes} = 2 \times 50\text{ cm}^3 = 100\text{ cm}^3$.
4. State the final answer with units: The volume of ammonia produced is $100\text{ cm}^3$.

### Properties and Uses of Ammonia

**Physical properties:**
- Ammonia is a colourless gas with a sharp, choking smell.
- It is highly soluble in water (demonstrated by the **fountain experiment**), dissolving to form a weakly alkaline solution called aqueous ammonia.
- It is much lighter (less dense) than air.

**Chemical properties:**
- **As a base:** It is the only common alkaline gas. It turns damp red litmus paper blue. It reacts with acids to form ammonium salts:
  $\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} + \text{HCl(g)} \rightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{Cl(s)}$
- **As a reducing agent:** It reduces hot copper(II) oxide to copper metal, while it is oxidized to nitrogen gas:
  $3\text{CuO(s)} + 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} \rightarrow 3\text{Cu(s)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{N}_2\text{(g)}$
- **Catalytic oxidation:** Ammonia burns in oxygen in the presence of a platinum-rhodium catalyst to form nitrogen(II) oxide ($\text{NO}$) and water. This is the first step in making nitric acid (the Ostwald process).

**Uses:**
- The primary use of ammonia is in manufacturing **fertilizers** such as urea, ammonium trioxonitrate(V) ($\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$), and ammonium tetraoxosulphate(VI) ($\text{(NH}_4\text{)}_2\text{SO}_4$), which are vital for Nigerian agriculture.
- Used to manufacture **nitric acid**.
- Liquid ammonia is used as a refrigerant in heavy industries.
- Used in household cleaning agents.

### Test for Ammonia and Ammonium Ions

- **Test for Ammonia gas:** If a glass rod dipped in concentrated hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$) is brought near the gas, **dense white fumes** of ammonium chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$) are formed. It also turns damp red litmus paper blue.
- **Test for Ammonium ion ($\text{NH}_4^+$):** Add a strong alkali like sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) solution to the unknown substance and warm it gently. If the substance contains $\text{NH}_4^+$, **ammonia gas will be evolved**. You can confirm the gas using the dense white fumes test with $\text{HCl}$ or with damp red litmus paper.

### Reagents for Preparation, Properties and Uses of Trioxonitrate (V) Acid

Trioxonitrate(V) acid, commonly known as **nitric acid** ($\text{HNO}_3$), is a strong mineral acid.

**Laboratory preparation:**
Reagents used are **potassium trioxonitrate(V)** ($\text{KNO}_3$) (or sodium trioxonitrate(V)) and **concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid** ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
The mixture is gently heated in an **all-glass retort** because the hot acid vapour is highly corrosive and would attack rubber or cork stoppers.
$$\text{KNO}_3\text{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(l)} \rightarrow \text{KHSO}_4\text{(s)} + \text{HNO}_3\text{(g)}$$
The $\text{HNO}_3$ vapour is condensed by running cold water over the receiver flask. (On an industrial scale, it is prepared by the catalytic oxidation of ammonia in the **Ostwald process**).

**Properties:**
- Pure $\text{HNO}_3$ is a colourless liquid, but the laboratory reagent often appears yellowish-brown because it decomposes slightly in sunlight to yield dissolved nitrogen(IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$).
- It is a **strong acid**, ionizing completely in water.
- It is a **powerful oxidizing agent**. It rarely yields hydrogen gas when reacting with metals. Instead, it is reduced to oxides of nitrogen ($\text{NO}$ or $\text{NO}_2$) depending on the acid's concentration.
  For example, with copper:
  $3\text{Cu(s)} + 8\text{HNO}_3\text{(aq, dil)} \rightarrow 3\text{Cu(NO}_3\text{)}_2\text{(aq)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + 2\text{NO(g)}$

**Action of heat on Trioxonitrate(V) salts:**
JAMB frequently asks how different nitrates decompose on heating:
- **Sodium and potassium:** Decompose to give the trioxonitrate(III) (nitrite) and oxygen gas. (e.g., $2\text{NaNO}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{NaNO}_2 + \text{O}_2$).
- **Heavy metals (Ca, Mg, Zn, Pb, Cu):** Decompose to give the metal oxide, nitrogen(IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$), and oxygen gas.
- **Silver and mercury:** Decompose completely to yield the metal, $\text{NO}_2$, and $\text{O}_2$.
- **Ammonium trioxonitrate(V):** Decomposes to yield dinitrogen(I) oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$) and water.

**Worked Example 2: Trioxonitrate(V) Decomposition**
Calculate the mass of oxygen produced when $20.2\text{ g}$ of potassium trioxonitrate(V) is heated strongly. (K = 39, N = 14, O = 16)
1. Write the balanced equation for the decomposition:
   $2\text{KNO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow 2\text{KNO}_2\text{(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{KNO}_3$:
   $39 + 14 + (16 \times 3) = 39 + 14 + 48 = 101\text{ g mol}^{-1}$
3. Find the number of moles of $\text{KNO}_3$ heated:
   $\text{Moles} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Molar mass}} = \frac{20.2\text{ g}}{101\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.2\text{ mol}$
4. Use the mole ratio from the equation:
   $2\text{ moles}$ of $\text{KNO}_3$ yield $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{O}_2$. Therefore, $0.2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{KNO}_3$ will yield $\frac{0.2}{2} = 0.1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{O}_2$.
5. Convert moles of $\text{O}_2$ to mass:
   $\text{Molar mass of O}_2 = 16 \times 2 = 32\text{ g mol}^{-1}$
   $\text{Mass} = 0.1\text{ mol} \times 32\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 3.2\text{ g}$
6. Answer: The mass of oxygen produced is $3.2\text{ g}$.

**Uses of Trioxonitrate(V) acid:**
- Manufacturing fertilizers.
- Manufacturing explosives (e.g., TNT, dynamite).
- Producing synthetic dyes, drugs, and plastics.

### Properties of N₂O, NO, and NO₂ Gases

Nitrogen forms several oxides. The three most common ones tested in JAMB are compared below:

| Feature | Dinitrogen (I) oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$) | Nitrogen (II) oxide ($\text{NO}$) | Nitrogen (IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Common name** | Laughing gas | Nitric oxide | Nitrogen dioxide |
| **Colour** | Colourless | Colourless | Reddish-brown |
| **Smell** | Faint sweet smell | Odourless | Pungent, choking smell |
| **Acid-base nature**| Neutral (does not affect litmus) | Neutral | Acidic (forms $\text{HNO}_2$ and $\text{HNO}_3$ in water) |
| **Key properties** | Supports combustion heavily (relights a glowing splint like oxygen). | Reacts instantly with oxygen in the air to form brown $\text{NO}_2$. | Dimerizes (joins together) at low temperatures to form colourless liquid $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$. Poisonous. |

### Specific Tests for N₂O, NO and NO₃⁻

**Test for $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ (Laughing gas):**
It easily relights a glowing splint (similar to oxygen). To distinguish it from oxygen, note that $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ has a sweet smell, whereas oxygen is odourless. Also, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ does not form brown fumes when mixed with nitrogen(II) oxide ($\text{NO}$), while oxygen does.

**Test for $\text{NO}$:**
Expose the colourless gas to air (oxygen). It will immediately turn into the **reddish-brown gas** nitrogen(IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$).

**Test for $\text{NO}_3^-$ (The Brown Ring Test):**
1. Add an equal volume of freshly prepared iron(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) ($\text{FeSO}_4$) solution to the unknown trioxonitrate(V) solution in a test tube.
2. Slant the test tube and carefully add a few drops of **concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$** down the side of the tube.
3. The heavy acid settles at the bottom. At the junction where the acid and the aqueous mixture meet, a **brown ring** forms if $\text{NO}_3^-$ is present. The brown ring is a complex compound called nitroso-iron(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI), with the formula **$\text{FeSO}_4\cdot\text{NO}$**.

### The Nitrogen Cycle and its Relevance

The nitrogen cycle is the continuous circulation of nitrogen between the atmosphere, the soil, and living organisms. It ensures that unreactive atmospheric nitrogen is converted to usable forms for plants, and eventually returned to the air.

1. **Nitrogen Fixation:** Converting inert atmospheric $\text{N}_2$ into usable nitrogen compounds in the soil.
   - **Biological:** Symbiotic bacteria like *Rhizobium* living in the root nodules of leguminous plants (like cowpea and groundnuts, widely farmed in northern Nigeria) fix nitrogen directly. Free-living soil bacteria like *Azotobacter* also do this.
   - **Physical:** Lightning energy causes atmospheric $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$ to combine into oxides, which wash into the soil as weak acids, forming nitrates.
   - **Artificial:** Haber process makes ammonia for fertilizers.
2. **Nitrification:** The conversion of ammonia and ammonium compounds in the soil into nitrites (by *Nitrosomonas* bacteria), and then into nitrates (by *Nitrobacter* bacteria). Plants absorb these nitrates.
3. **Assimilation:** Plants use nitrates to make plant proteins. Animals eat the plants to get protein.
4. **Ammonification (Decay):** When plants and animals die, fungi and bacteria decompose their proteins back into ammonia and ammonium compounds in the soil.
5. **Denitrification:** The process where specific bacteria (e.g., *Pseudomonas*) break down soil nitrates, returning nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$) back into the atmosphere.

**Relevance:** The cycle maintains the concentration of nitrogen in the atmosphere and preserves soil fertility, ensuring that life on Earth can continue to synthesize essential proteins and DNA.

## Carbon

Carbon is a non-metal in Group 14 of the periodic table. It is the basis of all organic chemistry and life, and occurs in nature both in the free state (as diamond, graphite, and coal) and chemically combined (as carbon(IV) oxide in the air, trioxocarbonates(IV) like limestone, and in petroleum).

### Allotropes of Carbon

Allotropy is the existence of an element in two or more physical forms in the same physical state. Carbon exists in several allotropes. The two main crystalline allotropes are **diamond** and **graphite**. Modern science also recognises fullerenes (e.g., buckminsterfullerene) as crystalline allotropes. Forms like coal, charcoal, and soot are considered **amorphous** (non-crystalline) allotropes, although microscopically they contain scattered graphite-like structures.

**Comparison of Diamond and Graphite:**

| Property | Diamond | Graphite |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Structure** | Giant 3D tetrahedral network. Each carbon is covalently bonded to four others. | Flat hexagonal layers held together by weak van der Waals forces. Each carbon is bonded to three others. |
| **Hardness** | Extremely hard (the hardest known natural substance). | Soft, slippery, and flakes easily. |
| **Electrical conductivity**| Non-conductor (no free/mobile electrons). | Good conductor (has one mobile/delocalized electron per carbon atom). |
| **Density** | Very dense ($3.5\text{ g cm}^{-3}$). | Less dense ($2.2\text{ g cm}^{-3}$). |
| **Appearance** | Transparent, brilliant, sparkles when cut. | Opaque, black, and shiny. |
| **Uses** | Jewellery, drilling rocks, cutting glass. | Electrodes in dry cells, lubricant for heavy machines, pencil leads. |

### Reagents for the Laboratory Preparation of Carbon(IV) Oxide

Carbon(IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$) is commonly prepared in the laboratory by the action of dilute acids on trioxocarbonate(IV) salts.
- **Reagents:** The usual reagents are **marble chips** (calcium trioxocarbonate(IV), $\text{CaCO}_3$) and **dilute hydrochloric acid** ($\text{HCl}$).
  $$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$
- **Important Note:** Dilute tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) is **never** used with marble chips because it forms insoluble calcium tetraoxosulphate(VI) ($\text{CaSO}_4$), which coats the marble and immediately stops the reaction.
- **Collection:** The gas is collected by **downward delivery (upward displacement of air)** because it is denser (heavier) than air. It can be dried using concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.

**Worked Example 3: Preparation of $\text{CO}_2$**
What volume of carbon(IV) oxide is evolved at s.t.p. when $5.0\text{ g}$ of calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) reacts completely with dilute hydrochloric acid? (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16, Molar volume at s.t.p. = $22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$)
1. Write the balanced equation:
   $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{CaCO}_3$:
   $40 + 12 + (16 \times 3) = 100\text{ g mol}^{-1}$
3. Find the number of moles of $\text{CaCO}_3$ used:
   $\text{Moles} = \frac{5.0\text{ g}}{100\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.05\text{ mol}$
4. Use the mole ratio:
   From the equation, $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ yields $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{CO}_2$. So, $0.05\text{ mol}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ yields $0.05\text{ mol}$ of $\text{CO}_2$.
5. Convert moles of $\text{CO}_2$ to volume at s.t.p.:
   $\text{Volume} = \text{moles} \times \text{molar volume} = 0.05\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1} = 1.12\text{ dm}^3$
6. Answer: The volume of $\text{CO}_2$ evolved is $1.12\text{ dm}^3$.

### Properties of Carbon(IV) Oxide and its Uses

**Properties:**
- It is a colourless, odourless gas, heavier than air.
- It is slightly soluble in water, forming weak **carbonic acid** (trioxocarbonate(IV) acid, $\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$), which turns damp blue litmus paper pale pink.
- It is an **acidic oxide**.
- It does not burn and generally does not support combustion. However, highly reactive metals like magnesium will burn in it to produce carbon soot:
  $2\text{Mg(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{MgO(s)} + \text{C(s)}$

**Uses:**
- Used in **fire extinguishers** because it is heavy (blankets the fire) and does not support burning.
- Used in manufacturing carbonated (fizzy) drinks.
- Solid $\text{CO}_2$ is called **dry ice**; it sublimes without melting and is used as a refrigerant by ice-cream sellers and in preserving food.
- Used in baking; yeast or baking powder generates $\text{CO}_2$, causing dough to rise.

### Reagents for the Laboratory Preparation of Carbon(II) Oxide

Carbon(II) oxide ($\text{CO}$), also known as carbon monoxide, is prepared by dehydrating certain organic acids.
- **Reagents:** Crystals of **ethanedioic acid** (oxalic acid, $\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4$) and **concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid** ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
- The concentrated acid acts as a powerful dehydrating agent, removing water from the oxalic acid:
  $\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{CO(g)}$
- The resulting gas mixture is then passed through concentrated **sodium hydroxide** ($\text{NaOH}$) or potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$) to absorb and remove the acidic $\text{CO}_2$, leaving only the neutral $\text{CO}$ gas to be collected over water.

### Effects of Carbon(II) Oxide on Humans

**Carbon(II) oxide is highly poisonous.** Its main sources include the incomplete combustion of charcoal, wood fires, and exhaust fumes from cars and portable generators. (In Nigeria, keeping a running generator indoors or in poorly ventilated spaces frequently leads to tragic fatalities).
- **Effect:** When inhaled, $\text{CO}$ passes into the bloodstream and binds strongly with the haemoglobin in red blood cells. It forms a highly stable compound called **carboxyhaemoglobin**. This permanently blocks the red blood cells from carrying oxygen to the body's tissues, quickly leading to suffocation and death.

### Specific Tests for HCO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, and CO₂

**Test for $\text{CO}_2$ gas:**
Bubble the unknown gas through clear **lime water** (calcium hydroxide solution, $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$). The lime water **turns milky** (forms a white precipitate of $\text{CaCO}_3$) if $\text{CO}_2$ is present. If you continue to bubble the gas for a long time, the milkiness disappears because the precipitate dissolves to form soluble calcium hydrogen trioxocarbonate(IV), $\text{Ca(HCO}_3\text{)}_2$.

**Test to distinguish $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ (Trioxocarbonates) and $\text{HCO}_3^-$ (Hydrogen trioxocarbonates):**
Both salts will effervesce (bubble vigorously) to give off $\text{CO}_2$ when dilute acid is added. To tell them apart:
- Add a solution of **magnesium tetraoxosulphate(VI)** ($\text{MgSO}_4$) to a solution of the salt.
- If a **white precipitate** forms immediately at room temperature, the salt is a **trioxocarbonate(IV)** ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$).
- If **no precipitate** forms at room temperature, but a white precipitate appears only **after heating**, the salt is a **hydrogen trioxocarbonate(IV)** ($\text{HCO}_3^-$).

*Note: Another difference is that all solid hydrogen trioxocarbonates(IV) decompose on heating to yield $\text{CO}_2$, but the solid trioxocarbonates(IV) of sodium and potassium do not decompose on heating.*

### Different Forms of Coal

Coal is a fossil fuel formed over millions of years from the buried remains of plants. Depending on the age and carbon content, it exists in several forms:
1. **Peat:** The first stage of coal formation. It has the lowest carbon content (about 60%), is very smoky when burned, and has a low heating value.
2. **Lignite (Brown coal):** Slightly older than peat, containing about 70% carbon.
3. **Bituminous coal (Soft coal):** Contains about 80% to 85% carbon. It is the most abundant type (e.g., the coal mined in Enugu, Nigeria, is largely sub-bituminous). It burns with a smoky flame.
4. **Anthracite (Hard coal):** The oldest form, containing about 90% to 95% carbon. It burns cleanly with little to no smoke and provides the most heat.

### Uses of Different Forms of Coal

- **Peat and Lignite:** Used occasionally as local domestic fuel and in some thermal power plants.
- **Bituminous coal:** Used heavily in industry for electricity generation, as a fuel, and destructively distilled to produce coke and coal gas.
- **Anthracite:** Used as a premium, smokeless domestic and industrial fuel because of its high heat output.

### Products of the Destructive Distillation of Wood and Coal

**Destructive distillation** is the process of heating a complex solid material to very high temperatures in the **absence of air**.

**Destructive distillation of Coal yields four main products:**
1. **Coal gas:** A gaseous mixture mainly consisting of hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$), methane ($\text{CH}_4$), and carbon(II) oxide ($\text{CO}$). It is an excellent fuel gas.
2. **Coal tar:** A thick, black, smelly liquid. It is a source of many important organic chemicals (like benzene, toluene, phenol) which are separated by fractional distillation to make dyes, drugs, plastics, and explosives.
3. **Ammoniacal liquor:** An aqueous solution of ammonia. It is usually reacted with sulphuric acid to produce ammonium tetraoxosulphate(VI) for use as fertilizer.
4. **Coke:** The solid carbon residue left behind.

**Destructive distillation of Wood yields:**
1. **Wood gas:** A fuel gas mixture.
2. **Pyroligneous acid:** An aqueous liquid containing methanol (wood spirit), ethanoic acid (acetic acid), and propanone (acetone).
3. **Wood tar:** A viscous liquid used to preserve timber.
4. **Wood charcoal:** The solid porous residue, widely used in Nigeria as a domestic cooking fuel and in industry to absorb gases or decolourise liquids.

### Uses of Coke and Synthesis Gas

**Coke** is predominantly pure carbon. It burns with a clean, smokeless flame.
- **Uses:** It is mainly used as a fuel and as a vital **reducing agent** in metallurgy (e.g., in the blast furnace for the extraction of iron). 
- It is also gasified to produce industrial fuel gases: producer gas and synthesis gas (water gas).

**Producer Gas and Synthesis Gas:**
1. **Producer Gas:** Made by passing air over red-hot coke.
   Equation: $2\text{C(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} + 4\text{N}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{CO(g)} + 4\text{N}_2\text{(g)}$
   It is a mixture of carbon(II) oxide and nitrogen. Because nitrogen does not burn, it has a low heating value, but it is cheap to produce.
2. **Synthesis Gas (Water Gas):** Made by passing steam ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$ gas) over red-hot coke.
   Equation: $\text{C(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$
   It is a mixture of carbon(II) oxide and hydrogen. Both gases are combustible, giving it a much higher heating value than producer gas.
   - **Uses of Synthesis gas:** It is an important industrial fuel. Crucially, it is used as a commercial source of hydrogen gas (for the Haber process) and used directly in the chemical industry to manufacture methanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Allotropy** | The existence of an element in two or more physical forms in the same physical state. |
| **Haber Process** | The industrial process used to manufacture ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen gases. |
| **Ostwald Process** | The industrial process used to manufacture nitric acid through the catalytic oxidation of ammonia. |
| **Destructive distillation** | Heating a substance to high temperatures in the absence of air. |
| **Synthesis gas (Water gas)** | A combustible mixture of carbon(II) oxide and hydrogen, produced by passing steam over hot coke. |
| **Producer gas** | A mixture of carbon(II) oxide and nitrogen, produced by passing air over hot coke. |
| **Brown ring test** | A specific laboratory test used to confirm the presence of trioxonitrate(V) ($\text{NO}_3^-$) ions. |
| **Carboxyhaemoglobin** | A stable compound formed when carbon(II) oxide binds permanently to haemoglobin in the blood. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Wrong drying agent for Ammonia:** A common JAMB trap is asking how to dry ammonia. Never choose concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ or $\text{CaCl}_2$ (they react with the alkaline gas). The correct drying agent is **quicklime ($\text{CaO}$)**.
- **Oxides of Nitrogen:** Remember that $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ is neutral and supports combustion, $\text{NO}$ is neutral and turns brown in air, and $\text{NO}_2$ is acidic, brown, and poisonous.
- **Brown Ring complex:** The formula for the brown ring is frequently asked; it is **$\text{FeSO}_4\cdot\text{NO}$**.
- **Carbon(II) vs Carbon(IV) Oxide:** Ensure you do not confuse them. $\text{CO}$ is neutral, poisonous (binds to blood), and supports combustion in itself. $\text{CO}_2$ is acidic, not toxic (causes suffocation simply by lack of oxygen), and extinguishes fire.
- **Action of heat on Carbonates:** Remember that Sodium ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) and Potassium ($\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3$) trioxocarbonates(IV) **do not decompose** on heating. All other carbonates decompose to yield the metal oxide and $\text{CO}_2$.
- **Distinguishing Carbonates and Bicarbonates:** Both give off $\text{CO}_2$ with dilute acids. To distinguish them, use $\text{MgSO}_4$. Trioxocarbonates(IV) give a precipitate immediately at room temperature; hydrogen trioxocarbonates(IV) require heat.

## Quick summary

1. Nitrogen is prepared in the lab from a mixture of ammonium chloride and sodium nitrite. It is produced industrially by the fractional distillation of liquid air.
2. Ammonia is prepared via the Haber process using finely divided iron at $450\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and $200\text{ atm}$. It is an alkaline gas, tested using damp red litmus or by the white fumes formed with $\text{HCl}$.
3. Trioxonitrate(V) acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) is a powerful oxidizing agent. Its presence as a salt is confirmed using the Brown ring test.
4. The nitrogen cycle converts inert atmospheric nitrogen into useful compounds (fixation, nitrification), and ultimately recycles it back to the atmosphere (denitrification).
5. Carbon exists as two main crystalline allotropes (diamond and graphite) and several amorphous forms. Diamond is hard and non-conducting; graphite is soft and conducts electricity.
6. Carbon(IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$) is prepared using marble chips and dilute $\text{HCl}$. It turns lime water milky. Carbon(II) oxide ($\text{CO}$) is prepared by dehydrating oxalic acid; it is extremely poisonous because it forms carboxyhaemoglobin in the blood.
7. The destructive distillation of coal in the absence of air produces coal gas, coal tar, ammoniacal liquor, and solid coke.
8. Coke is reacted with steam to form synthesis gas (a mixture of $\text{CO}$ and $\text{H}_2$), which is used as a fuel and as a commercial source of hydrogen.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following mixtures is typically heated to prepare nitrogen gas in the laboratory?**

- A. Ammonium chloride and sodium nitrite
- B. Ammonium nitrate and sodium chloride
- C. Sodium trioxonitrate (V) and ammonium chloride
- D. Ammonia and concentrated tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid

**Answer: A.** Nitrogen is prepared by heating a mixture of ammonium chloride and sodium nitrite, which react to form ammonium nitrite, decomposing to yield nitrogen. Option B is wrong because ammonium nitrate yields dinitrogen (I) oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), not nitrogen.

**2. What volume of ammonia is produced when $40\text{ cm}^3$ of nitrogen reacts completely with excess hydrogen at s.t.p.?**

- A. $20\text{ cm}^3$
- B. $40\text{ cm}^3$
- C. $80\text{ cm}^3$
- D. $120\text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Write the balanced equation for the Haber process: $\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)}$.
2. Identify the mole ratio (which equals the volume ratio for gases): $1\text{ volume}$ of $\text{N}_2$ produces $2\text{ volumes}$ of $\text{NH}_3$.
3. Substitute the given volume: $1\text{ volume} = 40\text{ cm}^3$.
4. Calculate the volume of ammonia: $2 \times 40\text{ cm}^3 = 80\text{ cm}^3$.
The most tempting wrong option is B ($40\text{ cm}^3$), which comes from assuming a 1:1 volume ratio between nitrogen and ammonia.

**3. A gas gives dense white fumes with a glass rod dipped in concentrated hydrochloric acid. The gas is**

- A. Nitrogen (IV) oxide
- B. Carbon (IV) oxide
- C. Hydrogen sulphide
- D. Ammonia

**Answer: D.** Ammonia gas reacts with hydrogen chloride gas to form fine, solid particles of ammonium chloride, appearing as dense white fumes. Option A is wrong because nitrogen (IV) oxide is a brown gas that does not form white fumes with $\text{HCl}$.

**4. Calculate the mass of potassium trioxonitrate(III) formed when $10.1\text{ g}$ of potassium trioxonitrate(V) is heated strongly. (K = 39, N = 14, O = 16)**

- A. $4.25\text{ g}$
- B. $8.50\text{ g}$
- C. $9.30\text{ g}$
- D. $17.00\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Write the balanced equation: $2\text{KNO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow 2\text{KNO}_2\text{(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{KNO}_3$: $39 + 14 + (16 \times 3) = 101\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Find the number of moles of $\text{KNO}_3$ heated: $\frac{10.1\text{ g}}{101\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.1\text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio: $2\text{ moles}$ of $\text{KNO}_3$ yield $2\text{ moles}$ of $\text{KNO}_2$. So, $0.1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{KNO}_3$ yields $0.1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{KNO}_2$.
5. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{KNO}_2$: $39 + 14 + (16 \times 2) = 85\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
6. Calculate the final mass: $0.1\text{ mol} \times 85\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 8.5\text{ g}$.
The most tempting wrong option is A ($4.25\text{ g}$), which comes from incorrectly assuming $2\text{ moles}$ of $\text{KNO}_3$ yield $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{KNO}_2$ and dividing the mass by two.

**5. What volume of carbon(IV) oxide is evolved at s.t.p. when $2.5\text{ g}$ of calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) reacts completely with dilute hydrochloric acid? (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16, Molar volume at s.t.p. = $22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$)**

- A. $0.28\text{ dm}^3$
- B. $0.56\text{ dm}^3$
- C. $1.12\text{ dm}^3$
- D. $2.24\text{ dm}^3$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{CaCO}_3$: $40 + 12 + (16 \times 3) = 100\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Find the number of moles of $\text{CaCO}_3$: $\frac{2.5\text{ g}}{100\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.025\text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio: $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ yields $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{CO}_2$. So, $0.025\text{ mol}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ is produced.
5. Convert moles to volume: $0.025\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1} = 0.56\text{ dm}^3$.
The most tempting wrong option is C ($1.12\text{ dm}^3$), which comes from incorrectly calculating the moles of $\text{CaCO}_3$ as $0.05\text{ mol}$ instead of $0.025\text{ mol}$.

**6. In the brown ring test for trioxonitrate (V) ions, the brown ring is formed by**

- A. $\text{FeSO}_4\cdot\text{NO}$
- B. $\text{Fe(NO}_3\text{)}_2$
- C. $\text{NO}_2$
- D. $\text{Fe}_2\text{(SO}_4\text{)}_3$

**Answer: A.** The brown ring formed at the junction of the two liquids is a complex salt called nitroso-iron (II) tetraoxosulphate (VI), $\text{FeSO}_4\cdot\text{NO}$. Option B is wrong because it is just iron (II) nitrate, which does not form the brown ring.

**7. When $9.0\text{ g}$ of ethanedioic acid is completely dehydrated using concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid, what volume of carbon(II) oxide is produced at s.t.p. after passing the gases through sodium hydroxide? (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Molar volume = $22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$)**

- A. $1.12\text{ dm}^3$
- B. $2.24\text{ dm}^3$
- C. $4.48\text{ dm}^3$
- D. $22.40\text{ dm}^3$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{CO(g)}$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of ethanedioic acid ($\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4$): $(1 \times 2) + (12 \times 2) + (16 \times 4) = 2 + 24 + 64 = 90\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Find the moles of acid used: $\frac{9.0\text{ g}}{90\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.1\text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio: $1\text{ mole}$ of acid yields $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{CO(g)}$. (The $\text{CO}_2$ is absorbed by $\text{NaOH}$). So, $0.1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{CO}$ is collected.
5. Convert moles to volume: $0.1\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1} = 2.24\text{ dm}^3$.
The most tempting wrong option is C ($4.48\text{ dm}^3$), which comes from adding the volume of $\text{CO}_2$ to $\text{CO}$ by failing to account for the sodium hydroxide absorbing the $\text{CO}_2$.

**8. What mass of oxygen gas is evolved when $17.0\text{ g}$ of sodium trioxonitrate(V) is heated to constant mass? (Na = 23, N = 14, O = 16)**

- A. $1.6\text{ g}$
- B. $3.2\text{ g}$
- C. $6.4\text{ g}$
- D. $32.0\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Write the balanced equation for the decomposition: $2\text{NaNO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaNO}_2\text{(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{NaNO}_3$: $23 + 14 + (16 \times 3) = 85\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Find the number of moles of $\text{NaNO}_3$: $\frac{17.0\text{ g}}{85\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.2\text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio: $2\text{ moles}$ of $\text{NaNO}_3$ yield $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{O}_2$. So, $0.2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{NaNO}_3$ yields $\frac{0.2}{2} = 0.1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{O}_2$.
5. Calculate the mass of $\text{O}_2$ (molar mass = $32\text{ g mol}^{-1}$): $0.1\text{ mol} \times 32\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 3.2\text{ g}$.
The most tempting wrong option is C ($6.4\text{ g}$), which comes from forgetting the $2:1$ mole ratio and assuming $0.2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{NaNO}_3$ yields $0.2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{O}_2$.

**9. What is the vapour density of the reddish-brown gas produced when nitrogen(II) oxide reacts with oxygen? (N = 14, O = 16)**

- A. 15
- B. 23
- C. 30
- D. 46

**Answer: B.** 
1. Identify the reddish-brown gas: It is nitrogen(IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$).
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{NO}_2$: $14 + (16 \times 2) = 14 + 32 = 46\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Recall the formula for vapour density: $\text{Vapour Density} = \frac{\text{Molar mass}}{2}$.
4. Calculate the vapour density: $\frac{46}{2} = 23$. (Vapour density has no units).
The most tempting wrong option is D (46), which is the molar mass of the gas, chosen if the student forgets to divide by 2 for vapour density.

**10. Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium trioxonitrate(V). (H = 1, N = 14, O = 16)**

- A. $17.5\%$
- B. $21.2\%$
- C. $35.0\%$
- D. $42.0\%$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Write the formula of ammonium trioxonitrate(V): $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of the compound: $14 + (1 \times 4) + 14 + (16 \times 3) = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Determine the total mass of nitrogen in one mole: There are two nitrogen atoms, so total mass = $14 + 14 = 28\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
4. Calculate the percentage: $\frac{28}{80} \times 100\% = 35.0\%$.
The most tempting wrong option is A ($17.5\%$), which comes from only counting one nitrogen atom ($14\text{ g}$) instead of the two present in the formula.
