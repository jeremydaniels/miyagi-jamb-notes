## Metals of the First Transition Series

**Content f: Metals of the first transition series.**

The first transition series consists of the elements from Scandium ($\text{Sc}$, atomic number 21) to Zinc ($\text{Zn}$, atomic number 30) in the d-block of the Periodic Table. A strict **transition metal** is defined as an element that forms at least one stable ion with a *partially filled d-orbital*. Because zinc only forms the $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ ion which has a completely full $3\text{d}^{10}$ configuration, it does not show typical transition metal properties (like coloured ions or variable oxidation states), but it is studied alongside them.

### General Properties of the First Transition Metals
**Objective xv: identify the general properties of the first transition metals.**

Transition metals are typical, hard metals. They display the following general physical and chemical properties:
1. **High melting and boiling points:** Their delocalised electrons (from both 4s and 3d subshells) form very strong metallic bonds.
2. **High density and tensile strength:** They are heavy and structurally strong.
3. **Good conductors:** They conduct heat and electricity excellently.
4. **Variable oxidation states:** They can form ions with different charges (e.g., iron can be $+2$ or $+3$).
5. **Formation of complex ions:** They easily bind with molecules or negative ions (ligands) to form complex ions.
6. **Formation of coloured compounds:** Most of their compounds and aqueous ions are brightly coloured.
7. **Catalytic activity:** The metals themselves and their compounds act as excellent catalysts in industrial processes.

### Reasons for the Specific Properties of Transition Metals
**Objective xvi: deduce reasons for the specific properties of the transition metals.**

#### 1. Electron Configuration
The electronic configuration explains all other transition metal properties. According to the Aufbau principle, the 4s subshell fills before the 3d subshell. For the first transition series, electrons enter the 3d orbitals after the 4s orbital is full. 
- General configuration: $[\text{Ar}] 4\text{s}^2 3\text{d}^{1 \text{ to } 10}$.
- **Exceptions (A JAMB favourite):** Chromium ($\text{Cr}$, atomic number 24) and Copper ($\text{Cu}$, atomic number 29) do not follow the rule. 
  - Chromium is $[\text{Ar}] 4\text{s}^1 3\text{d}^5$ (not $4\text{s}^2 3\text{d}^4$). This is because a **half-filled 3d subshell** is highly stable.
  - Copper is $[\text{Ar}] 4\text{s}^1 3\text{d}^{10}$ (not $4\text{s}^2 3\text{d}^9$). This is because a **fully-filled 3d subshell** is highly stable.

*Important Rule for forming ions:* When transition metals form ions, **they lose their 4s electrons FIRST**, before they lose any 3d electrons.

#### 2. Variable Oxidation States
The 4s and 3d energy levels are very close to each other in energy. After a transition metal loses its 4s electrons to form a $+2$ ion, it requires very little extra energy to remove one or more 3d electrons to form higher oxidation states (like $+3, +4, +5, +6,$ or $+7$). For example, Manganese ($\text{Mn}$) can show oxidation states from $+2$ up to $+7$.

#### 3. Complex Ion Formation
A complex ion consists of a central metal ion surrounded by molecules or negatively charged ions called **ligands**. Transition metal ions readily form complex ions for two reasons:
- They have a **small size and high positive charge density**, which strongly attracts electron pairs.
- They have **vacant d-orbitals** of low energy that can accept lone pairs of electrons from ligands to form coordinate (dative) covalent bonds.

An arrow points from the nitrogen atom of each NH3 molecule to the central Cu2+ ion, representing the donation of a lone pair of electrons (coordinate bond). The entire structure is enclosed in large square brackets with a "2+" charge outside. Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

#### 4. Formation of Coloured Ions
When ligands attach to a transition metal ion to form a complex, their electric fields cause the five normally equal-energy 3d orbitals to split into two slightly different energy levels. Because the d-orbitals are *partially filled*, an electron can absorb a specific wavelength of visible light to jump from the lower d-level to the higher d-level (a **d-d electron transition**). The colour we see is the complementary colour of the light that was absorbed. 
- *Note:* Ions with empty d-orbitals (like $\text{Sc}^{3+}$) or completely full d-orbitals (like $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Cu}^+$) are **colourless** (white in solid form) because d-d transitions cannot occur.

#### 5. Catalysis
Transition metals (like $\text{Fe}, \text{Ni}, \text{Pt}$) and their compounds (like $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5, \text{MnO}_2$) are excellent catalysts because:
- Their **variable oxidation states** allow them to easily participate in reaction steps, temporarily forming intermediate compounds, and then returning to their original state.
- They provide a solid **surface with vacant d-orbitals** where gaseous or liquid reactant molecules can temporarily stick (adsorption). This weakens the bonds in the reactants, lowering the activation energy.

### Naming Simple Transition Metal Complexes
**Objective xvii: determine the IUPAC names of simple transition metal complexes.**

The IUPAC rules for naming a complex ion are straightforward. 

**Steps for naming:**
1. **Name the ligands first:** List them in alphabetical order. 
   - Neutral ligands: water is **aqua**, ammonia is **ammine** (note the double 'm'), carbon(II) oxide is **carbonyl**.
   - Anionic (negative) ligands end in '-o': $\text{Cl}^-$ is **chloro**, $\text{F}^-$ is **fluoro**, $\text{CN}^-$ is **cyano**, $\text{OH}^-$ is **hydroxo**.
2. **Add a prefix for the number of ligands:** di- (2), tri- (3), tetra- (4), penta- (5), hexa- (6). The total number of coordinate bonds is the *coordination number*.
3. **Name the metal:**
   - If the complex ion has a positive charge (cation) or is neutral, keep the normal metal name (e.g., copper, iron, cobalt).
   - If the complex ion has a negative charge (anion), the metal name must end in **-ate** (e.g., copper becomes **cuprate**, iron becomes **ferrate**, cobalt becomes **cobaltate**, zinc becomes **zincate**).
4. **State the oxidation state of the metal:** Write it in Roman numerals in brackets at the end.

**Worked Example 1: Name the complex ion $[\text{Cu(NH}_3\text{)}_4]^{2+}$**
1. Identify the ligand: $\text{NH}_3$ is "ammine". There are $4$ ligands, making it "tetraammine".
2. Identify the metal: The complex has a positive $+2$ charge, so we use the standard name "copper".
3. Calculate the oxidation state of copper (this value is unitless). Let the oxidation state be $x$:
   Write the equation for the charges: $x + 4(\text{charge on NH}_3) = +2$
   Substitute the charge of neutral ammonia ($0$): $x + 4(0) = +2 \Rightarrow x = +2$.
4. State the final name: **tetraamminecopper(II) ion**.

**Worked Example 2: Name the complex ion $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$**
1. Identify the ligand: $\text{CN}^-$ is "cyano". There are $6$ ligands, making it "hexacyano".
2. Identify the metal: The complex has a negative $-4$ charge (an anion), so the metal name "iron" becomes "ferrate".
3. Calculate the oxidation state of iron (unitless). Let the oxidation state be $x$:
   Write the equation for the charges: $x + 6(\text{charge on CN}^-) = -4$
   Substitute the charge of the cyanide ion ($-1$): $x + 6(-1) = -4$
   Solve for $x$: $x - 6 = -4 \Rightarrow x = +2$.
4. State the final name: **hexacyanoferrate(II) ion**. (Note: If the whole compound was $\text{K}_4[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]$, the full name would be potassium hexacyanoferrate(II)).

## Iron: Extraction, Properties, and Tests

**Content g: Iron: Extraction from sulphide and oxide ores, properties and uses, different forms of iron, their properties and advantages of steel over iron. Test for $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ and $\text{Fe}^{3+}$.**

Iron is the most widely used metal in the world. In Nigeria, the Ajaokuta Steel Company in Kogi State was built to process local iron ore (mostly from Itakpe) into usable iron and steel.

### Suitable Method of Extraction of Iron
**Objective xviii: determine the suitable method of extraction of iron.**

Iron is extracted from its ores by **reduction** with carbon (coke) in a huge, tower-like structure called a **Blast Furnace**. 
- **Main oxide ores:** Haematite ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) and Magnetite ($\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$).
- **Other ores:** Siderite ($\text{FeCO}_3$) and Iron pyrites ($\text{FeS}_2$, a sulphide ore).
- **Pre-treatment:** If a sulphide ore ($\text{FeS}_2$) or carbonate ore ($\text{FeCO}_3$) is used, it is first roasted in air to convert it into an oxide ore (like haematite) before it is fed into the blast furnace, because the blast furnace is designed to reduce oxides.

**Steps and Reactions in the Blast Furnace:**
1. A solid mixture (the charge) of **iron ore**, **coke** (carbon), and **limestone** ($\text{CaCO}_3$) is continuously fed into the top of the furnace. A blast of hot air is blown in at the bottom.
2. **Production of the reducing agent:** The coke burns in the hot air blast to form carbon(IV) oxide in a highly exothermic reaction, generating the intense heat (up to $1900 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) needed for the furnace.
   $\text{C(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$
   As the $\text{CO}_2$ rises, it is reduced by more hot coke to form **carbon(II) oxide**, which is the actual reducing agent for the iron ore.
   $\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{C(s)} \rightarrow 2\text{CO(g)}$
3. **Reduction of the iron ore:** The carbon(II) oxide gas rises and reduces the descending haematite to molten iron.
   $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)} + 3\text{CO(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{Fe(l)} + 3\text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$
4. **Removal of impurities (silica):** Iron ore contains sand (silica, $\text{SiO}_2$) as a major earthy impurity. The heat of the furnace decomposes the limestone into calcium oxide and carbon(IV) oxide.
   $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaO(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$
   The basic calcium oxide reacts with the acidic silica to form liquid calcium trioxosilicate(IV) ($\text{CaSiO}_3$), universally known as **slag**.
   $\text{CaO(s)} + \text{SiO}_2\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaSiO}_3\text{(l)}$
5. The molten slag is less dense than the molten iron. It floats on top of the liquid iron pool at the bottom of the furnace, protecting the iron from being re-oxidised by the incoming hot air blast. The iron and slag are tapped off separately.

Near the bottom, pipes blow in 'Hot air blast'. Inside, label three temperature zones: top (about 400°C), middle (about 1000°C), bottom (about 1500°C). **Worked Example 3: Extraction calculation for Iron**
What mass of iron is produced from $1.6 \text{ kg}$ of pure haematite ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$)? [$\text{Fe} = 56, \text{O} = 16$]
1. Write the balanced equation for the reduction:
   $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)} + 3\text{CO(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{Fe(l)} + 3\text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$:
   $(2 \times 56 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) + (3 \times 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) = 112 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + 48 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 160 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Convert the given mass of haematite into grams, then to moles:
   $1.6 \text{ kg} = 1600 \text{ g}$.
   Moles of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 = 1600 \text{ g} / 160 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 10 \text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio (unitless) from the balanced equation:
   $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ yields $2 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Fe}$.
   Moles of $\text{Fe} = 10 \text{ mol} \times 2 = 20 \text{ mol}$.
5. Convert the moles of iron back into mass:
   Mass of $\text{Fe} = 20 \text{ mol} \times 56 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 1120 \text{ g}$ (or $1.12 \text{ kg}$).

### Properties and Uses of Iron
**Objective xix: specify the properties and uses of iron.**

**Physical properties:** Pure iron is a greyish-white, soft, malleable, and ductile metal. It is highly magnetic and has a high melting point ($1538 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$).

**Chemical properties:**
- **Rusting:** In the presence of **both oxygen and water**, iron corrodes easily to form rust (hydrated iron(III) oxide, $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}$). This is a major economic problem globally, leading to the rapid decay of abandoned vehicles left outside during the Nigerian rainy season.
- **Reaction with acids:** Iron is moderately reactive (it sits above hydrogen in the electrochemical series). It reacts with dilute $\text{HCl}$ or $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ to release hydrogen gas and form iron(II) salts.
  $\text{Fe(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{FeCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$
- **Reaction with halogens:** Heated iron reacts directly with chlorine gas. Because chlorine is a strong oxidising agent, it oxidises the iron fully to iron(III) chloride.
  $2\text{Fe(s)} + 3\text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{FeCl}_3\text{(s)}$

**Tests for Iron Ions:**
To distinguish between $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ and $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ in the laboratory, add a few drops of sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH(aq)}$) or aqueous ammonia ($\text{NH}_3\text{(aq)}$) to the unknown solution.
- **Test for $\text{Fe}^{2+}$:** Gives a **dirty-green precipitate** of $\text{Fe(OH)}_2$, which is insoluble in excess of the reagent. If left to stand in air, the green precipitate slowly turns reddish-brown as it oxidises to iron(III) hydroxide.
- **Test for $\text{Fe}^{3+}$:** Gives a **reddish-brown precipitate** of $\text{Fe(OH)}_3$, which is insoluble in excess of the reagent. 

### Different Forms of Iron and Advantages of Steel
**Objective xx: identify the different forms of iron, their compositions, properties and uses.**

The properties of iron depend heavily on how much carbon it contains as an impurity. The iron tapped directly from the blast furnace contains too much carbon, making it brittle.

| Form of Iron | Carbon Content | Properties | Uses |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pig iron / Cast iron** | $2\%$ to $5\%$ | Very hard but **brittle** (shatters if struck). Cannot be forged or welded. Melts relatively easily. | Manhole covers, engine blocks, heavy machine bases, stoves. |
| **Wrought iron** | Less than $0.1\%$ | The **purest** commercial form. Soft, tough, highly malleable, and ductile. Can be forged and welded. | Agricultural implements (hoes, cutlasses), ornamental gates, chains, electromagnets. |
| **Steel** | $0.1\%$ to $1.5\%$ | An alloy that combines the hardness of cast iron with the toughness and ductility of wrought iron. | Structural beams, car bodies, ships, bridges, surgical instruments (if alloyed with other metals). |

**Advantages of steel over pure iron:**
Steel is vastly superior to pure iron or cast iron because its properties can be finely tuned. By controlling the exact amount of carbon and adding other transition metals (like chromium, nickel, or manganese), steel can be made incredibly strong, flexible, and completely resistant to rusting (unlike pure iron, which rusts easily and flakes apart).

## Copper: Extraction, Properties, and Tests

**Content h: Copper: Extraction from sulphide and oxide ores, properties and uses of copper. Preparation and uses of copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI). Test for $\text{Cu}^{2+}$.**

Copper is a transition metal famous for its distinctive reddish-brown colour, excellent conductivity, and its unreactive nature.

### Method of Extraction of Copper
**Objective xxi: identify the appropriate method of extraction of copper from its compounds.**

The primary ore of copper is **copper pyrites** (chalcopyrite, $\text{CuFeS}_2$), a sulphide ore containing both copper and iron. It can also be extracted from simple sulphide ores like copper glance ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$) or oxide ores like cuprite ($\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$).

**Extraction from Sulphide Ores (Smelting and Self-Reduction):**
1. The crushed ore is concentrated using froth flotation to remove earthy impurities.
2. **Roasting:** The concentrated ore is roasted in a limited supply of air. The iron is mostly converted to iron(II) oxide ($\text{FeO}$), and the copper to copper(I) sulphide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$).
   $2\text{CuFeS}_2\text{(s)} + 4\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{Cu}_2\text{S(s)} + 2\text{FeO(s)} + 3\text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$
3. **Removal of iron:** Silica ($\text{SiO}_2$) is added to the furnace to react with the iron(II) oxide, forming an iron silicate slag ($\text{FeSiO}_3$), which is poured off.
4. **Self-reduction:** The remaining $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ is partially oxidised by roasting in an air blast to form copper(I) oxide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$). 
   $2\text{Cu}_2\text{S(s)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{Cu}_2\text{O(s)} + 2\text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$
   Then the air supply is stopped. The unreacted copper(I) sulphide reduces the copper(I) oxide to liquid copper metal. This is known as **self-reduction**.
   $\text{Cu}_2\text{S(s)} + 2\text{Cu}_2\text{O(s)} \rightarrow 6\text{Cu(l)} + \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$
5. The solid product obtained upon cooling is called **blister copper** because escaping $\text{SO}_2$ gas creates blisters on its surface.

**Worked Example 4: Calculation of Copper Extraction**
What mass of copper is produced from the complete self-reduction of $31.8 \text{ kg}$ of copper(I) sulphide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$) reacting with excess copper(I) oxide? [$\text{Cu} = 63.5, \text{S} = 32$]
1. Write the balanced equation for self-reduction:
   $\text{Cu}_2\text{S(s)} + 2\text{Cu}_2\text{O(s)} \rightarrow 6\text{Cu(l)} + \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$:
   $(2 \times 63.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) + 32 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 127 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + 32 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 159 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Convert the mass of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ to moles:
   $31.8 \text{ kg} = 31800 \text{ g}$.
   Moles of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S} = 31800 \text{ g} / 159 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 200 \text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio (unitless) from the balanced equation to find moles of copper:
   $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ yields $6 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}$.
   Moles of $\text{Cu} = 200 \text{ mol} \times 6 = 1200 \text{ mol}$.
5. Convert moles of $\text{Cu}$ to mass:
   Mass of $\text{Cu} = 1200 \text{ mol} \times 63.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 76200 \text{ g}$ (or $76.2 \text{ kg}$).

**Electrolytic Purification:**
Blister copper is impure. It is purified by electrolysis. 
- The **anode** (positive electrode) is made of the impure blister copper.
- The **cathode** (negative electrode) is a thin strip of very pure copper.
- The **electrolyte** is an acidified solution of copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) ($\text{CuSO}_4$).
During electrolysis, copper dissolves from the anode as $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions and deposits as extremely pure copper metal on the cathode. Valuable impurities like silver and gold do not dissolve but fall to the bottom as "anode sludge."

### Properties of Copper and Its Compounds and Their Uses
**Objective xxii: relate the properties of copper and its compound to their uses.**

**Physical properties:** It is a soft, malleable, ductile, reddish-brown metal. It has extremely high electrical and thermal conductivity, second only to silver among all elements.
*Uses based on properties:* Its unmatched conductivity-to-cost ratio makes it the preferred metal for **electrical wiring** and **cables**. Its lack of reaction with water and excellent heat conductivity make it perfect for **plumbing pipes** and the **bases of cooking boilers**.

**Chemical properties:** Copper is very unreactive and sits below hydrogen in the electrochemical series.
- **Action of dilute acids:** Copper **does not react** with dilute, non-oxidising acids like dilute $\text{HCl}$ or dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, so it never displaces hydrogen gas in the lab.
- **Action of oxidising acids:** It only reacts with powerful oxidising agents like trioxonitrate(V) acid ($\text{HNO}_3$). 
  - With dilute $\text{HNO}_3$, it forms colourless nitrogen(II) oxide ($\text{NO}$) gas.
  - With concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$, it forms reddish-brown nitrogen(IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$) gas.
  $\text{Cu(s)} + 4\text{HNO}_3\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3\text{)}_2\text{(aq)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + 2\text{NO}_2\text{(g)}$

**Test for $\text{Cu}^{2+}$:**
To test for copper(II) ions in a solution:
- Add a few drops of sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH(aq)}$): A **pale blue precipitate** of $\text{Cu(OH)}_2$ forms, which is insoluble in excess $\text{NaOH}$.
- Add aqueous ammonia ($\text{NH}_3\text{(aq)}$): A pale blue precipitate forms initially. When **excess ammonia** is added, the precipitate dissolves to form a **deep blue solution**. This is due to the formation of the complex tetraamminecopper(II) ion, $[\text{Cu(NH}_3\text{)}_4]^{2+}$. This ammonia test is a definitive proof for copper.

### Preparation of Copper(II) Tetraoxosulphate(VI)
**Objective xxiii: specify the method for the preparation of $\text{CuSO}_4$.**

Because copper metal cannot displace hydrogen, it cannot be used directly with dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ to make the salt. Instead, we use an insoluble basic copper compound, usually black copper(II) oxide ($\text{CuO}$) or green copper(II) trioxocarbonate(IV) ($\text{CuCO}_3$).

**Step-by-step preparation:**
1. Place a known volume of dilute tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid in a beaker and warm it gently.
2. Add the black copper(II) oxide powder a little at a time while stirring, until no more solid dissolves (this ensures the acid is completely neutralized). 
   $\text{CuO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$
3. Filter the warm mixture to remove the excess unreacted copper(II) oxide. The filtrate is a clear blue solution of $\text{CuSO}_4$.
4. Heat the filtrate in an evaporating dish to evaporate some water (concentrating the solution), then allow it to cool slowly.
5. Large **blue crystals** of copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) pentahydrate ($\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$) will crystallize out. Filter and dry them between filter papers.

**Uses of $\text{CuSO}_4$:** 
- Used extensively in agriculture as a fungicide (e.g., Bordeaux mixture).
- Used as the electrolyte in copper electroplating and purification.
- When heated heavily, the blue crystals lose their water of crystallization to become a **white powder** (anhydrous $\text{CuSO}_4$). This white powder is used in the laboratory to **test for the presence of water**, as adding even a drop of water immediately turns it blue again.

## Common Alloys

**Content i: Alloy: Steel, stainless steel, brass, bronze, type-metal, duralumin, soft solder, permallory and alnico.**

An alloy is a uniform solid mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal (like carbon).

### Constituents and Uses of Various Alloys
**Objective xxiv: specify the constituents and uses of the various alloys mentioned.**

JAMB expects you to memorize the exact metallic constituents of these specific alloys.

| Alloy | Constituents | Major Uses |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Steel** | Iron ($\text{Fe}$), Carbon ($\text{C}$) | Construction of bridges, structural beams, ships, vehicles. |
| **Stainless steel** | Iron ($\text{Fe}$), Carbon ($\text{C}$), Chromium ($\text{Cr}$), Nickel ($\text{Ni}$) | Cutlery, surgical instruments, chemical plant equipment (it resists rusting). |
| **Brass** | Copper ($\text{Cu}$), Zinc ($\text{Zn}$) | Decorative items, door handles, moving machine parts, ammunition casings, musical instruments (e.g., Bida brass works in Niger State). |
| **Bronze** | Copper ($\text{Cu}$), Tin ($\text{Sn}$) | Medals, coins, statues (e.g., historic Benin and Ife bronzes), ship propellers. |
| **Duralumin** | Aluminium ($\text{Al}$), Copper ($\text{Cu}$), Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), Manganese ($\text{Mn}$) | Construction of aircraft and lightweight vehicles due to its extremely low density and high strength. |
| **Soft solder** | Lead ($\text{Pb}$), Tin ($\text{Sn}$) | Joining electrical wires and plumbing joints (it has a safely low melting point). |
| **Type-metal** | Lead ($\text{Pb}$), Antimony ($\text{Sb}$), Tin ($\text{Sn}$) | Making printing type blocks (the antimony makes it expand slightly on cooling to give sharp letters). |
| **Permalloy*** | Nickel ($\text{Ni}$), Iron ($\text{Fe}$) | Magnetic shielding and cores for electrical transformers. (*Note: spelt "permallory" in the JAMB syllabus*). |
| **Alnico** | Aluminium ($\text{Al}$), Nickel ($\text{Ni}$), Cobalt ($\text{Co}$), Iron ($\text{Fe}$) | Making powerful permanent magnets for loudspeakers and electric motors. |

### Comparing Properties and Uses of Alloys to Pure Metals
**Objective xxv: compare the properties and uses of alloys to pure metals.**

Pure metals are often too soft, too reactive, or too easily worn away to be used on their own in engineering. Alloying completely changes their physical properties to make them more useful.

| Property | Pure Metals | Alloys | Why the difference occurs |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Hardness & Strength** | Softer and more malleable. | **Harder and stronger.** | In a pure metal, atoms are in regular layers that slide easily over one another. Adding different-sized atoms disrupts these layers, preventing them from sliding. |
| **Corrosion** | Often react easily (e.g., iron rusts quickly). | **More resistant.** | Added elements (like Chromium in stainless steel) form thin, invisible protective oxide layers. |
| **Melting Point** | Sharp, highly specific melting point. | **Lower melting point**, often melting over a temperature range. | The disrupted lattice structure requires less thermal energy to break apart. Solder melts before the wires it joins. |
| **Conductivity** | Excellent electrical and thermal conductors. | **Lower conductivity.** | The irregular arrangement of mixed atoms scatters the free-flowing delocalised electrons. |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Transition metal** | A metal that forms at least one stable ion with a partially filled d-orbital. |
| **Ligand** | An ion or molecule that donates a lone pair of electrons to a central metal ion to form a complex. |
| **Complex ion** | A central metal ion surrounded by coordinately bonded ligands. |
| **Coordination number** | The total number of coordinate bonds formed between a central metal ion and its ligands. |
| **Blast furnace** | A tall industrial furnace used to extract iron from its ore by reduction with carbon. |
| **Slag** | A waste product formed in smelting when an acidic impurity (like sand) reacts with a basic flux (like calcium oxide). |
| **Smelting** | The process of extracting a metal from its ore by heating it with carbon. |
| **Self-reduction** | A process where a partially roasted ore (like $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$) is reduced by its own unroasted sulphide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$). |
| **Alloy** | A solid mixture of a metal with other elements, designed to improve its physical properties. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mistake:** Assuming zinc is a typical transition metal. It is a d-block element, but because its d-orbital is completely full ($3\text{d}^{10}$) in both its atomic and ionic states, it forms white/colourless compounds and does not show variable oxidation states.
- **Mistake:** Writing the electron configuration for copper as $[\text{Ar}] 4\text{s}^2 3\text{d}^9$. The correct configuration is $[\text{Ar}] 4\text{s}^1 3\text{d}^{10}$. Chromium is $[\text{Ar}] 4\text{s}^1 3\text{d}^5$.
- **Mistake:** Forgetting that transition metals lose their **4s electrons first** when forming ions. $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is $[\text{Ar}] 3\text{d}^5$, because it lost two from 4s and one from 3d.
- **Mistake:** Confusing the colours of iron ions. **$\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is green**; **$\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is reddish-brown**. 
- **Mistake:** Confusing Brass and Bronze. Use the memory trick: *Bronze has a Z in its name, but NO zinc in its metal (it's Cu and Sn). Brass has NO Z in its name, but DOES have zinc (it's Cu and Zn).*
- **Mistake:** Thinking that copper reacts with dilute $\text{HCl}$ to produce hydrogen gas. Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series; it **does not react** with dilute non-oxidising acids.

## Quick summary

1. Transition metals (Sc to Zn) typically have partially filled d-orbitals, resulting in variable oxidation states, complex ion formation, coloured compounds, and catalytic ability.
2. Chromium and copper have anomalous electron configurations to achieve the extra stability of half-filled and fully-filled 3d subshells.
3. Simple complexes are named by listing ligands alphabetically with prefixes, then the metal (using an '-ate' suffix if it is a negatively charged anion), followed by its oxidation state in Roman numerals.
4. Iron is extracted in a blast furnace using carbon(II) oxide to reduce haematite. Limestone decomposes to calcium oxide, which removes silica impurities as calcium trioxosilicate(IV) slag.
5. Pig iron is hard and brittle (high carbon); wrought iron is soft and pure (low carbon). Steel is an alloy with adjustable carbon levels making it stronger than pure iron.
6. Copper is extracted via self-reduction of copper pyrites and purified by electrolysis. It does not react with dilute non-oxidising acids but dissolves in powerful oxidising acids like nitric acid.
7. Adding excess aqueous ammonia to $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions produces a definitive deep blue solution (the tetraamminecopper(II) complex).
8. Alloys (like bronze, brass, steel, and duralumin) are generally harder, stronger, more corrosion-resistant, and have lower melting points than their pure base metals.

## Practice questions

**1. What is the oxidation state of iron in the complex ion $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$?**
- A. $+2$
- B. $+3$
- C. $+4$
- D. $+6$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Identify the charge on the complex ion: $-4$.
2. Identify the charge on each cyano ligand ($\text{CN}^-$): $-1$.
3. Let the unitless oxidation state of iron be $x$. Write the algebraic equation: $x + 6(-1) = -4$.
4. Solve for $x$: $x - 6 = -4 \Rightarrow x = -4 + 6 = +2$.
Mistake behind option C: assuming the oxidation state of the metal is simply equal to the positive magnitude of the overall charge on the complex.

**2. What mass of iron is produced from the complete reduction of $160 \text{ g}$ of iron(III) oxide ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) in a blast furnace? [$\text{Fe} = 56, \text{O} = 16$]**
- A. $56 \text{ g}$
- B. $112 \text{ g}$
- C. $160 \text{ g}$
- D. $224 \text{ g}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{CO} \rightarrow 2\text{Fe} + 3\text{CO}_2$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$: $(2 \times 56 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) + (3 \times 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) = 160 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate the number of moles of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$: $160 \text{ g} / 160 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 1 \text{ mol}$.
4. Use the unitless mole ratio: $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ yields $2 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Fe}$.
5. Calculate the mass of iron produced: $2 \text{ mol} \times 56 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 112 \text{ g}$.
Mistake behind option A: calculating the molar mass of one iron atom ($56 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$) but failing to multiply by the mole ratio of 2.

**3. What is the percentage by mass of water of crystallization in copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) pentahydrate ($\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$)? [$\text{Cu} = 63.5, \text{S} = 32, \text{O} = 16, \text{H} = 1$]**
- A. $36.1\%$
- B. $45.0\%$
- C. $63.5\%$
- D. $90.0\%$

**Answer: A.**
1. Calculate the molar mass of water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$): $(2 \times 1 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) + 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 18 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. Calculate the mass of $5$ moles of water: $5 \times 18 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 90 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate the molar mass of the entire compound ($\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$): $63.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + 32 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + (4 \times 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) + 90 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 249.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
4. Calculate the percentage of water (unitless): $(90 \text{ g mol}^{-1} / 249.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) \times 100\% = 36.07\% \approx 36.1\%$.
Mistake behind option D: correctly calculating the mass of the water ($90 \text{ g}$) but failing to divide it by the total molar mass to find the percentage.

**4. If a piece of bronze is $90\%$ copper and $10\%$ tin by mass, what mass of copper is present in a $50 \text{ g}$ sample of this alloy?**
- A. $5 \text{ g}$
- B. $10 \text{ g}$
- C. $45 \text{ g}$
- D. $50 \text{ g}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Identify the total mass of the alloy sample: $50 \text{ g}$.
2. Identify the percentage composition of copper in bronze: $90\%$.
3. Convert the percentage to a unitless decimal: $90 / 100 = 0.90$.
4. Calculate the mass of copper: $0.90 \times 50 \text{ g} = 45 \text{ g}$.
Mistake behind option A: calculating the mass of the minor constituent (tin), which is $10\%$ of $50 \text{ g} = 5 \text{ g}$, instead of copper.

**5. In the self-reduction extraction of copper, what mass of copper is produced from the complete reaction of $15.9 \text{ g}$ of copper(I) sulphide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$) with excess copper(I) oxide? [$\text{Cu} = 63.5, \text{S} = 32$]**
- A. $6.35 \text{ g}$
- B. $12.7 \text{ g}$
- C. $38.1 \text{ g}$
- D. $76.2 \text{ g}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{Cu}_2\text{S} + 2\text{Cu}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 6\text{Cu} + \text{SO}_2$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$: $(2 \times 63.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) + 32 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 159 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate moles of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$: $15.9 \text{ g} / 159 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 0.1 \text{ mol}$.
4. Use the unitless mole ratio: $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ yields $6 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}$. So, $0.1 \text{ mol} \times 6 = 0.6 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}$.
5. Calculate the mass of copper: $0.6 \text{ mol} \times 63.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 38.1 \text{ g}$.
Mistake behind option B: ignoring the balanced chemical equation entirely and assuming that $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ simply splits to yield $2 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}$ ($0.2 \text{ mol} \times 63.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 12.7 \text{ g}$).

**6. How many moles of electrons (Faradays) are required to deposit $63.5 \text{ g}$ of pure copper at the cathode during the electrolytic purification of blister copper? [$\text{Cu} = 63.5$]**
- A. $1.0 \text{ F}$
- B. $2.0 \text{ F}$
- C. $3.0 \text{ F}$
- D. $4.0 \text{ F}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Write the half-equation at the cathode: $\text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu(s)}$.
2. Calculate the number of moles of copper to be deposited: $63.5 \text{ g} / 63.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 1.0 \text{ mol}$.
3. Use the unitless mole ratio from the half-equation: $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cu}$ requires $2 \text{ mol}$ of electrons.
4. Calculate the Faradays (where $1 \text{ F} = 1 \text{ mol}$ of electrons): $1.0 \text{ mol} \times 2 = 2.0 \text{ F}$.
Mistake behind option A: assuming that the copper ion is $\text{Cu}^+$ instead of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$, which would lead to a calculation of $1.0 \text{ F}$.

**7. What is the mass of calcium trioxosilicate(IV) ($\text{CaSiO}_3$) slag formed when $100 \text{ g}$ of calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) ($\text{CaCO}_3$) decomposes completely and reacts with excess silica in the blast furnace? [$\text{Ca} = 40, \text{C} = 12, \text{O} = 16, \text{Si} = 28$]**
- A. $56 \text{ g}$
- B. $100 \text{ g}$
- C. $116 \text{ g}$
- D. $216 \text{ g}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Write the balanced overall equation: $\text{CaCO}_3 + \text{SiO}_2 \rightarrow \text{CaSiO}_3 + \text{CO}_2$.
2. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{CaCO}_3$: $40 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + 12 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + (3 \times 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) = 100 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate moles of $\text{CaCO}_3$: $100 \text{ g} / 100 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 1 \text{ mol}$.
4. Use the unitless mole ratio: $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ yields $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{CaSiO}_3$.
5. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{CaSiO}_3$: $40 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + 28 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + (3 \times 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) = 116 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
6. Calculate the mass of slag: $1 \text{ mol} \times 116 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 116 \text{ g}$.
Mistake behind option A: calculating the mass of calcium oxide ($\text{CaO}$) produced as an intermediate ($56 \text{ g}$), instead of the final slag.

**8. What is the oxidation state of copper in the complex ion $[\text{Cu(NH}_3\text{)}_4]^{2+}$?**
- A. $0$
- B. $+1$
- C. $+2$
- D. $+4$

**Answer: C.**
1. Identify the overall charge on the complex ion: $+2$.
2. Identify the charge on each ammine ligand ($\text{NH}_3$): neutral, so $0$.
3. Let the unitless oxidation state of copper be $x$. Write the equation: $x + 4(0) = +2$.
4. Solve for $x$: $x = +2$.
Mistake behind option D: incorrectly assuming the oxidation state is equal to the coordination number ($4$), rather than solving algebraically for the charge.

**9. How many 3d electrons are present in the iron(III) ion ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$)? [Atomic number of $\text{Fe} = 26$]**
- A. $3$
- B. $4$
- C. $5$
- D. $6$

**Answer: C.**
1. Write the electron configuration of a neutral iron atom ($26$ electrons): $[\text{Ar}] 4\text{s}^2 3\text{d}^6$.
2. Identify the total number of electrons that must be removed to form an $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ion: $3$ electrons.
3. Apply the rule that transition metals lose their 4s electrons before their 3d electrons: remove two electrons from the 4s subshell.
4. Remove the remaining one electron from the 3d subshell ($6 - 1 = 5$). The final configuration is $[\text{Ar}] 3\text{d}^5$.
5. Count the remaining 3d electrons: $5$.
Mistake behind option D: stating the number of 3d electrons in the *neutral* iron atom ($3\text{d}^6$) instead of accounting for the electrons lost to form the $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ion.

**10. What is the concentration in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$ of a solution made by dissolving $12.5 \text{ g}$ of copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) pentahydrate ($\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$) in $250 \text{ cm}^3$ of water? [$\text{Cu} = 64, \text{S} = 32, \text{O} = 16, \text{H} = 1$]**
- A. $0.0125 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- B. $0.05 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- C. $0.20 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- D. $2.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Calculate the molar mass of $\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (using $\text{Cu}=64$ for JAMB simplicity): $64 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + 32 \text{ g mol}^{-1} + (4 \times 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) + (5 \times 18 \text{ g mol}^{-1}) = 250 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. Calculate the number of moles: $12.5 \text{ g} / 250 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 0.05 \text{ mol}$.
3. Convert the volume from $\text{cm}^3$ to $\text{dm}^3$: $250 \text{ cm}^3 / 1000 \text{ cm}^3 \text{ dm}^{-3} = 0.25 \text{ dm}^3$.
4. Calculate the concentration: $0.05 \text{ mol} / 0.25 \text{ dm}^3 = 0.20 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
Mistake behind option B: stopping after finding the number of moles ($0.05 \text{ mol}$) and failing to divide by the volume to find the concentration.
