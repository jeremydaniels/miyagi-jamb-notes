## Alkanols

Alkanols (commonly known as alcohols) are organic compounds containing the **hydroxyl group** ($-\text{OH}$) attached to an alkyl group. Their general formula is $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{OH}$ (or $\text{R-OH}$). 

The $-\text{OH}$ functional group dictates their chemical properties. For example, they react with highly electropositive metals like sodium ($\text{Na}$) to release hydrogen gas, and they undergo oxidation and esterification.

### Comparing classes of alkanols

Alkanols are classified based on the number of alkyl groups (carbon atoms) directly attached to the specific carbon atom bearing the $-\text{OH}$ group.

| Class | Structure / Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary ($1^\circ$) alkanol** | The carbon with the $-\text{OH}$ group is attached to only one (or zero) alkyl group. It is located at the end of a carbon chain. | Ethanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$), Propan-1-ol |
| **Secondary ($2^\circ$) alkanol** | The carbon with the $-\text{OH}$ group is attached to exactly two alkyl groups. | Propan-2-ol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)CH}_3$) |
| **Tertiary ($3^\circ$) alkanol** | The carbon with the $-\text{OH}$ group is attached to exactly three alkyl groups. | 2-methylpropan-2-ol |

**Polyhydric alkanols:**
Most basic alkanols studied at this level are monohydric (containing one $-\text{OH}$ group). However, polyhydric alkanols contain two or more hydroxyl groups. A very important example from your syllabus is **glycerol** (IUPAC name: propane-1,2,3-triol). Glycerol is a sweet, viscous, colourless liquid with three $-\text{OH}$ groups. It is obtained as a major by-product during soap making (saponification) and is used in cosmetics (to trap moisture on the skin) and in the manufacture of explosives.

### Distinguishing the classes of alkanols

Chemists distinguish between $1^\circ$, $2^\circ$, and $3^\circ$ alkanols using two main laboratory tests:

**1. The Lucas Test**
This test uses the **Lucas reagent**, which is a mixture of anhydrous zinc chloride ($\text{ZnCl}_2$) and concentrated hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$). The reaction replaces the $-\text{OH}$ group with a chlorine atom ($-\text{Cl}$), forming an insoluble chloroalkane that separates from the mixture, making the solution look cloudy (turbid).
- **Tertiary ($3^\circ$) alkanols:** React immediately. **Immediate turbidity** is observed because they form a stable carbocation very quickly.
- **Secondary ($2^\circ$) alkanols:** React moderately. **Turbidity appears in 5 to 10 minutes**.
- **Primary ($1^\circ$) alkanols:** Do not react at room temperature. **No turbidity** is observed unless the mixture is strongly heated.

Labels: "1° alkanol", "2° alkanol", "3° alkanol", "Lucas reagent (ZnCl₂ + conc. HCl)". Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**2. Oxidation Test**
Alkanols can be oxidized using strong oxidizing agents like acidified potassium heptaoxodichromate(VI) ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$) or acidified potassium tetraoxomanganate(VII) ($\text{KMnO}_4$).
- **Primary ($1^\circ$) alkanols:** Easily oxidize to form **alkanals** (aldehydes). With further oxidation, they form **alkanoic acids**. The oxidizing agent changes colour (e.g., orange $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ turns to green $\text{Cr}^{3+}$).
- **Secondary ($2^\circ$) alkanols:** Oxidize to form **alkanones** (ketones). The reaction stops there because the carbon chain would have to be broken for further oxidation. The oxidizing agent changes colour.
- **Tertiary ($3^\circ$) alkanols:** **Do not oxidize** under normal conditions. The oxidizing agent keeps its original colour.

> **Common Mistake in Alkanols:** Students often assume that because "tertiary" is a larger number, it reacts slower. In the Lucas test, the $3^\circ$ alkanol is actually the **fastest** to react. However, in oxidation, the $3^\circ$ alkanol does not react at all.

### Processes involved in ethanol production

Ethanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$) is the most economically important alkanol. It can be produced both locally and industrially through fermentation, or industrially from petrochemicals.

**1. Fermentation of carbohydrates (Traditional and Industrial)**
Fermentation is the slow breakdown of sugars by microorganisms (yeast) in the absence of oxygen, producing ethanol and carbon (IV) oxide.
- **Local production:** In Nigeria, local gin (often called *ogogoro*, *kaikai*, or *sapele water*) is made from palm wine. Tappers collect the sweet sap of the oil palm or raffia palm. Wild yeast naturally present in the air and on the tree ferments the sugars into ethanol within hours. The fermented palm wine is then boiled in a crude distillation setup. Because ethanol boils at $78^\circ\text{C}$ (lower than water's $100^\circ\text{C}$), it evaporates first, passes through a cooling pipe, and condenses into a highly concentrated, clear spirit.
- **Industrial fermentation:** Starchy foods like cassava or yam, or molasses (a by-product of sugar cane processing), are used on a large scale. 
  - **Step 1:** The enzyme **diastase** (found in malt) breaks down complex starch into maltose.
    $$ 2(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{10}\text{O}_5)_n\text{(s)} + n\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow n\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}\text{(aq)} $$
  - **Step 2:** The enzyme **maltase** (from yeast) converts maltose into simple glucose.
    $$ \text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\text{(aq)} $$
  - **Step 3:** The enzyme **zymase** (also from yeast) directly ferments glucose down into ethanol and carbon (IV) oxide.
    $$ \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH(aq)} + 2\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} $$

[IMAGE: A traditional distillation set-up for making local gin from palm wine. A metal drum over a firewood fire contains boiling fermented palm wine. A pipe leads from the top of the drum through a large wooden barrel filled with cooling water, then out the bottom where clear liquid drops into a collection calabash or glass bottle. Labels: "firewood", "drum of boiling palm wine", "ethanol vapour", "cooling water barrel", "condensed local gin (ogogoro)". Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

**2. From petroleum by-products (Industrial)**
Ethanol is manufactured on a massive scale by the catalytic hydration of ethene (an alkene obtained from the cracking of crude oil). 
Ethene gas reacts with steam ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) at $300^\circ\text{C}$ and $70 \text{ atm}$ pressure, using concentrated phosphoric(V) acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$) or tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) as a catalyst.
$$ \text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \rightleftharpoons \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH(g)} $$

### Ethanol as an alternative energy provider

Because crude oil is a non-renewable fossil fuel and its combustion causes severe environmental pollution, ethanol is increasingly used as a renewable fuel, known as **bioethanol**. 
- It burns cleanly in plenty of oxygen to produce only carbon (IV) oxide and water, releasing a substantial amount of heat energy:
  $$ \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH(l)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(g)} + \text{Heat} $$
- In some countries (and increasingly proposed in agricultural programs in Nigeria), bioethanol derived from cassava or sugarcane is blended with standard petrol (gasoline) to form **gasohol**. This reduces dependence on imported crude oil, supports local farming, and lowers net greenhouse gas emissions because the plants absorb $\text{CO}_2$ while growing.

## Alkanals and alkanones

Alkanals (aldehydes) and alkanones (ketones) are two classes of organic compounds that both contain the reactive **carbonyl group** ($>\text{C=O}$).

- **Alkanals:** The carbonyl group is at the *end* of the carbon chain, meaning the carbon atom is attached to at least one hydrogen atom ($-\text{CHO}$). Examples include Methanal ($\text{HCHO}$) and Ethanal ($\text{CH}_3\text{CHO}$).
- **Alkanones:** The carbonyl group is in the *middle* of the carbon chain, meaning the carbon atom is attached to two alkyl groups. The simplest example is Propanone ($\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3$), widely used as nail polish remover.

### Differentiating between alkanals and alkanones

Because alkanals have a hydrogen atom attached directly to the carbonyl carbon, they are easily oxidized to alkanoic acids. This makes alkanals **mild reducing agents**. Alkanones cannot be oxidized easily (as that would require breaking strong carbon-carbon bonds) and are not reducing agents. We use mild oxidizing tests to tell them apart:

| Test / Reagent | Result with Alkanals | Result with Alkanones |
|---|---|---|
| **Tollens' reagent** (Ammoniacal silver trioxonitrate(V)) | The alkanal reduces the silver ions ($\text{Ag}^+$) to metallic silver, forming a shiny **silver mirror** on the inside wall of the test tube. | No reaction |
| **Fehling's solution** (Alkaline copper(II) complex) | The alkanal reduces the blue copper(II) ions ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$) to copper(I) oxide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$), forming a **brick-red precipitate**. | No reaction |

> **Common Mistake in Carbonyls:** Students sometimes memorize that both alkanals and alkanones react with Tollens' reagent because they share the carbonyl group. This is false. Only alkanals react with Tollens' and Fehling's reagents.

## Alkanoic acids

Alkanoic acids (carboxylic acids) contain the **carboxyl group** ($-\text{COOH}$). Their general formula for straight-chain aliphatic types is $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{COOH}$. They are weak acids that only partially dissociate into ions in water, turning blue litmus paper red.

### Comparing types of alkanoic acids

Alkanoic acids are categorized based on their structure and the total number of carboxyl groups they possess:

| Type | Description | JAMB Syllabus Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Aliphatic monocarboxylic acid** | An open straight-chain structure containing exactly one $-\text{COOH}$ group. | **Ethanoic acid** ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$): Found in vinegar, used heavily as a food preservative and flavouring. |
| **Aliphatic dicarboxylic acid** | An open chain containing two $-\text{COOH}$ groups. | **Ethanedioic acid** (Oxalic acid, $\text{HOOC-COOH}$): Found in tomatoes and rhubarb; used industrially to bleach rust stains. |
| **Aromatic acid** | The $-\text{COOH}$ group is attached directly to a benzene ring. | **Benzene carboxylic acid** (Benzoic acid, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$): Used widely to preserve fruit juices and canned foods. |

**Chemical reactions of Alkanoic Acids**
Alkanoic acids undergo two major reactions required by JAMB:
1. **Neutralization:** Like inorganic mineral acids, they react with bases (like $\text{NaOH}$ or $\text{KOH}$) to form an organic salt and water.
2. **Esterification:** They react with alkanols to form alkanoates (esters) and water.

**Worked Example 1: Acid-Base Neutralization Calculation**
**Question:** What volume of a $0.1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) solution is required to completely neutralize $4.5 \text{ g}$ of ethanedioic acid ($\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4$)? (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16)
1. Write the balanced chemical equation for this dibasic acid:
   $$ \text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4\text{(aq)} + 2\text{NaOH(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4\text{(aq)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} $$
2. Find the molar mass ($M$) of ethanedioic acid:
   $M = (2 \times 1) + (2 \times 12) + (4 \times 16) = 2 + 24 + 64 = 90 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate the moles ($n$) of the acid using the formula $n = \frac{m}{M}$, where $m$ is mass ($\text{g}$) and $M$ is molar mass ($\text{g mol}^{-1}$):
   $n = \frac{4.5 \text{ g}}{90 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.05 \text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation:
   1 mole of acid requires 2 moles of $\text{NaOH}$. 
   Moles of $\text{NaOH} = 0.05 \text{ mol} \times 2 = 0.1 \text{ mol}$.
5. Calculate the volume ($V$) of the $\text{NaOH}$ solution using $V = \frac{n}{c}$, where $n$ is moles (mol) and $c$ is concentration ($\text{mol dm}^{-3}$):
   $V = \frac{0.1 \text{ mol}}{0.1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}} = 1.0 \text{ dm}^3$ (which is $1000 \text{ cm}^3$).

> **Common Mistake in Alkanoic Acids:** Forgetting that ethanedioic (oxalic) acid has two $-\text{COOH}$ groups (making it dibasic). If you use a 1:1 mole ratio in neutralisation calculations, your final answer will be exactly half of what it should be.

## Alkanoates

Alkanoates (commonly called esters) have the general formula $\text{RCOOR}'$. They are typically volatile liquids characterized by pleasant, sweet, or fruity smells. 

### Natural sources of alkanoates
In nature, simple short-chain alkanoates are found in fruits, giving them their characteristic smells (like the smell of pineapple, apple, or banana).
Crucially, **fats and oils** (such as palm oil, groundnut oil, and animal fat) are naturally occurring large alkanoates. They are esters formed by long-chain alkanoic acids (fatty acids) combining with the polyhydric alkanol, glycerol. 

### Products of alkanoic acids and alkanols

When an alkanoic acid is heated with an alkanol in the presence of a few drops of concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) as a catalyst, an **alkanoate (ester)** and **water** are produced. This condensation process is called **esterification**. It is a reversible reaction. The concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ also acts as a dehydrating agent, removing water to drive the equilibrium forward.

**Equation:**
$$ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH(l)} + \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COOCH}_2\text{CH}_3\text{(l)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} $$
(Ethanoic acid + Ethanol $\rightleftharpoons$ Ethyl ethanoate + Water)

**Worked Example 2: Esterification Calculation**
**Question:** Calculate the mass of ethyl ethanoate formed when $4.6 \text{ g}$ of ethanol reacts completely with excess ethanoic acid, assuming a 100% yield. (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16)
1. Write the balanced equation for the reaction:
   $$ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH(l)} + \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH(l)} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COOCH}_2\text{CH}_3\text{(l)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} $$
2. Find the molar masses ($M$):
   Ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_6\text{O}$) = $(2 \times 12) + (6 \times 1) + (1 \times 16) = 46 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
   Ethyl ethanoate ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_8\text{O}_2$) = $(4 \times 12) + (8 \times 1) + (2 \times 16) = 88 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate moles ($n$) of ethanol using $n = \frac{m}{M}$:
   $n = \frac{4.6 \text{ g}}{46 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.1 \text{ mol}$.
4. Use the mole ratio:
   1 mole of ethanol yields 1 mole of ethyl ethanoate. 
   Therefore, $0.1 \text{ mol}$ of ethanol yields $0.1 \text{ mol}$ of ethyl ethanoate.
5. Convert moles of product to mass ($m$) using $m = n \times M$:
   $m = 0.1 \text{ mol} \times 88 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 8.8 \text{ g}$.

### Methods for the production of soap, detergent and margarine

**1. Saponification (Soap Production)**
Soap is manufactured by heating naturally occurring fats or oils (which are long-chain alkanoates) with a concentrated alkali, usually sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) or potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$). This alkaline hydrolysis reaction is called **saponification**.
$$ \text{Fat or Oil} + 3\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\text{Soap} + \text{Glycerol} $$
After boiling, common salt (sodium chloride, $\text{NaCl}$) is added to the mixture. This decreases the solubility of the soap, causing it to precipitate and float to the top as a solid (a process called *salting out*). The glycerol remains at the bottom in the liquid layer.
Sodium salts produce hard soaps (like laundry bar soap), while potassium salts produce soft soaps (like shampoos and shaving creams).

Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**Worked Example 3: Saponification Mass Calculation**
**Question:** What mass of soap (sodium stearate) is produced when $8.9 \text{ g}$ of the fat tristearin is completely saponified by sodium hydroxide? 
(Molar mass of tristearin = $890 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$; Molar mass of sodium stearate = $306 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$)
1. Understand the reaction mole ratio:
   1 mole of a triglyceride fat (tristearin) reacts with $3 \text{ moles}$ of $\text{NaOH}$ to yield **3 moles of soap**.
2. Calculate the moles ($n$) of tristearin using $n = \frac{m}{M}$:
   $n = \frac{8.9 \text{ g}}{890 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.01 \text{ mol}$.
3. Use the mole ratio to find moles of soap:
   Moles of soap = $0.01 \text{ mol} \times 3 = 0.03 \text{ mol}$.
4. Convert moles of soap to mass ($m$) using $m = n \times M$:
   $m = 0.03 \text{ mol} \times 306 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 9.18 \text{ g}$.

**2. Detergent (Soapless Detergent) Production**
Unlike ordinary soap, detergents are completely synthetic and are manufactured from **petrochemicals** (by-products of petroleum refining). Long-chain hydrocarbons (like alkylbenzenes) are treated with concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) in a process called sulfonation. The product is then neutralized with $\text{NaOH}$ to form the detergent (e.g., sodium alkylbenzene sulfonate).

**3. Margarine Production (Hydrogenation)**
Vegetable oils (like palm or groundnut oil) are liquid at room temperature because their carbon chains contain double bonds (they are unsaturated). Margarine is produced by bubbling hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) through the oil at about $200^\circ\text{C}$ in the presence of a **finely divided nickel catalyst**. The hydrogen atoms add across the double bonds in an addition reaction called hydrogenation, turning the liquid oil into a solid, saturated fat (margarine).

### Distinguishing between detergent and soap

JAMB frequently asks for the differences between these two cleaning agents, particularly regarding how they behave in "hard water" (water containing dissolved calcium, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, or magnesium, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions, such as borehole water).

| Feature | Soaps | Soapless Detergents |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin / Source** | Made from natural fats and oils (plants and animals). | Made from synthetic petrochemicals. |
| **Action in hard water** | Reacts with $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions to form an insoluble white precipitate called **scum**. Wastes soap and dirties clothes. | Does not form scum; forms soluble salts with $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions, lathering easily. |
| **Action in acidic water** | Precipitates out as insoluble fatty acids, losing cleaning power. | Remains soluble and cleans effectively. |
| **Environmental impact** | Completely biodegradable (easily broken down by bacteria in rivers). | Early types were non-biodegradable (causing huge foam buildups in rivers), though modern detergents are mostly biodegradable. |

> **Common Mistake in Alkanoates:** In saponification calculation questions, students frequently forget that 1 mole of fat yields 3 moles of soap, applying a 1:1 ratio instead. Always multiply the moles of fat by 3 to find the moles of soap.

## Amines (Alkanamines)

Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) in which one, two, or all three of the hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl groups. They are basic compounds that turn damp red litmus paper blue and often have a strong "fishy" or ammonia-like smell. 

### Comparing the classes of alkanamines

Amines are classified differently than alkanols. They are classed entirely by **how many alkyl groups are directly attached to the nitrogen ($\text{N}$) atom**, regardless of the shape of the carbon chain.

| Class | Nitrogen attachment | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary ($1^\circ$) amine** | Nitrogen is attached to exactly **one** alkyl group (and two hydrogens). | $\text{R-NH}_2$ | Methylamine ($\text{CH}_3\text{NH}_2$) |
| **Secondary ($2^\circ$) amine** | Nitrogen is attached to exactly **two** alkyl groups (and one hydrogen). | $\text{R}_2\text{NH}$ | Dimethylamine ($(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{NH}$) |
| **Tertiary ($3^\circ$) amine** | Nitrogen is attached to exactly **three** alkyl groups (no hydrogens). | $\text{R}_3\text{N}$ | Trimethylamine ($(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{N}$) |

> **Common Mistake in Amines:** Students often classify amines the same way they classify alkanols (by looking at the carbon atom). For example, they might look at 2-aminopropane ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH(NH}_2\text{)CH}_3$) and call it a secondary amine because the carbon is attached to two other carbons. However, because the nitrogen itself is only attached to ONE carbon group, it is actually a **primary** amine. Always count the carbon groups attached directly to the Nitrogen atom.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mixing up classes in the Lucas test:** Remember that in the Lucas test, the $3^\circ$ alkanol is the **fastest** (immediate turbidity), while $1^\circ$ is the **slowest** (no reaction at room temperature).
- **Alkanals vs Alkanones:** Alkanals (aldehydes) are the ones that react with Tollens' and Fehling's reagents because they are reducing agents. Alkanones (ketones) do not react.
- **Mole ratio in saponification:** Never forget that 1 mole of fat reacts with 3 moles of $\text{NaOH}$ to yield 3 moles of soap. Forgetting this 1:3 ratio ruins calculation questions.
- **Soap vs Detergent source:** Soaps come from natural fats/oils; detergents come from petroleum (petrochemicals). Detergents do not form scum in hard water.
- **Esterification catalyst:** The catalyst for forming an ester is **concentrated** $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, which also acts as a dehydrating agent to pull water out and drive the reversible reaction forward.

## Quick summary

1. Alkanols contain the $-\text{OH}$ group and are classed as primary, secondary, or tertiary. The Lucas test and oxidation reactions easily distinguish them.
2. Ethanol is produced locally by fermenting palm wine or industrially from starchy foods (using diastase, maltase, and zymase) or from crude oil ethene. It is a vital clean-burning alternative fuel (bioethanol).
3. Alkanals and alkanones both contain a carbonyl ($>\text{C=O}$) group. Alkanals can reduce Tollens' and Fehling's solutions; alkanones cannot.
4. Alkanoic acids include aliphatic monocarboxylic (ethanoic acid), aliphatic dicarboxylic (ethanedioic/oxalic acid), and aromatic (benzoic acid).
5. Alkanoic acids react with alkanols via esterification to form sweet-smelling alkanoates (esters) and water.
6. Fats and oils are natural alkanoates. Saponification boils them with concentrated $\text{NaOH}$ to produce soap and glycerol.
7. Soapless detergents are derived from petrochemicals and perform better in hard water because they do not form insoluble scum, unlike ordinary soap.
8. Amines are derivatives of ammonia and are classed as primary, secondary, or tertiary based solely on the number of alkyl groups attached to the nitrogen atom.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following alkanols will produce immediate turbidity when treated with a mixture of anhydrous zinc chloride and concentrated hydrochloric acid?**
- A. Propan-1-ol
- B. Propan-2-ol
- C. 2-methylpropan-2-ol
- D. Ethanol

**Answer: C.** 1. Identify the reagent: The mixture is Lucas reagent, used to distinguish alkanols. 2. **2. A student warms an unknown organic liquid with Fehling's solution, and a brick-red precipitate forms. The liquid is most likely an**

**3. Calculate the mass of ethyl ethanoate formed when $9.2 \text{ g}$ of ethanol reacts completely with excess ethanoic acid, assuming a 100% yield. (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16)**

- B. $12.0 \text{ g}$
- C. $17.6 \text{ g}$

**5. Soapless detergents are preferred to soapy detergents in hard water areas because they**

**7. What mass of soap (sodium stearate) is produced when $0.05 \text{ mol}$ of tristearin is completely saponified by concentrated sodium hydroxide? (Molar mass of sodium stearate = $306 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$)**

**10. What volume of a $0.20 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution of potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$) is required to completely neutralize $0.05 \text{ mol}$ of ethanedioic acid?**
- A. $0.125 \text{ dm}^3$
- B. $0.250 \text{ dm}^3$
- C. $0.500 \text{ dm}^3$
- D. $1.000 \text{ dm}^3$

**Answer: C.** 1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + 2\text{KOH} \rightarrow \text{K}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Ethanedioic acid is dibasic. 2. Note the moles of acid given: $0.05 \text{ mol}$. 3. Use the mole ratio (1:2) to find moles of base: $0.05 \text{ mol} \times 2 = 0.10 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{KOH}$. 4. Calculate the volume: $\text{Volume} = \text{Moles} / \text{Concentration} = 0.10 \text{ mol} / 0.20 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.500 \text{ dm}^3$. 5. The mistake behind option B ($0.250 \text{ dm}^3$) is ignoring the 1:2 reaction ratio and assuming a 1:1 ratio ($0.05 / 0.20 = 0.250$).
