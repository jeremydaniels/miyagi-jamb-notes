## The Kinetic Theory of Matter

The **Kinetic Theory of Matter** is a scientific model that explains the physical properties of matter in terms of the motion of its particles. The basic postulates (assumptions) of the kinetic theory are:
1. All matter is made up of tiny, discrete particles (atoms, molecules, or ions).
2. These particles are in constant, random motion, meaning they possess kinetic energy.
3. The degree of movement and the kinetic energy of the particles depend on the temperature. The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move.
4. There are intermolecular forces of attraction holding the particles together. These forces are strongest in solids and weakest in gases.

### Distinguishing Between Solids, Liquids and Gases

**Objective i: apply the theory to distinguish between solids, liquids and gases.**

The kinetic theory perfectly explains why matter exists in three fundamental physical states. We can distinguish them based on particle arrangement, motion, and forces.

| Property | Solids | Liquids | Gases |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Arrangement of particles** | Tightly packed in a regular, fixed pattern (rigid lattice). | Closely packed but irregularly arranged. | Far apart and completely randomly arranged. |
| **Motion of particles** | Particles cannot move freely; they only **vibrate** about their fixed positions. | Particles can **slide and roll** past one another, moving slowly. | Particles move **freely, rapidly and randomly** in all directions. |
| **Intermolecular forces** | Very strong (strong cohesive forces). | Moderate (weaker than in solids, stronger than in gases). | Very weak (almost negligible). |
| **Kinetic Energy (KE)** | Very low KE. | Moderate KE. | Very high KE. |
| **Shape and Volume** | Definite shape and definite volume. | Definite volume, but takes the shape of the container. | No definite shape and no definite volume (expands to fill the container). |
| **Compressibility** | Incompressible. | Almost incompressible. | Highly compressible. |

### Molecular Motion and Inferences (Brownian Movement)

**Objective iii: draw inferences based on molecular motion.**

The constant, random motion of particles in a fluid (a liquid or gas) is the foundation of the kinetic theory. The most direct evidence for this is **Brownian movement** (or Brownian motion).

**Brownian movement** is the continuous, rapid, zig-zag, and random motion of tiny particles suspended in a fluid. It was first observed in 1827 by the botanist Robert Brown, who noticed under a microscope that pollen grains suspended in water were constantly "dancing" about. 

**Laboratory examples of Brownian movement:**
1. **Pollen grains in water:** Under a microscope, they are seen moving in a random zig-zag path.
2. **The Smoke Cell Experiment:** If smoke is introduced into a small glass cell and viewed under a microscope illuminated by a strong light, tiny bright specks (smoke particles or soot) are seen darting about randomly in the air.

**Inferences drawn from Brownian movement:**
Why do the visible smoke particles or pollen grains dance around? They are being constantly bombarded from all sides by the unseen, fast-moving molecules of the gas (air) or liquid (water). 
- **Inference 1:** Matter is composed of discrete, tiny particles.
- **Inference 2:** These particles are in constant, random, and continuous motion.

Another everyday phenomenon that supports molecular motion is **diffusion** (e.g., the smell of perfume spreading across a room without any breeze).

### Reasons for Change of State

**Objective ii: deduce reasons for change of state.**

A change of state occurs when matter absorbs or loses heat energy. The kinetic theory explains changes of state in terms of changes in the **kinetic energy** (motion) of the particles and the **intermolecular forces** between them.

**1. Melting (Solid to Liquid)**
When a solid is heated, its particles absorb heat energy and convert it into kinetic energy. They vibrate faster and more vigorously. At a certain temperature (the **melting point**), the kinetic energy becomes strong enough to overcome the strong intermolecular forces holding the particles in the rigid lattice. The lattice collapses, and the particles begin to slide over each other. The solid has changed into a liquid.

**2. Boiling (Liquid to Gas)**
When a liquid is heated, its particles gain more kinetic energy and move faster. At a specific temperature (the **boiling point**), the kinetic energy is so high that the particles completely overcome the intermolecular forces holding them in the liquid phase. The particles break entirely free and spread far apart to form a gas. At this point, the vapour pressure of the liquid equals the atmospheric pressure.

**3. Vapourization / Evaporation (Liquid to Gas below boiling point)**
Even without boiling, some particles at the *surface* of a liquid naturally possess higher kinetic energy than others. These fast-moving surface particles can break the intermolecular forces of the surrounding liquid molecules and escape into the air as a vapour. This is evaporation. Heating increases the rate of evaporation because more particles gain enough kinetic energy to escape.

**4. Freezing (Liquid to Solid)**
When a liquid is cooled, it loses heat energy, which means the kinetic energy of the particles decreases. They slow down. As their speed drops, the intermolecular forces of attraction become strong enough to pull the particles back into fixed, rigid positions. At the **freezing point**, the liquid changes into a solid.

**5. Condensation (Gas to Liquid)**
When a gas is cooled, its rapidly moving particles lose kinetic energy and slow down. As they move slower and get closer together, the weak intermolecular forces become significant enough to pull them together, forming drops of liquid.

## The Gas Laws and Properties of Gases

Gases behave in predictable ways depending on four measurable properties: **Volume** ($V$, unit: $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{dm}^3$), **Pressure** ($P$, unit: $\text{atm}$ or $\text{mmHg}$), **Temperature** ($T$, unit: $\text{K}$), and the **number of moles** ($n$, unit: $\text{mol}$). 

**Standard conditions to note:**
- **Temperature:** Must always be measured in **Kelvin (K)** for gas calculations. 
  - To convert Celsius to Kelvin: $\text{K} = ^\circ\text{C} + 273$.
  - Absolute Zero ($0 \text{ K}$ or $-273^\circ\text{C}$) is the theoretical temperature where all molecular motion is assumed to stop, and a gas would have zero volume.
- **S.T.P. (Standard Temperature and Pressure):** 
  - Standard Temperature = $0^\circ\text{C}$ or $273 \text{ K}$.
  - Standard Pressure = $1 \text{ atm}$ or $760 \text{ mmHg}$ or $101325 \text{ N m}^{-2}$.
  - *Textbook note:* While IUPAC now recommends 1 bar for STP, JAMB UTME chemistry questions strictly use $1 \text{ atm}$ ($760 \text{ mmHg}$) and a molar volume of $22.4 \text{ dm}^3$. Always stick to the JAMB standard.

### Deducing Gas Laws from Expressions and Statements

**Objective iv: deduce gas laws from given expressions/ statements.**

You must be able to match the name of each law to its definition (statement) and mathematical expression.

#### 1. Boyle's Law
**Statement:** Boyle’s law states that the volume of a given mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure, provided the temperature remains constant.
**Expression:** $V \propto \frac{1}{P}$ 
**Equation:** $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ (where $P_1, V_1$ are initial conditions, and $P_2, V_2$ are final conditions).

#### 2. Charles's Law
**Statement:** Charles’s law states that the volume of a given mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (in Kelvin), provided the pressure remains constant.
**Expression:** $V \propto T$
**Equation:** $\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2}$

#### 3. The General Gas Equation
By combining Boyle's and Charles's laws, we deduce an equation for a fixed mass of gas undergoing changes in both pressure and temperature.
**Equation:** $\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$

#### 4. The Ideal Gas Equation
By combining Boyle's, Charles's, and Avogadro's laws, we get a universal equation for any amount of gas.
**Equation:** $PV = nRT$
Where $n$ is the number of moles, and $R$ is the universal gas constant. 
- If $P$ is in $\text{atm}$ and $V$ in $\text{dm}^3$, $R = 0.082 \text{ atm dm}^3 \text{ K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$.
- If $P$ is in $\text{N m}^{-2}$ and $V$ in $\text{m}^3$, $R = 8.314 \text{ J K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$.

#### 5. Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes
**Statement:** When gases react, they do so in volumes which bear a simple ratio to one another and to the volumes of the products (if gaseous), provided that temperature and pressure remain constant.
*Example:* $2\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$. The volume ratio is $2:1:2$. If $20 \text{ cm}^3$ of oxygen reacts, it requires $40 \text{ cm}^3$ of hydrogen and forms $40 \text{ cm}^3$ of steam.

#### 6. Avogadro's Law
**Statement:** Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules.
**Expression:** $V \propto n$ (at constant $P$ and $T$).
This leads directly to the concept of **Molar Volume**.

#### 7. Molar Volume and Atomicity of Gases
Because of Avogadro's Law, one mole of ANY gas at S.T.P. occupies exactly the same volume.
- **Molar Volume at S.T.P. = $22.4 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ mol}^{-1}$** (or $22,400 \text{ cm}^3 \text{ mol}^{-1}$).

Avogadro's law was essential for deducing the **atomicity of gases**. Atomicity is the number of atoms in one molecule of an element. Atomicity has no unit.
- **Monatomic (1 atom):** Noble gases like Helium ($\text{He}$) and Neon ($\text{Ne}$).
- **Diatomic (2 atoms):** Hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$), Nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$), Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$), Chlorine ($\text{Cl}_2$).
- **Triatomic (3 atoms):** Ozone ($\text{O}_3$).

#### 8. Dalton's Law of Partial Pressure
**Statement:** In a mixture of non-reacting gases, the total pressure exerted is equal to the sum of the partial pressures of the individual gases.
**Equation:** $P_{\text{total}} = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \dots$
**Application in the lab:** When a gas is collected over water, it is mixed with water vapour. The dry gas pressure is found by subtracting the water's vapour pressure (aqueous tension) from the total pressure.
$P_{\text{dry gas}} = P_{\text{total}} - P_{\text{water vapour}}$

#### 9. Graham's Law of Diffusion
**Statement:** The rate of diffusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its density (or relative molecular mass), provided temperature and pressure are constant.
**Expression:** $R \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}$ or $R \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}$
**Equation:** $\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}}$
Since rate is volume over time ($R = \frac{V}{t}$), if equal volumes are diffusing, the *time* taken is directly proportional to the square root of the molecular mass:
$\frac{t_1}{t_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_2}}$
*Note:* Lighter gases diffuse faster than heavier gases. A classic textbook example: Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$, $M=17$) diffuses faster than Hydrogen chloride ($\text{HCl}$, $M=36.5$). If released at opposite ends of a glass tube, a white ring of ammonium chloride forms closer to the $\text{HCl}$ end.

#### 10. Relationship Between Vapour Density and Relative Molecular Mass
**Vapour Density (VD)** is the mass of a given volume of gas divided by the mass of an equal volume of hydrogen at the same temperature and pressure. Since hydrogen is a diatomic molecule ($\text{H}_2$) with a mass of 2:
**Equation:** $\text{Relative Molecular Mass (RMM)} = 2 \times \text{Vapour Density}$
Note that Vapour Density and Relative Molecular Mass are both ratios, so they have no unit.

### Performing Calculations Involving Gas Laws

**Objective vi: perform simple calculations based on these laws, equations and relationships.**

Calculations in JAMB require careful unit conversions (always use Kelvin for temperature!) and selecting the right formula. Follow these worked examples.

**Worked Example 1: Boyle’s Law**
1. **Question:** A given mass of gas occupies a volume of $400 \text{ cm}^3$ at a pressure of $760 \text{ mmHg}$. What volume will it occupy at $380 \text{ mmHg}$ if the temperature remains constant?
2. **Identify the formula:** Constant temperature means Boyle's Law: $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$.
3. **List variables:** $V_1 = 400 \text{ cm}^3$, $P_1 = 760 \text{ mmHg}$, $P_2 = 380 \text{ mmHg}$.
4. **Substitute and solve:** 
   $760 \text{ mmHg} \times 400 \text{ cm}^3 = 380 \text{ mmHg} \times V_2$
   $V_2 = \frac{760 \times 400}{380} \text{ cm}^3 = 2 \times 400 \text{ cm}^3 = 800 \text{ cm}^3$.

**Worked Example 2: Charles's Law**
1. **Question:** A balloon contains $250 \text{ cm}^3$ of air at $27^\circ\text{C}$. If it is heated in the sun to $87^\circ\text{C}$ at constant pressure, what is its new volume?
2. **Convert temperatures to Kelvin (Crucial step!):** 
   $T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300 \text{ K}$
   $T_2 = 87 + 273 = 360 \text{ K}$
3. **Identify the formula:** Charles's Law: $\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2}$.
4. **Substitute and solve:** 
   $\frac{250 \text{ cm}^3}{300 \text{ K}} = \frac{V_2}{360 \text{ K}}$
   $V_2 = \frac{250 \times 360}{300} \text{ cm}^3 = 300 \text{ cm}^3$.

**Worked Example 3: General Gas Equation**
1. **Question:** A certain mass of gas occupies $300 \text{ cm}^3$ at $27^\circ\text{C}$ and $800 \text{ mmHg}$ pressure. Calculate its volume at S.T.P.
2. **Recall S.T.P. values:** $T_2 = 273 \text{ K}$, $P_2 = 760 \text{ mmHg}$.
3. **Convert initial temperature:** $T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300 \text{ K}$.
4. **Identify formula:** $\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$
5. **Substitute and solve:**
   $\frac{800 \text{ mmHg} \times 300 \text{ cm}^3}{300 \text{ K}} = \frac{760 \text{ mmHg} \times V_2}{273 \text{ K}}$
   $800 = \frac{760 \times V_2}{273}$
   $V_2 = \frac{800 \times 273}{760} \text{ cm}^3 = 287.37 \text{ cm}^3$.

**Worked Example 4: Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes**
1. **Question:** $20 \text{ cm}^3$ of methane ($\text{CH}_4$) was sparked with $60 \text{ cm}^3$ of oxygen to produce carbon (IV) oxide and liquid water. What is the total volume of the resulting gases?
2. **Write the balanced chemical equation:**
   $\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + 2\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$
3. **Identify the volume ratio:** 1 volume of $\text{CH}_4$ reacts with 2 volumes of $\text{O}_2$ to produce 1 volume of $\text{CO}_2$. (Water is liquid, so its gaseous volume is negligible).
4. **Determine the reacting volumes:** 
   $20 \text{ cm}^3$ of $\text{CH}_4$ will require $2 \times 20 = 40 \text{ cm}^3$ of $\text{O}_2$.
   $20 \text{ cm}^3$ of $\text{CH}_4$ will produce $1 \times 20 = 20 \text{ cm}^3$ of $\text{CO}_2$.
5. **Calculate excess and total volume:**
   Excess oxygen = Initial volume - Reacted volume = $60 \text{ cm}^3 - 40 \text{ cm}^3 = 20 \text{ cm}^3$.
   Total gas volume left = Volume of $\text{CO}_2$ produced + Volume of excess $\text{O}_2$.
   Total = $20 \text{ cm}^3 + 20 \text{ cm}^3 = 40 \text{ cm}^3$.

**Worked Example 5: Dalton's Law of Partial Pressure**
1. **Question:** A mixture of gases contains $2.0 \text{ mol}$ of Nitrogen, $3.0 \text{ mol}$ of Oxygen, and $1.0 \text{ mol}$ of Carbon (IV) oxide. If the total pressure of the mixture is $1200 \text{ mmHg}$, calculate the partial pressure of Oxygen.
2. **Calculate total moles:** $n_{\text{total}} = 2.0 + 3.0 + 1.0 = 6.0 \text{ mol}$.
3. **Calculate mole fraction of Oxygen (no unit):** $X_{\text{O}_2} = \frac{n_{\text{O}_2}}{n_{\text{total}}} = \frac{3.0}{6.0} = 0.5$.
4. **Calculate partial pressure:** $P_{\text{O}_2} = X_{\text{O}_2} \times P_{\text{total}} = 0.5 \times 1200 \text{ mmHg} = 600 \text{ mmHg}$.

**Worked Example 6: Graham's Law of Diffusion**
1. **Question:** $50 \text{ cm}^3$ of hydrogen gas diffuses through a porous plug in $10 \text{ s}$. How long will it take $50 \text{ cm}^3$ of oxygen to diffuse through the same plug under the same conditions? ($\text{H}=1, \text{O}=16$).
2. **Find Relative Molecular Mass (no unit):** $\text{H}_2 = 1 \times 2 = 2$. $\text{O}_2 = 16 \times 2 = 32$.
3. **Identify formula for time:** $\frac{t_{\text{O}_2}}{t_{\text{H}_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{M_{\text{O}_2}}{M_{\text{H}_2}}}$
4. **Substitute and solve:**
   $\frac{t_{\text{O}_2}}{10 \text{ s}} = \sqrt{\frac{32}{2}} = \sqrt{16} = 4$
   $t_{\text{O}_2} = 4 \times 10 \text{ s} = 40 \text{ s}$.

**Worked Example 7: The Ideal Gas Equation**
1. **Question:** Find the volume occupied by $2.0 \text{ mol}$ of an ideal gas at $0^\circ\text{C}$ and $2.0 \text{ atm}$ pressure. ($R = 0.082 \text{ atm dm}^3 \text{ K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$)
2. **Convert temperature to Kelvin:** $T = 0 + 273 = 273 \text{ K}$.
3. **Identify formula:** $PV = nRT$.
4. **Rearrange for Volume:** $V = \frac{nRT}{P}$
5. **Substitute and solve:**
   $V = \frac{2.0 \text{ mol} \times 0.082 \text{ atm dm}^3 \text{ K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1} \times 273 \text{ K}}{2.0 \text{ atm}} = 22.386 \text{ dm}^3$.

**Worked Example 8: Vapour Density**
1. **Question:** A gas has a vapour density of 22 and an empirical formula of $\text{N}_2\text{O}$. Determine its molecular formula. ($\text{N}=14, \text{O}=16$)
2. **Calculate Relative Molecular Mass (RMM, no unit):** $RMM = 2 \times \text{VD} = 2 \times 22 = 44$.
3. **Calculate empirical formula mass (no unit):** For $\text{N}_2\text{O} = (14 \times 2) + 16 = 28 + 16 = 44$.
4. **Find the ratio ($n$, no unit):** $n = \frac{RMM}{\text{Empirical Mass}} = \frac{44}{44} = 1$.
5. **Determine molecular formula:** $(\text{N}_2\text{O})_1 = \text{N}_2\text{O}$.

### Ideal and Real Gases: Deviations from Ideal Behaviour

**Objective vii: state factors responsible for the deviation of real gases from ideal situations.**

An **ideal gas** is a theoretical gas that perfectly obeys all the gas laws (and $PV = nRT$) under *all* conditions of temperature and pressure. In reality, no such gas exists. All gases are **real gases** (like $\text{O}_2$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{NH}_3$).

**Why do real gases deviate from ideal behaviour?**
The kinetic theory makes two assumptions that are only strictly true for ideal gases:
1. **Assumption 1:** The volume occupied by the gas molecules themselves is negligible compared to the total volume of the container. 
2. **Assumption 2:** There are no intermolecular forces of attraction between gas molecules.

Real gases deviate from these assumptions under two specific conditions: **High Pressure** and **Low Temperature**.

- **At High Pressure:** The gas molecules are squashed very close together. The space between them decreases so much that the actual volume of the molecules is no longer negligible compared to the volume of the container.
- **At Low Temperature:** The gas molecules move very slowly because their kinetic energy is low. As they move slowly past one another, the weak intermolecular forces of attraction (van der Waals forces) take effect and pull the molecules together. If cooled enough, the gas will deviate so much that it turns into a liquid (condensation).

**When do real gases behave most like ideal gases?**
To make a real gas behave almost perfectly like an ideal gas, you must ensure the molecules are far apart and moving fast. Therefore, real gases approach ideal behaviour at **low pressure** and **high temperature**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Kinetic Theory** | A theory explaining the states of matter based on the continuous random motion of particles. |
| **Brownian Movement** | The continuous, rapid, zig-zag motion of suspended particles caused by bombardment from fluid molecules. |
| **Ideal Gas** | A hypothetical gas that perfectly obeys $PV=nRT$ at all temperatures and pressures. |
| **Real Gas** | A gas that exists in nature and deviates from ideal gas laws at high pressure and low temperature. |
| **Absolute Zero** | $-273^\circ\text{C}$ or $0 \text{ K}$; the temperature at which all molecular motion theoretically stops and gas volume becomes zero. |

| **Atomicity** | The number of atoms present in a single molecule of an element. |
| **Vapour Density** | Half the relative molecular mass of a gas. |
| **Partial Pressure** | The pressure that one individual gas in a mixture would exert if it occupied the entire volume alone. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Temperature units:** The most common mistake in JAMB gas law calculations is forgetting to convert Celsius to Kelvin. Always add 273 to $^\circ\text{C}$ before multiplying or dividing!
- **Graham's Law trap:** Remember the square root! A gas that is 4 times heavier does *not* diffuse 4 times slower; it diffuses $\sqrt{4} = 2$ times slower.
- **Ideal gas conditions:** JAMB frequently tests this theory. Remember the rule: Real gases behave ideally at **High Temperature and Low Pressure**. (Think: HTLP).
- **Vapour density relationship:** $\text{RMM} = 2 \times \text{VD}$. Don't divide by 2 when you are supposed to multiply. 
- **Water vapour pressure:** If a question says a gas is "collected over water" and gives the saturated vapour pressure of water, you *must* subtract that vapour pressure from the total pressure to find the pressure of the dry gas before applying Boyle's or Charles's law.
- **Gay-Lussac volumes:** Water is a liquid at room temperature. If an equation produces $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and asks for the total volume of "resulting gases" at room temperature, do not count the volume of the water!

## Quick summary

1. The kinetic theory of matter states that all matter is made of particles in constant random motion, with the highest kinetic energy in gases and the lowest in solids.
2. Brownian movement (e.g., pollen grains in water, smoke in air) is the physical evidence for the continuous, random motion of invisible particles.
3. Changes of state (melting, boiling, freezing, condensation) occur because adding or removing heat changes the kinetic energy of particles, altering their ability to overcome intermolecular forces.
4. Gas laws describe the relationships between Pressure, Volume, and Temperature: Boyle ($P \propto \frac{1}{V}$), Charles ($V \propto T$), and General ($\frac{PV}{T} = \text{constant}$).
5. Avogadro’s law leads to the molar volume of gases ($22.4 \text{ dm}^3$ at S.T.P.).
6. Lighter gases diffuse faster than heavier gases. The time taken to diffuse is directly proportional to the square root of the relative molecular mass (Graham's Law).
7. Real gases deviate from the ideal gas equation ($PV=nRT$) at **high pressures** and **low temperatures** because gas molecules have actual volume and experience intermolecular attraction.

## Practice questions

**1. A given mass of gas occupies a volume of $200 \text{ cm}^3$ at a pressure of $1.5 \text{ atm}$. What volume will it occupy at $3.0 \text{ atm}$ if the temperature remains constant?**
- A. $400 \text{ cm}^3$
- B. $300 \text{ cm}^3$
- C. $150 \text{ cm}^3$
- D. $100 \text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Identify the formula for constant temperature (Boyle's law): $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$.
2. Substitute the given values: $1.5 \text{ atm} \times 200 \text{ cm}^3 = 3.0 \text{ atm} \times V_2$.
3. Solve for $V_2$: $V_2 = \frac{1.5 \times 200}{3.0} \text{ cm}^3 = 100 \text{ cm}^3$.
Option A comes from mistakenly multiplying the initial volume by the pressure ratio instead of dividing ($3.0 \times 200 / 1.5 = 400 \text{ cm}^3$).

**2. The rate of diffusion of gas X is twice that of gas Y. If the relative molecular mass of Y is 64, what is the relative molecular mass of X? (Relative molecular mass has no unit)**
- A. 32
- B. 128
- C. 16
- D. 256

**Answer: C.** 
1. Identify Graham's Law of diffusion: $\frac{R_X}{R_Y} = \sqrt{\frac{M_Y}{M_X}}$.
2. Substitute the rate ratio (2) and the mass of Y (64): $2 = \sqrt{\frac{64}{M_X}}$.
3. Square both sides to remove the square root: $4 = \frac{64}{M_X}$.
4. Solve for $M_X$: $M_X = \frac{64}{4} = 16$.
Option A comes from forgetting to square the rate ratio before dividing ($64 / 2 = 32$).

**3. What is the volume occupied by $0.25 \text{ mol}$ of an ideal gas at a pressure of $0.82 \text{ atm}$ and a temperature of $127^\circ\text{C}$? ($R = 0.082 \text{ atm dm}^3 \text{ K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$)**
- A. $10.0 \text{ dm}^3$
- B. $3.2 \text{ dm}^3$
- C. $22.4 \text{ dm}^3$
- D. $5.0 \text{ dm}^3$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Convert the temperature to Kelvin: $T = 127 + 273 = 400 \text{ K}$.
2. Identify the ideal gas equation: $PV = nRT$.
3. Rearrange for volume: $V = \frac{nRT}{P}$.
4. Substitute values: $V = \frac{0.25 \text{ mol} \times 0.082 \text{ atm dm}^3 \text{ K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1} \times 400 \text{ K}}{0.82 \text{ atm}}$.
5. Solve: $V = \frac{8.2}{0.82} \text{ dm}^3 = 10.0 \text{ dm}^3$.
Option B comes from mistakenly using the Celsius temperature directly in the equation ($V = \frac{0.25 \times 0.082 \times 127}{0.82} = 3.175 \text{ dm}^3$).

**4. $10 \text{ cm}^3$ of ethene ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$) is burnt in $50 \text{ cm}^3$ of oxygen. What is the total volume of gases left at room temperature?**
**Equation: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{(g)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$**
- A. $20 \text{ cm}^3$
- B. $60 \text{ cm}^3$
- C. $50 \text{ cm}^3$
- D. $40 \text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Note the Gay-Lussac volume ratio: $1$ vol of $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$ requires $3$ vols of $\text{O}_2$ and produces $2$ vols of $\text{CO}_2$.
2. Calculate the required $\text{O}_2$: $10 \text{ cm}^3 \times 3 = 30 \text{ cm}^3$.
3. Calculate the excess $\text{O}_2$: $50 \text{ cm}^3 - 30 \text{ cm}^3 = 20 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. Calculate the produced $\text{CO}_2$: $10 \text{ cm}^3 \times 2 = 20 \text{ cm}^3$.
5. Add the remaining gases: Excess $\text{O}_2$ ($20 \text{ cm}^3$) + produced $\text{CO}_2$ ($20 \text{ cm}^3$) = $40 \text{ cm}^3$.
Option B comes from mistakenly adding the volume of the produced water ($20 \text{ cm}^3$) to the total, forgetting that water is a liquid at room temperature and contributes negligible gas volume ($40 + 20 = 60 \text{ cm}^3$).

**5. A gas occupies $300 \text{ cm}^3$ at $27^\circ\text{C}$. What is its volume at $127^\circ\text{C}$ at constant pressure?**
- A. $1411 \text{ cm}^3$
- B. $225 \text{ cm}^3$
- C. $600 \text{ cm}^3$
- D. $400 \text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Convert temperatures to Kelvin: $T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300 \text{ K}$, $T_2 = 127 + 273 = 400 \text{ K}$.
2. Identify Charles's law formula for constant pressure: $\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2}$.
3. Substitute the values: $\frac{300 \text{ cm}^3}{300 \text{ K}} = \frac{V_2}{400 \text{ K}}$.
4. Solve for $V_2$: $V_2 = \frac{300 \times 400}{300} \text{ cm}^3 = 400 \text{ cm}^3$.
Option A comes from the common mistake of substituting the Celsius temperatures directly into the formula ($300 \times 127 / 27 = 1411.11 \text{ cm}^3$).

**6. A gas has an empirical formula of $\text{CH}_2$. If its vapour density is 28, what is its molecular formula? ($\text{C}=12, \text{H}=1$)**
- A. $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$
- B. $\text{C}_3\text{H}_6$
- C. $\text{C}_4\text{H}_8$
- D. $\text{CH}_2$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Use the relationship between relative molecular mass (RMM) and vapour density (VD): $\text{RMM} = 2 \times \text{VD}$.
2. Calculate RMM: $\text{RMM} = 2 \times 28 = 56$.
3. Calculate the empirical formula mass of $\text{CH}_2$: $12 + (1 \times 2) = 14$.
4. Find the multiplier $n$: $n = \frac{56}{14} = 4$.
5. Determine the molecular formula: $(\text{CH}_2)_4 = \text{C}_4\text{H}_8$.
Option A comes from forgetting to multiply the vapour density by 2, leading to $n = \frac{28}{14} = 2$.

**7. A gas is collected over water at a total pressure of $760 \text{ mmHg}$. If the saturated vapour pressure of water at that temperature is $15 \text{ mmHg}$, what is the pressure of the dry gas?**
- A. $775 \text{ mmHg}$
- B. $760 \text{ mmHg}$
- C. $15 \text{ mmHg}$
- D. $745 \text{ mmHg}$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Identify Dalton's Law of Partial Pressure: $P_{\text{total}} = P_{\text{dry gas}} + P_{\text{water vapour}}$.
2. Substitute the known pressures: $760 \text{ mmHg} = P_{\text{dry gas}} + 15 \text{ mmHg}$.
3. Solve for dry gas pressure: $P_{\text{dry gas}} = 760 \text{ mmHg} - 15 \text{ mmHg} = 745 \text{ mmHg}$.
Option A comes from mistakenly adding the saturated vapour pressure to the total pressure instead of subtracting it ($760 + 15 = 775 \text{ mmHg}$).

**8. How many moles of a gas occupy $11.2 \text{ dm}^3$ at standard temperature and pressure (S.T.P)? (Molar volume at S.T.P = $22.4 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ mol}^{-1}$)**
- A. $2.0 \text{ mol}$
- B. $1.0 \text{ mol}$
- C. $0.25 \text{ mol}$
- D. $0.50 \text{ mol}$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Recall the relationship between volume, molar volume, and moles: $n = \frac{V}{V_m}$.
2. Substitute the values: $n = \frac{11.2 \text{ dm}^3}{22.4 \text{ dm}^3 \text{ mol}^{-1}}$.
3. Solve for $n$: $n = 0.50 \text{ mol}$.
Option A comes from incorrectly dividing the molar volume by the given volume ($22.4 / 11.2 = 2.0 \text{ mol}$).

**9. A certain mass of gas occupies $50 \text{ cm}^3$ at $2.0 \text{ atm}$ and $300 \text{ K}$. What volume will it occupy at $1.0 \text{ atm}$ and $600 \text{ K}$?**
- A. $100 \text{ cm}^3$
- B. $50 \text{ cm}^3$
- C. $25 \text{ cm}^3$
- D. $200 \text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Identify the General Gas Equation: $\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$.
2. List the variables: $P_1 = 2.0 \text{ atm}$, $V_1 = 50 \text{ cm}^3$, $T_1 = 300 \text{ K}$, $P_2 = 1.0 \text{ atm}$, $T_2 = 600 \text{ K}$.
3. Rearrange for $V_2$: $V_2 = \frac{P_1 \times V_1 \times T_2}{P_2 \times T_1}$.
4. Substitute and solve: $V_2 = \frac{2.0 \times 50 \times 600}{1.0 \times 300} \text{ cm}^3 = \frac{60000}{300} \text{ cm}^3 = 200 \text{ cm}^3$.
Option B comes from mistakenly using an inverse temperature relationship instead of direct ($\frac{2.0 \times 50 \times 300}{1.0 \times 600} = 50 \text{ cm}^3$).

**10. It takes $20 \text{ s}$ for a certain volume of oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$, $M=32$) to diffuse through a porous plug. How long will it take the same volume of hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$, $M=2$) to diffuse under the same conditions?**
- A. $80 \text{ s}$
- B. $40 \text{ s}$
- C. $5 \text{ s}$
- D. $1.25 \text{ s}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Identify Graham's law formula for time: $\frac{t_{\text{H}_2}}{t_{\text{O}_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{M_{\text{H}_2}}{M_{\text{O}_2}}}$.
2. Substitute the values: $\frac{t_{\text{H}_2}}{20 \text{ s}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{32}}$.
3. Simplify the square root: $\sqrt{\frac{2}{32}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{16}} = \frac{1}{4}$.
4. Solve for $t_{\text{H}_2}$: $t_{\text{H}_2} = 20 \text{ s} \times \frac{1}{4} = 5 \text{ s}$.
Option A comes from incorrectly inverting the mass ratio inside the square root ($\sqrt{\frac{32}{2}} = 4$, making $t = 20 \times 4 = 80 \text{ s}$).
