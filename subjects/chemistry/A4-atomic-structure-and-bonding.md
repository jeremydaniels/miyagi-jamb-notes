## The concept of atoms and atomic structure

### Objective i: Distinguish between atoms, molecules and ions

All matter is made up of tiny, invisible particles. The three fundamental particles of matter are atoms, molecules, and ions.

| Particle | Definition | Independence | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Atom** | The smallest part of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction. | Most atoms cannot exist independently (except noble gases like $\text{He}$, $\text{Ne}$). | $\text{Na}$, $\text{C}$, $\text{Fe}$, $\text{O}$ |
| **Molecule** | The smallest part of a substance (element or compound) that can normally exist alone and still retain the chemical properties of that substance. | Can exist independently. | $\text{H}_2$, $\text{O}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}_2$ |
| **Ion** | An atom or group of atoms that possesses an electrical charge. Positive ions are cations; negative ions are anions. | Cannot exist independently without an opposite charge nearby. | $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ |

### Objective ii: Identify the contributions of scientists to the development of the atomic structure

The modern understanding of the atom was built over more than a century through the experiments of several brilliant scientists.

1. **John Dalton (1808):** Proposed the first modern **Atomic Theory**. He described atoms as solid, indivisible, and indestructible spheres. He stated that all atoms of an element are exactly alike, but differ from atoms of other elements.
2. **J.J. Thomson (1897):** Discovered the **electron** using a cathode-ray tube. Since atoms are neutral, he proposed the "plum pudding" model: a solid sphere of positive charge with negatively charged electrons embedded in it like plums in a pudding. *(Remember, JAMB sometimes spells his name "Thompson").*
3. **Robert Millikan (1909):** Conducted the famous **oil-drop experiment** to determine the exact electrical charge of an electron (unit: Coulombs, $\text{C}$).
4. **Ernest Rutherford (1911):** Discovered the **atomic nucleus** through his gold-foil experiment. By firing positively charged alpha particles at a thin gold foil, he found that a few bounced back. He concluded that an atom is mostly empty space, with a dense, tiny, positively charged nucleus at the centre containing most of the mass.
5. **Niels Bohr (1913):** Proposed the **planetary model** of the atom. He suggested that electrons do not spiral into the nucleus but move around it in fixed circular paths called **energy levels** or **orbits** (shells). 
6. **Henry Moseley (1913):** Discovered the **atomic number** of elements using X-ray spectra. He proved that the identity of an element depends strictly on the number of protons in its nucleus, leading to the modern periodic table.

### Objective iii: Deduce the number of protons, neutrons and electrons

Atoms are made of three main subatomic particles: **protons** (positively charged, in the nucleus), **neutrons** (no charge, in the nucleus), and **electrons** (negatively charged, outside the nucleus).

Two vital numbers describe an atom (both are dimensionless counts):
- **Atomic Number ($Z$):** The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It defines the element.
- **Mass Number ($A$):** The sum of the number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom (also called nucleons).

From these definitions, we can deduce the number of particles:
- **Number of Protons** = Atomic Number ($Z$)
- **Number of Neutrons** = Mass Number ($A$) - Atomic Number ($Z$)
- **Number of Electrons in a neutral atom** = Number of Protons ($Z$)
- **Number of Electrons in an ion** = Number of Protons - (Charge of the ion)

**Notation:** An element $X$ is written as $^{A}_{Z}X$, where $A$ is the mass number and $Z$ is the atomic number.

**Worked Example 1:**
Find the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in a neutral sodium atom, $^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$.
1. **Identify $Z$ and $A$:** The atomic number $Z = 11$, and the mass number $A = 23$.
2. **Find Protons:** Protons = $Z = 11$.
3. **Find Neutrons:** Neutrons = $A - Z = 23 - 11 = 12$.
4. **Find Electrons:** Since the atom is neutral, Electrons = Protons = $11$.
**Answer:** The neutral sodium atom has $11$ protons, $12$ neutrons, and $11$ electrons.

**Worked Example 2:**
Find the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in an oxide ion, $^{16}_{8}\text{O}^{2-}$.
1. **Identify $Z$ and $A$:** The atomic number $Z = 8$, and the mass number $A = 16$.
2. **Find Protons:** Protons = $Z = 8$.
3. **Find Neutrons:** Neutrons = $A - Z = 16 - 8 = 8$.
4. **Find Electrons:** Electrons = Protons - Charge = $8 - (-2) = 10$.
**Answer:** The oxide ion has $8$ protons, $8$ neutrons, and $10$ electrons.

### Quantum numbers and atomic orbitals

While Bohr pictured electrons in flat circular orbits, modern quantum mechanics describes electrons in 3D regions of space called **orbitals**—regions where there is a high probability of finding an electron. Every electron in an atom is described by a unique set of four **quantum numbers** (all dimensionless values):

1. **Principal Quantum Number ($n$):** Represents the main energy level or shell. Values are $n = 1, 2, 3, 4\dots$ (corresponding to K, L, M, N shells). As $n$ increases, the shell is further from the nucleus and has higher energy.
2. **Azimuthal / Angular Momentum Quantum Number ($l$):** Describes the subshell and the **shape** of the orbital. Its values range from $0$ to $(n-1)$.
   - $l = 0$ is an **$s$** subshell.
   - $l = 1$ is a **$p$** subshell.
   - $l = 2$ is a **$d$** subshell.
   - $l = 3$ is an **$f$** subshell.
3. **Magnetic Quantum Number ($m_l$):** Determines the spatial orientation of the orbital. Values range from $-l$ to $+l$.
4. **Spin Quantum Number ($m_s$):** Indicates the direction of the electron's spin. An electron can spin clockwise ($+1/2$) or anti-clockwise ($-1/2$).

### Objective viii: Differentiate between the shapes of the orbitals

Different subshells have different orbital shapes.
- **$s$-orbital:** It has a **spherical** shape. The nucleus is at the centre of the sphere.
- **$p$-orbital:** It has a **dumb-bell** shape (two lobes meeting at a node at the nucleus). Because of the magnetic quantum number, there are three $p$-orbitals pointing along the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes: $p_x$, $p_y$, and $p_z$.

### Objective ix: Determine the number of electrons in s and p atomic orbitals

Each individual orbital can hold a maximum of **$2$ electrons** (with opposite spins).
- **$s$ subshell:** Contains $1$ spherical orbital. Maximum capacity = **$2$ electrons**.
- **$p$ subshell:** Contains $3$ dumb-bell orbitals ($p_x, p_y, p_z$). Each holds $2$ electrons. Maximum capacity = $3 \times 2 =$ **$6$ electrons**.
*(For completeness, the $d$ subshell holds $10$ electrons, and the $f$ subshell holds $14$, but JAMB specifically tests $s$ and $p$).*

### Objective iv: Apply the rules guiding the arrangement of electrons in an atom

Electron configuration is the arrangement of electrons in the orbitals of an atom. It is governed by three fundamental rules:

1. **The Aufbau Principle:** Electrons fill orbitals of the lowest energy first before occupying higher energy levels. The order of filling is: 
   $$1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d$$
   *(Note: The $4s$ orbital is slightly lower in energy than the $3d$ orbital, so $4s$ fills first).*
2. **The Pauli Exclusion Principle:** No two electrons in the same atom can have the exact same set of four quantum numbers. Practically, this means an orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons, and they must have opposite spins.
3. **Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity:** When degenerate orbitals (orbitals of the same energy, like $p_x, p_y, p_z$) are available, electrons fill them singly with parallel spins before they start to pair up. This minimizes repulsion.

**Examples of Electron Configurations (Atomic numbers 1 to 20):**
- **Carbon (Atomic No. 6):** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$
- **Oxygen (Atomic No. 8):** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$. Following Hund's rule, the $2p$ subshell looks like: $[ \uparrow\downarrow ]_{p_x} \, [ \uparrow ]_{p_y} \, [ \uparrow ]_{p_z}$
- **Sodium (Atomic No. 11):** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$
- **Calcium (Atomic No. 20):** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2$

## Isotopy and mass number

### Objective v: Identify common elements exhibiting isotopy

**Isotopy** is a phenomenon whereby atoms of the **same element** have the **same atomic number** but **different mass numbers**. These differing atoms are called **isotopes**.

In nature, many elements exist as a mixture of isotopes. Common examples include:
- **Hydrogen:** Has three isotopes: Protium ($^{1}\text{H}$), Deuterium ($^{2}\text{H}$), and Tritium ($^{3}\text{H}$, radioactive).
- **Carbon:** $^{12}\text{C}$, $^{13}\text{C}$, and $^{14}\text{C}$ (used for radiocarbon dating, e.g., on the ancient Nok terracotta figures in Nigeria).
- **Oxygen:** $^{16}\text{O}$, $^{17}\text{O}$, and $^{18}\text{O}$.
- **Chlorine:** $^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $^{37}\text{Cl}$.

### Objective vi: Relate isotopy to mass number

Isotopes of an element have the exact same number of protons and electrons, which is why they have **identical chemical properties** (chemical reactions depend on electrons). 

However, they have different mass numbers because they have a **different number of neutrons** in their nuclei. This difference in mass gives them slightly different **physical properties** (like density and diffusion rates). Because the element occurs as a mixture of these different masses, the atomic mass of the element as found in nature is usually not a whole number.

### Objective vii: Perform simple calculations relating to isotopy

The **Relative Atomic Mass (RAM)** of an element is the weighted average of the masses of its isotopes, taking into account their percentage abundances. As a ratio, RAM is a dimensionless value (it has no units).

**Steps for calculation:**
1. State the mass and percentage abundance of each isotope.
2. Convert each percentage abundance to a fraction (divide by $100$).
3. Multiply the mass of each isotope by its fractional abundance.
4. Add the resulting values together to find the relative atomic mass.

**Worked Example 1: Calculating RAM from abundances**
Naturally occurring chlorine consists of $75\%$ $^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $25\%$ $^{37}\text{Cl}$. Calculate its relative atomic mass.
1. **Identify isotopes:** Isotope 1: mass $= 35$, abundance $= 75\%$. Isotope 2: mass $= 37$, abundance $= 25\%$.
2. **Convert to fractions:** $75\% = 0.75$, and $25\% = 0.25$.
3. **Multiply mass by fraction:** 
   - $35 \times 0.75 = 26.25$
   - $37 \times 0.25 = 9.25$
4. **Add values:** $26.25 + 9.25 = 35.50$.
**Answer:** The relative atomic mass of chlorine is $35.5$ (no unit).

**Worked Example 2: Calculating abundances from RAM**
Neon has two main isotopes, $^{20}\text{Ne}$ and $^{22}\text{Ne}$. If the relative atomic mass of neon is $20.2$, calculate the percentage abundance of each isotope.
1. **Assign variables:** Let the percentage abundance of $^{20}\text{Ne}$ be $x\%$.
2. **Express the second variable:** The percentage abundance of $^{22}\text{Ne}$ will be $(100 - x)\%$.
3. **Set up the RAM equation:**
   $$20 \times \frac{x}{100} + 22 \times \frac{(100 - x)}{100} = 20.2$$
4. **Multiply through by $100$:**
   $$20x + 22(100 - x) = 2020$$
5. **Expand and solve for $x$:**
   $$20x + 2200 - 22x = 2020$$
   $$-2x = 2020 - 2200$$
   $$-2x = -180$$
   $$x = 90$$
6. **State final answer:** The abundance of $^{20}\text{Ne}$ is $90\%$, and the abundance of $^{22}\text{Ne}$ is $(100 - 90)\% = 10\%$.

**Worked Example 3: Three isotopes**
An element M has three isotopes with masses $24$, $25$, and $26$. Their percentage abundances are $79\%$, $10\%$, and $11\%$ respectively. Calculate the relative atomic mass of M.
1. **Convert to fractions:** Mass $24$ ($0.79$), Mass $25$ ($0.10$), Mass $26$ ($0.11$).
2. **Multiply mass by fraction:**
   - $24 \times 0.79 = 18.96$
   - $25 \times 0.10 = 2.50$
   - $26 \times 0.11 = 2.86$
3. **Add the values:** $18.96 + 2.50 + 2.86 = 24.32$.
**Answer:** The relative atomic mass of M is $24.32$ (no unit).

## The periodic table and periodicity of elements

### Objective x: Relate atomic number to the position of an element on the periodic table

The modern periodic table is an arrangement of elements in order of increasing atomic number. An element's position can be deduced directly from its electron configuration.

- **Period (Horizontal Row):** Indicated by the **number of electron shells** (the highest principal quantum number, $n$). For example, sodium ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$) has $3$ shells, so it is in Period $3$.
- **Group (Vertical Column):** For $s$ and $p$ block elements, the group is determined by the **number of valence electrons** (electrons in the outermost shell). Sodium has $1$ valence electron, so it is in Group $1$. *(Note: By modern IUPAC naming, elements with 3 to 8 valence electrons in the p-block are in Groups 13 to 18, but older textbooks and JAMB often refer to them as Groups III to VIII).*
- **Blocks ($s, p, d, f$):** Determined by the subshell into which the last electron is added. Sodium's last electron goes into an $s$-orbital, placing it in the $s$-block.

### Objective xi: Relate properties of groups of elements on the periodic table

Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and thus exhibit similar chemical properties.

| Family of Elements | Group | Key Properties |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Alkali Metals** | Group 1 | Highly reactive metals (stored under oil). They have $1$ valence electron, form $+1$ ions easily, and react vigorously with water to form alkaline solutions. |
| **Alkaline-Earth Metals** | Group 2 | Reactive metals (though less than Group 1). Have $2$ valence electrons and form $+2$ ions. |
| **Halogens** | Group 17 (VII) | Highly reactive non-metals (fluorine, chlorine, etc.). Have $7$ valence electrons, form $-1$ ions, and exist as diatomic molecules. |
| **Noble Gases** | Group 18 (VIII) | Extremely unreactive (inert) gases like neon and argon. They have a completely filled outer shell (stable octet or duplet). |
| **Transition Metals** | $d$-block | Hard metals located in the centre of the periodic table. They exhibit variable oxidation states, form coloured compounds, act as catalysts, and form complex ions. |

### Objective xii: Identify reasons for variation in properties across the period and down the groups

Periodic properties vary due to two opposing factors:
1. **Effective Nuclear Charge:** As atomic number increases across a period, more protons are added to the nucleus. This stronger positive charge pulls the electron shells closer to the nucleus.
2. **Shielding (Screening) Effect:** As you go down a group, new electron shells are added. The inner shells shield the outer valence electrons from the pull of the nucleus.

**Key Variations:**
- **Ionization Energy (IE):** The energy required to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom (unit: $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$).
  - *Across a period:* IE **increases**. Effective nuclear charge increases while atomic radius decreases, making it harder to remove an electron.
  - *Down a group:* IE **decreases**. Extra shells increase the shielding effect and atomic radius, making it easier to lose the outermost electron.
- **Ionic Radii:** The size of an ion (unit: nanometres, $\text{nm}$). Positive ions (cations) are always smaller than their parent atoms because they lose an electron shell. Negative ions (anions) are larger than their parent atoms because added electrons increase electron-electron repulsion, causing the shell to expand.
- **Electron Affinity:** The energy released when a gaseous atom gains an electron (unit: $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$). It generally becomes **more negative (increases)** across a period (as non-metals readily gain electrons) and **decreases** down a group.
- **Electronegativity:** The ability of an atom in a molecule to attract shared electrons to itself (dimensionless Pauling scale). It **increases** across a period (Fluorine is the most electronegative) and **decreases** down a group.
- **Electrical and Thermal Conductivities:** Metals are excellent conductors. Across a period, as you move from metals on the left to non-metals on the right, electrical and thermal conductivity **decreases** drastically.

## Chemical bonding

Atoms bond together to achieve a stable electron configuration, similar to the noble gases (an octet of $8$ valence electrons, or a duplet of $2$ for hydrogen and helium).

### Objective xiii: Differentiate between the different types of bonding

| Type of Bond | How it forms | Typical between... |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond** | Complete **transfer of electrons** from a metal atom to a non-metal atom. Electrostatic forces hold the resulting oppositely charged ions together. | Metal + Non-metal |
| **Covalent Bond** | **Sharing of electrons** between atoms so both achieve a stable octet/duplet. | Non-metal + Non-metal |
| **Coordinate (Dative) Bond** | A special type of covalent bond where **both shared electrons are provided by only one** of the bonding atoms. | A molecule with a lone pair + an electron-deficient ion |
| **Hydrogen Bond** | A strong intermolecular dipole-dipole attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, or F) and another electronegative atom. | Molecules containing H-F, H-O, or H-N bonds (e.g., water). |
| **Metallic Bond** | The electrostatic attraction between a regular lattice of positive metal ions and a "sea" of delocalized electrons moving freely among them. | Metal + Metal (within a piece of metal) |
| **Van der Waals' forces** | Weak, short-range intermolecular forces arising from temporary shifts in electron density (induced dipoles). | Present between all molecules, but main force in noble gases and non-polar molecules. |

**Important Note on the Syllabus:** The JAMB syllabus specifically groups hydrogen bonding as a **"special type of electrovalency"** (due to the strong electrostatic dipole attraction) and metallic bonding as a **"special type of covalency"** (due to the sharing of delocalized electrons). Keep this unique classification in mind for exam questions.

**Coordinate Bonding and Complex Ions**
Transition metals frequently form complex ions via coordinate bonding. The transition metal ion is electron-deficient, and molecules or ions called **ligands** (like $\text{NH}_3$ or $\text{CN}^-$) use their lone pairs to form coordinate bonds with the central metal ion.
- **$[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{3-}$** (Hexacyanoferrate(III) ion): Central $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ion coordinates with six $\text{CN}^-$ ligands.
- **$[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$** (Hexacyanoferrate(II) ion): Central $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ion coordinates with six $\text{CN}^-$ ligands.
- **$[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4]^{2+}$** (Tetraamminecopper(II) ion): Central $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ion coordinates with four $\text{NH}_3$ ligands.
- **$[\text{Ag(NH}_3)_2]^{+}$** (Diamminesilver(I) ion): Central $\text{Ag}^+$ ion coordinates with two $\text{NH}_3$ ligands.

### Objective xiv: Deduce bond types based on electron configurations

You can predict the type of bond two elements will form by looking at their electron configurations.
- Elements with **$1$, $2$, or $3$ valence electrons** are metals. They tend to lose electrons.
- Elements with **$5$, $6$, or $7$ valence electrons** are non-metals. They tend to gain electrons.
- **Metal + Non-metal = Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond.**
  *Example:* Element X is $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$ (Group 1, metal). Element Y is $1s^2 2s^2 2p^5$ (Group 17, non-metal). X transfers its $1$ electron to Y to form XY, an ionic compound.
- **Non-metal + Non-metal = Covalent Bond.**
  *Example:* Carbon ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$) and Oxygen ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$) will share electrons to form covalent bonds (as in $\text{CO}_2$).

### Objective xv: Relate the nature of bonding to properties of compounds

The physical properties of a compound depend entirely on its bond type.

| Property | Electrovalent (Ionic) Compounds | Covalent Compounds |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **State at room temp.** | Hard, crystalline solids. | Mostly liquids or gases (some soft solids). |
| **Melting / Boiling points** | Very high (strong electrostatic forces between ions take a lot of heat to break). | Low (weak intermolecular forces between molecules are easy to break). |
| **Solubility** | Soluble in polar solvents like water. Insoluble in non-polar organic solvents (like kerosene or benzene). | Insoluble in water. Soluble in non-polar organic solvents. |
| **Electrical Conductivity** | Do not conduct electricity when solid. Conduct electricity when molten (melted) or dissolved in water, because the ions are free to move. | Do not conduct electricity in any state (no free ions or electrons). |

## Shapes of simple molecules

### Objective xvi: Differentiate between the various shapes of molecules

The shape of a simple molecule is determined by the repulsion between electron pairs in the valence shell of the central atom. This is the **Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory**. Electron pairs arrange themselves as far apart as possible to minimize repulsion. Lone pairs (unshared pairs) repel more strongly than bond pairs.

1. **Linear ($180^\circ$):** The atoms are arranged in a straight line.
   - Examples: $\text{H}_2$, $\text{O}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{HCl}$, and $\text{CO}_2$.
   - In $\text{CO}_2$, the central carbon forms double bonds with two oxygen atoms. With no lone pairs on the carbon, the two regions of electron density repel to $180^\circ$.
2. **Non-linear / V-shaped / Bent ($104.5^\circ$):** 
   - Example: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
   - The central oxygen atom has two bond pairs and two lone pairs. The strong repulsion from the two lone pairs pushes the hydrogen atoms closer together, reducing the angle to $104.5^\circ$.
3. **Pyramidal ($107^\circ$):** 
   - Example: $\text{NH}_3$ (Ammonia).
   - The central nitrogen atom has three bond pairs and one lone pair. The single lone pair repels the bond pairs downwards, forming a trigonal pyramid base with an angle of $107^\circ$.
4. **Tetrahedral ($109.5^\circ$):** 
   - Example: $\text{CH}_4$ (Methane).
   - The central carbon atom has exactly four bond pairs and zero lone pairs. They spread out equally in 3D space to form a tetrahedron with angles of $109.5^\circ$.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Wrong Protons/Electrons in Ions:** A very common mistake is calculating electrons for an ion and forgetting to subtract or add the charge. For $\text{K}^+$, subtract $1$ from the atomic number. For $\text{O}^{2-}$, add $2$ to the atomic number.
- **Mass Number vs Relative Atomic Mass:** Mass number is a whole number (protons + neutrons for one specific isotope). Relative Atomic Mass (RAM) is a weighted average and is usually a decimal (like $35.5$ for Chlorine).
- **JAMB Syllabus Trap:** Remember that JAMB considers hydrogen bonding a special type of electrovalency and metallic bonding a special type of covalency. If asked directly by a past question format, pick the syllabus's terminology.
- **Trend Confusions:** Across a period, atomic radius **decreases**, but ionization energy **increases**. Do not mix them up.
- **VSEPR Angles:** Memorize the specific bond angles for the shapes: Linear ($180^\circ$), Tetrahedral ($109.5^\circ$), Pyramidal ($107^\circ$), and Non-linear/Bent ($104.5^\circ$).
- **Electron Configuration of Calcium (Atomic No 20):** Do not write $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^2$. The $4s$ subshell is lower in energy than $3d$. The correct ending is $3p^6 4s^2$.

## Quick summary

1. Atoms are made of protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral) in the nucleus, surrounded by electrons (negative) in shells. The atomic number equals the number of protons.
2. Rutherford discovered the nucleus, Thomson the electron, and Bohr the planetary energy levels. 
3. Electrons reside in orbitals governed by quantum numbers. $s$-orbitals are spherical (hold $2$ electrons), and $p$-orbitals are dumb-bell shaped (hold $6$ electrons).
4. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Relative atomic mass is their weighted average.
5. The periodic table is arranged by atomic number. Elements in the same group have similar valence electrons. Properties like ionization energy increase across a period and decrease down a group.
6. Electrovalent (ionic) bonding involves electron transfer (metal to non-metal), creating hard, high-melting solids that conduct electricity when molten. Covalent bonding involves sharing electrons (non-metals), creating low-melting substances.
7. Coordinate bonding happens in complex ions like $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{3-}$ where one atom donates both shared electrons.
8. Molecule shapes depend on electron pair repulsion: $\text{CO}_2$ is linear, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is non-linear, $\text{NH}_3$ is pyramidal, and $\text{CH}_4$ is tetrahedral.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following defines an ion?**
- A. The smallest particle of an element that retains its properties.
- B. An atom or group of atoms that possesses an electric charge.
- C. A molecule that exists independently.
- D. A neutral particle in the nucleus.

**Answer: B.** 
1. An ion is formed when an atom gains or loses electrons to become charged.
2. For example, sodium loses $1$ electron to form the cation $\text{Na}^+$.
3. The most tempting wrong option is A, but it describes a neutral atom, not an ion.

**2. Who is credited with the discovery of the atomic nucleus?**
- A. J.J. Thomson
- B. John Dalton
- C. Ernest Rutherford
- D. Niels Bohr

**Answer: C.** 
1. Ernest Rutherford conducted the famous gold-foil experiment.
2. He fired alpha particles (which have a $+2$ charge) at a gold foil and observed their scattering, leading to the discovery of the dense nucleus.
3. The most tempting wrong option is A (J.J. Thomson), but he discovered the electron, not the nucleus.

**3. An atom of potassium can be represented as $^{39}_{19}\text{K}$. How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in the potassium ion, $\text{K}^+$?**
- A. 19 protons, 20 neutrons, 19 electrons
- B. 19 protons, 20 neutrons, 18 electrons
- C. 20 protons, 19 neutrons, 18 electrons
- D. 19 protons, 39 neutrons, 18 electrons

**Answer: B.** 
1. Protons $= Z = 19$ (from the atomic number).
2. Neutrons $= A - Z = 39 - 19 = 20$.
3. Electrons $= \text{Protons} - \text{Charge} = 19 - (+1) = 18$ electrons.
4. The most tempting wrong option is A, which is the configuration for a neutral atom, failing to subtract $1$ electron for the positive charge.

**4. Which rule states that electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly before they pair up?**
- A. Pauli Exclusion Principle
- B. Aufbau Principle
- C. Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity
- D. Bohr's Atomic Theory

**Answer: C.** 
1. Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity dictates that orbitals of equal energy (degenerate orbitals) are each occupied by $1$ electron before any pairing occurs.
2. The Pauli Exclusion Principle limits each orbital to a maximum of $2$ electrons.
3. The most tempting wrong option is B (Aufbau Principle), but it refers to filling lower energy levels (like $1s$ before $2s$), not how electrons pair in degenerate orbitals.

**5. Element $X$ has two isotopes, $^{10}X$ and $^{11}X$, with percentage abundances of 20% and 80% respectively. What is the relative atomic mass of $X$? (RAM has no unit)**
- A. 10.2
- B. 10.5
- C. 10.8
- D. 11.0

**Answer: C.** 
1. Convert the percentages to fractions: $20\% = 0.20$ and $80\% = 0.80$.
2. Multiply by the respective mass numbers: $10 \times 0.20 = 2.0$ and $11 \times 0.80 = 8.8$.
3. Add the values together: $2.0 + 8.8 = 10.8$.
4. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from mistakenly swapping the abundances ($10 \times 0.80 + 11 \times 0.20 = 10.2$).

**6. The increase in ionization energy across a period from left to right is due to:**
- A. increase in atomic radius
- B. decrease in effective nuclear charge
- C. increase in effective nuclear charge and decrease in atomic radius
- D. increase in the shielding effect

**Answer: C.** 
1. Across a period, the effective nuclear charge increases as protons are added to the nucleus.
2. The atomic radius decreases, so the nucleus pulls the valence electrons more strongly.
3. Therefore, more energy (in $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$) is required to remove an electron.
4. The most tempting wrong option is A, because atomic radius actually decreases, not increases, across a period.

**7. Based on JAMB syllabus classification, hydrogen bonding is considered a special type of:**
- A. covalency
- B. electrovalency
- C. dative bonding
- D. metallic bonding

**Answer: B.** 
1. The official JAMB syllabus explicitly classifies hydrogen bonding as a special type of electrovalency.
2. This relates to the strong electrostatic dipole attraction between atoms with high electronegativities (like $F$, $O$, or $N$).
3. The most tempting wrong option is A (covalency), but metallic bonding is the one classified by the syllabus as a special type of covalency, not hydrogen bonding.

**8. An element $Y$ has the electron configuration $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2$. An element $Z$ has the configuration $1s^2 2s^2 2p^5$. What is the formula and type of bond in the compound formed by $Y$ and $Z$?**
- A. $YZ_2$, electrovalent
- B. $Y_2Z$, covalent
- C. $YZ$, electrovalent
- D. $YZ_2$, covalent

**Answer: A.** 
1. Element $Y$ has $2$ valence electrons, so it is a metal with a valency of $2$ ($Y^{2+}$).
2. Element $Z$ has $7$ valence electrons, so it is a non-metal with a valency of $1$ ($Z^{-}$).
3. Exchanging valencies gives the chemical formula $YZ_2$.
4. A bond between a metal and a non-metal involves electron transfer and is electrovalent.
5. The most tempting wrong option is D, which gets the formula right but wrongly calls the metal-nonmetal bond covalent.

**9. What is the shape of a water molecule ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)?**
- A. Linear
- B. Tetrahedral
- C. Pyramidal
- D. Non-linear (V-shaped)

**Answer: D.** 
1. The central oxygen atom in water has $2$ bond pairs and $2$ lone pairs of electrons.
2. According to VSEPR theory, the strong repulsion from the lone pairs bends the molecule.
3. This creates a non-linear (V-shaped) geometry with a bond angle of $104.5^\circ$.
4. The most tempting wrong option is A (linear), which is the shape of $\text{CO}_2$ (where there are $0$ lone pairs on carbon), not water.

**10. The bond formed between the central iron ion and the cyanide ligands in the hexacyanoferrate(III) complex, $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{3-}$, is a:**
- A. metallic bond
- B. coordinate (dative) bond
- C. van der Waals' force
- D. pure covalent bond

**Answer: B.** 
1. The complex ion contains a central $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ion and $6$ cyanide ($\text{CN}^-$) ligands.
2. Each ligand donates a lone pair of electrons to form a bond with the electron-deficient iron ion.
3. A covalent bond where one species provides both electrons is called a coordinate (or dative) bond.
4. The most tempting wrong option is D (pure covalent), but it ignores that the bonding electrons come exclusively from the ligands.
