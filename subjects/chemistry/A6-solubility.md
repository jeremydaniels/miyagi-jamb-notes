## Unsaturated, saturated and supersaturated solutions

When a solid substance (the **solute**) is added to a liquid (the **solvent**), the crystal lattice of the solid breaks down and its particles mix evenly with the solvent particles. This uniform mixture is called a **solution**. Depending on how much solute is dissolved relative to the maximum amount the solvent can hold, solutions are classified into three types.

### Distinguish between the different types of solutions

**Objective i: distinguish between the different types of solutions.**

1. **Unsaturated solution:** This is a solution that can still dissolve more of the solute at a given temperature. If you add a small spoonful of sugar to a large cup of water and stir, it will disappear completely. Because the water has the capacity to dissolve more sugar at that temperature, it is unsaturated.
2. **Saturated solution:** This is a solution that contains the maximum amount of solute it can hold at a given temperature, in the presence of undissolved solute. It is in a state of **dynamic equilibrium**: the rate at which solid particles leave the crystal to dissolve is exactly equal to the rate at which dissolved particles return to the solid to crystallize. If you add more solid to a saturated solution, it will just sink to the bottom and remain undissolved.
3. **Supersaturated solution:** This is an unstable solution that contains *more* solute than it would normally be able to hold stably at that temperature. It is created by saturating a solution at a high temperature (where solubility is higher) and then cooling it very slowly and carefully without disturbing it. Because it is highly unstable, scratching the side of the container or dropping in a tiny piece of the solid (a **seed crystal**) will cause the excess solute to rapidly crystallize out, leaving behind a normal saturated solution.

### Calculate the amount of solute that can dissolve

**Objective iii: calculate the amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature.**

**Solubility** is the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a specific volume of solvent at a specific temperature. While some older chemistry textbooks define it as the mass (in grams) of solute that will saturate $100 \text{ g}$ of water, the JAMB syllabus strictly defines solubility as **molar concentration**: the number of moles of solute per cubic decimetre ($\text{dm}^3$) of solution.

The core formula is:
$$ \text{Solubility (mol dm}^{-3}\text{)} = \frac{\text{Amount of solute (mol)}}{\text{Volume of solution (dm}^3\text{)}} $$

**Key rules for JAMB calculations:**
- **Volume conversion:** Volume is almost always given in $\text{cm}^3$ in exam questions. You must convert it to $\text{dm}^3$ by dividing by $1000$ (since $1000 \text{ cm}^3 = 1 \text{ dm}^3$).
- **Mole conversion:** To convert a given mass to moles, use the formula $\text{Moles} = \frac{\text{Mass (g)}}{\text{Molar mass (g mol}^{-1}\text{)}}$.
- **Mass concentration:** If a question gives or asks for solubility in $\text{g dm}^{-3}$, remember that $\text{Mass concentration} = \text{Molar concentration} \times \text{Molar mass}$.

**Worked Examples**

**Example 1: Finding solubility in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$**
$12.0 \text{ g}$ of sodium hydroxide, $\text{NaOH}$, is dissolved in $200 \text{ cm}^3$ of water to form a saturated solution at $25 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate its solubility in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$. ($\text{Na} = 23, \text{O} = 16, \text{H} = 1$)

1. **Calculate the molar mass of $\text{NaOH}$:** Adding atomic masses, $23 + 16 + 1 = 40 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. **Find the number of moles of solute:** Using the formula $\text{Moles} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Molar mass}}$, $\text{Moles} = \frac{12.0 \text{ g}}{40 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.30 \text{ mol}$.
3. **Convert the volume to $\text{dm}^3$:** Since $1000 \text{ cm}^3 = 1 \text{ dm}^3$, divide by 1000: $\text{Volume} = \frac{200 \text{ cm}^3}{1000} = 0.20 \text{ dm}^3$.
4. **Calculate the solubility:** Divide moles by volume in $\text{dm}^3$: $\text{Solubility} = \frac{0.30 \text{ mol}}{0.20 \text{ dm}^3} = 1.5 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.

**Example 2: Finding the mass of solute required**
What mass of sodium trioxocarbonate(IV), $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, is required to prepare $500 \text{ cm}^3$ of a $0.2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ saturated solution? ($\text{Na} = 23, \text{C} = 12, \text{O} = 16$)

1. **Calculate the molar mass of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$:** $(23 \times 2) + 12 + (16 \times 3) = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. **Convert the solution volume to $\text{dm}^3$:** $\text{Volume} = \frac{500 \text{ cm}^3}{1000} = 0.50 \text{ dm}^3$.
3. **Find the number of moles needed:** Using $\text{Moles} = \text{Concentration} \times \text{Volume}$, $\text{Moles} = 0.2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 0.50 \text{ dm}^3 = 0.10 \text{ mol}$.
4. **Convert moles to mass:** Using $\text{Mass} = \text{Moles} \times \text{Molar mass}$, $\text{Mass} = 0.10 \text{ mol} \times 106 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 10.6 \text{ g}$.

**Example 3: Finding the volume of solvent required**
The solubility of potassium tetraoxomanganate(VII), $\text{KMnO}_4$, at a certain temperature is $0.25 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$. What volume of water (in $\text{cm}^3$) is required to completely dissolve $15.8 \text{ g}$ of the salt? ($\text{K} = 39, \text{Mn} = 55, \text{O} = 16$)

1. **Calculate the molar mass of $\text{KMnO}_4$:** $39 + 55 + (16 \times 4) = 94 + 64 = 158 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. **Find the number of moles of $\text{KMnO}_4$:** $\text{Moles} = \frac{15.8 \text{ g}}{158 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.10 \text{ mol}$.
3. **Find the volume in $\text{dm}^3$:** Rearranging the solubility formula to $\text{Volume} = \frac{\text{Moles}}{\text{Solubility}}$, $\text{Volume} = \frac{0.10 \text{ mol}}{0.25 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}} = 0.40 \text{ dm}^3$.
4. **Convert the volume back to $\text{cm}^3$:** Multiply by 1000: $\text{Volume in cm}^3 = 0.40 \text{ dm}^3 \times 1000 = 400 \text{ cm}^3$.

## Solubility curves and temperature

### Deduce that solubility is temperature-dependent

**Objective iv: deduce that solubility is temperature-dependent.**

The amount of solute that can dissolve in a solvent depends heavily on the temperature of the system.
- **For most solid solutes** (like potassium trioxonitrate(V), $\text{KNO}_3$, and potassium trioxochlorate(V), $\text{KClO}_3$), the dissolution process is endothermic (it absorbs heat). Therefore, their solubility **increases** as the temperature increases. Heat provides the necessary energy to break the attractive forces within the solid crystal lattice.
- **For a few solid solutes** (like sodium chloride, $\text{NaCl}$), the solubility remains practically constant and is largely unaffected by temperature changes.
- **For some solids** (like calcium hydroxide, $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) **and all gases** (like $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$), the dissolution process is exothermic. Therefore, their solubility **decreases** as temperature increases. This is why boiling water drives out dissolved air.

### Interpret solubility curves

**Objective ii: interpret solubility curves.**

A **solubility curve** is a graph that plots the solubility of a substance (on the vertical y-axis) against temperature (on the horizontal x-axis).

Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**Important deductions from solubility curves:**
1. **Finding exact solubility:** You can find the maximum amount of solute that will dissolve at any specific temperature by reading the value on the y-axis that corresponds to a temperature on the x-axis.
2. **Comparing solubilities:** You can identify which salt is most soluble at a given temperature by seeing which curve is highest at that specific point.
3. **Fractional crystallization:** If a hot solution containing a mixture of two dissolved salts is cooled, the salt whose solubility drops sharply (like $\text{KNO}_3$) will form solid crystals and precipitate out first. A salt whose solubility barely changes (like $\text{NaCl}$) will largely remain dissolved in the solution. This principle is used heavily in industry to purify and separate mixtures of salts.
4. **Calculating mass deposited upon cooling:** When a hot saturated solution is cooled down, its capacity to hold dissolved solute drops. The excess solute can no longer stay dissolved and forms solid crystals.

**Example 4: Calculating mass deposited on cooling (Fractional crystallization)**
The solubility of potassium trioxochlorate(V), $\text{KClO}_3$, is $1.2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ at $60 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and $0.4 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ at $20 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the mass of the salt that will crystallize out when $250 \text{ cm}^3$ of a saturated solution is cooled from $60 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $20 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. ($\text{K} = 39, \text{Cl} = 35.5, \text{O} = 16$)

1. **Calculate the molar mass of $\text{KClO}_3$:** $39 + 35.5 + (16 \times 3) = 122.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. **Find the change (decrease) in solubility:** $\text{Decrease} = 1.2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} - 0.4 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.8 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$. This means $0.8 \text{ moles}$ of salt will crystallize out of every $1 \text{ dm}^3$ of solution.
3. **Convert the actual volume to $\text{dm}^3$:** $\text{Volume} = \frac{250 \text{ cm}^3}{1000} = 0.25 \text{ dm}^3$.
4. **Calculate the actual moles of salt deposited:** $\text{Moles deposited} = 0.8 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 0.25 \text{ dm}^3 = 0.20 \text{ mol}$.
5. **Convert these moles to mass:** $\text{Mass deposited} = 0.20 \text{ mol} \times 122.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 24.5 \text{ g}$.

## Solvents for fats, oil, perspiration and paints

While water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is often called the "universal solvent" because it dissolves many ionic and polar substances, it cannot dissolve non-polar substances. The fundamental rule of solubility in chemistry is **"like dissolves like"**. Polar solvents dissolve polar and ionic solutes, while non-polar solvents dissolve non-polar solutes.

### Relate nature of solvents to their uses

**Objective v: relate nature of solvents to their uses.**

To remove a stain from clothing or surfaces, we must choose a solvent that is chemically similar in nature to the stain.

| Substance / Stain | Recommended Solvents for Removal | Chemical Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fats, oil and grease** | Petrol (gasoline), kerosene, ether, methylbenzene | Fats and palm oil are non-polar organic compounds. They will not mix with water, but dissolve perfectly in non-polar organic solvents like petrol. |
| **Paints** | Turpentine, white spirit, kerosene | Oil-based paints contain non-polar organic resins and oils. After painting a house, decorators use turpentine to clean their brushes because polar water cannot dissolve the non-polar paint. |
| **Perspiration (sweat)** | Dilute ammonia solution | Perspiration contains traces of grease alongside organic acids (like lactic acid). A mild base like dilute ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) solution neutralizes the acidic components and safely dissolves the greasy residues without destroying the fabric. |

## True and false solutions

When a substance is dispersed into a liquid or gas, it does not always form a perfect solution. Depending on the physical size of the particles dispersed in the medium, the resulting mixture is classified into three distinct categories: a true solution, a colloid (false solution), or a suspension.

### Differentiate among true solutions, suspensions and colloids

**Objective vi: differentiate among true solutions, suspensions and colloids.**

- **True solution:** A completely homogeneous mixture where the solute particles are broken down to individual molecules or ions (less than $1 \text{ nm}$ in diameter). It is perfectly clear (transparent) and the particles will never settle out.
- **Colloid (False solution):** An intermediate state between a true solution and a suspension. The dispersed particles are between $1 \text{ nm}$ and $1000 \text{ nm}$ in size. They are too small to be seen and do not settle on standing, but they are large enough to interfere with light.
- **Suspension:** A heterogeneous mixture containing large solid particles (greater than $1000 \text{ nm}$ in size) dispersed in a liquid or gas. The particles are visible to the naked eye and are so heavy that they will settle down at the bottom if left undisturbed (like muddy water from a river).

### Compare the properties of a true and a false solution

**Objective vii: compare the properties of a 'true' solution and a 'false' solution.**

A false solution (colloid) behaves very differently from a true solution. The most famous distinguishing characteristic is the **Tyndall effect**: if you shine a powerful beam of light through a true solution, the beam is entirely invisible. However, if you shine it through a colloid, the slightly larger particles scatter the light, making the entire path of the light beam glow brightly.

| Property | True Solution | Colloid (False Solution) | Suspension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Particle size** | Less than $1 \text{ nm}$ ($10^{-9} \text{ m}$) | Between $1 \text{ nm}$ and $1000 \text{ nm}$ | Greater than $1000 \text{ nm}$ |
| **Visibility of particles** | Invisible even under powerful electron microscopes | Invisible to the naked eye, but can be detected under an electron microscope | Visible to the naked eye |
| **Settling out** | Particles never settle | Particles do not settle on standing | Particles settle out under gravity |
| **Filterability** | Passes through ordinary filter paper easily | Passes through ordinary filter paper | Cannot pass through filter paper; leaves a solid residue |
| **Appearance** | Clear and transparent | Often cloudy, translucent or opaque | Opaque |
| **Tyndall effect** | Does not scatter light (no Tyndall effect) | Scatters light heavily (shows Tyndall effect) | May scatter or absorb light completely |

### Provide typical examples of suspensions and colloids

**Objective viii: provide typical examples of suspensions and colloids.**

**Textbook variation warning:** The classification of some mixtures can vary between physics, biology, and chemistry textbooks (for instance, some advanced texts call dust in the air a solid aerosol colloid). For your UTME, you **must** use the specific classifications explicitly listed in the JAMB syllabus.

**Typical Examples of Suspensions (according to JAMB):**
1. **Harmattan haze:** Solid dust particles suspended in the air.
2. **Water paints:** Large pigment particles temporarily suspended in water.
3. Other common real-world examples: Muddy water, chalk dust stirred in water.

**Typical Examples of Colloids (according to JAMB):**
Colloids are named based on the physical state of the dispersed particles and the medium they are suspended in.
1. **Fog:** An aerosol (liquid water droplets dispersed in a gas).
2. **Aerosol spray:** Liquid or solid particles dispersed in a gas (e.g., insecticide sprays, air fresheners).
3. **Milk:** An emulsion (liquid fat drops permanently dispersed in a liquid water base).
4. **Emulsion paints:** Liquid polymers dispersed in water.
5. **Blood:** A sol (solid blood cells dispersed in a liquid plasma).
6. **Rubber solution:** A sol (solid rubber polymer chains dispersed in a liquid solvent).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Solute | The solid, liquid, or gas that dissolves in a solvent. |
| Solvent | The liquid (or medium) that dissolves the solute. |
| Unsaturated solution | A solution that has the capacity to dissolve more solute at that temperature. |
| Saturated solution | A solution containing the maximum amount of dissolved solute it can hold stably at a given temperature, in equilibrium with undissolved solute. |
| Supersaturated solution | An unstable solution tricked into holding more solute than it normally should at that temperature. |
| Solubility | The maximum amount of solute (in moles) that can dissolve in $1 \text{ dm}^3$ of solution at a specific temperature. |
| Fractional crystallization | The process of separating two dissolved solutes by cooling a hot saturated solution, relying on their different temperature-solubility curves. |
| True solution | A completely homogeneous mixture with transparent particles smaller than $1 \text{ nm}$. |
| Colloid (False solution) | A mixture with intermediate particle sizes ($1$ to $1000 \text{ nm}$) that scatter light. |
| Suspension | A heterogeneous mixture with large particles ($>1000 \text{ nm}$) that eventually settle out. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Unit slips are the most common mistake.** Always check if your volume is given in $\text{cm}^3$ and remember to divide it by $1000$ to get $\text{dm}^3$ before calculating molar concentration. 
- **Solubility unit:** Do not confuse mass concentration ($\text{g dm}^{-3}$) with molar concentration ($\text{mol dm}^{-3}$). JAMB standard solubility questions expect $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$. You must divide the mass by the molar mass to get moles.
- **Difference between saturated and supersaturated:** A saturated solution contains the maximum it can hold *normally*; a supersaturated solution holds *more* than normal because it was created artificially by slow cooling.
- **Not all salts dissolve more when hot:** Remember that the solubility of gases and a few specific solids like $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ *decreases* when temperature increases.
- **Learn the exact JAMB colloid/suspension list:** Questions asking "Which of the following is a suspension?" often feature "Harmattan haze" as the correct answer. Do not choose fog, as JAMB classifies fog as a colloid.

## Quick summary

1. An unsaturated solution can dissolve more solute; a saturated solution is full and in equilibrium with undissolved solid; a supersaturated solution holds unnaturally large amounts of solute and is highly unstable.
2. Solubility is defined as the number of moles of a solute that can dissolve in $1 \text{ dm}^3$ of solution at a given temperature.
3. For most solids, solubility increases as temperature rises; for gases and a few solids, it decreases.
4. Solubility curves map how solubility changes with temperature, helping chemists separate mixtures of salts by fractional crystallization.
5. "Like dissolves like": non-polar solvents (like petrol, turpentine) dissolve non-polar stains (like fats, oils, paints), while dilute ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) is used for perspiration.
6. True solutions have completely invisible particles smaller than $1 \text{ nm}$, do not settle, and do not scatter light.
7. Colloids (false solutions) have particles between $1 \text{ nm}$ and $1000 \text{ nm}$ and exhibit the Tyndall effect (scattering light). JAMB examples include fog, milk, blood, aerosol sprays, emulsion paints and rubber solution.
8. Suspensions have large particles (greater than $1000 \text{ nm}$) that settle on standing. JAMB strictly classifies Harmattan haze and water paints as suspensions.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following statements best describes a supersaturated solution?**

- A. A solution that can dissolve more solute at $25 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
- B. A solution that contains exactly the maximum amount of solute it can hold stably at $25 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
- C. A solution containing more solute than it can normally hold stably at that temperature.
- D. A solution in which the solute particles are visible and settle down to the bottom.

**Answer: C.** 
1. A saturated solution holds the maximum amount of solute at a given temperature, e.g., $25 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
2. A supersaturated solution contains more solute than the saturated solution at that same temperature.
3. This state is highly unstable and usually requires slow cooling without disturbance.
Option B is the most tempting wrong option, but the mistake behind it is describing a normal saturated solution, missing the fact that a supersaturated solution holds an artificially higher amount of solute ($\text{moles}$).

**2. $5.85 \text{ g}$ of $\text{NaCl}$ (molar mass = $58.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$) is dissolved in $50 \text{ cm}^3$ of water to form a saturated solution at $25 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. What is its solubility?**

- A. $2.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- B. $0.2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- C. $1.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- D. $0.05 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Find moles of $\text{NaCl}$: $\frac{5.85 \text{ g}}{58.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.10 \text{ mol}$.
2. Convert volume to $\text{dm}^3$: $\frac{50 \text{ cm}^3}{1000} = 0.05 \text{ dm}^3$.
3. Calculate solubility: $\frac{0.10 \text{ mol}}{0.05 \text{ dm}^3} = 2.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
Option B ($0.2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$) is tempting; the mistake behind it is incorrectly converting the volume to $0.5 \text{ dm}^3$ instead of $0.05 \text{ dm}^3$, giving $\frac{0.10 \text{ mol}}{0.5 \text{ dm}^3} = 0.2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.

**3. What mass of $\text{NaOH}$ (molar mass = $40 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$) is present in $500 \text{ cm}^3$ of a $0.1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution?**

- A. $2.0 \text{ g}$
- B. $4.0 \text{ g}$
- C. $0.5 \text{ g}$
- D. $20.0 \text{ g}$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Convert the volume to $\text{dm}^3$: $\frac{500 \text{ cm}^3}{1000} = 0.50 \text{ dm}^3$.
2. Calculate the moles needed: $0.1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 0.50 \text{ dm}^3 = 0.05 \text{ mol}$.
3. Convert the moles to mass: $0.05 \text{ mol} \times 40 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 2.0 \text{ g}$.
Option B ($4.0 \text{ g}$) is tempting; the mistake behind it is multiplying the concentration ($0.1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$) directly by the molar mass ($40 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$) without multiplying by the $0.50 \text{ dm}^3$ volume, thus calculating the mass for a full $1 \text{ dm}^3$ instead.

**4. Which of the following solvents is best suited for removing oil-based paint stains from clothing?**

- A. Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)
- B. Turpentine
- C. Dilute ammonia solution
- D. Dilute hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$)

**Answer: B.** 
1. The chemical principle for dissolving stains is "like dissolves like".
2. Oil-based paints contain non-polar resins and oily compounds that will not mix with a polar solvent like water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
3. Turpentine is a non-polar organic solvent, making it perfectly effective for dissolving non-polar paint.
Option A is tempting; the mistake behind it is assuming that water, being the universal solvent, dissolves everything, ignoring the fact that $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is highly polar and cannot dissolve non-polar paint.

**5. A key property that distinguishes a true solution from a colloid is that**

- A. a true solution exhibits the Tyndall effect.
- B. particles in a true solution are larger than $1000 \text{ nm}$.
- C. particles in a true solution are completely invisible and do not scatter light.
- D. a true solution leaves a solid residue when filtered.

**Answer: C.** 
1. True solution particles are smaller than $1 \text{ nm}$ ($10^{-9} \text{ m}$).
2. Because they are so small, they are completely invisible and cannot scatter a beam of light.
3. Colloids contain larger particles ($1$ to $1000 \text{ nm}$) that do scatter light (the Tyndall effect).
Option A is tempting; the mistake behind it is confusing the properties of the two mixtures and falsely attributing the Tyndall effect to a true solution instead of a colloid.

**6. Which of the following statements about the solubility of most solid solutes in water is correct?**

- A. It decreases as temperature increases.
- B. It increases as temperature increases.
- C. It remains completely unchanged as temperature increases.
- D. It depends solely on the atmospheric pressure.

**Answer: B.** 
1. The dissolution of most solid salts (like $\text{KNO}_3$) in water is an endothermic process.
2. An increase in temperature provides the necessary heat energy ($\Delta H > 0$) to overcome the forces holding the solid crystal lattice together.
3. Thus, for most solid solutes, solubility increases as temperature rises.
Option A is tempting; the mistake behind it is confusing the general behaviour of solids with that of gases (like dissolved $\text{CO}_2$), whose solubility actually decreases as temperature increases.

**7. The solubility of $\text{KNO}_3$ ($\text{M} = 101 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$) is $8.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ at $60 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and $2.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ at $20 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. What mass of the salt will crystallize out if $250 \text{ cm}^3$ of a saturated solution is cooled from $60 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $20 \text{ }^\circ\text{C}$?**

- A. $15.15 \text{ g}$
- B. $151.5 \text{ g}$
- C. $606.0 \text{ g}$
- D. $75.75 \text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Find the decrease in solubility: $8.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} - 2.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 6.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
2. Convert the solution volume to $\text{dm}^3$: $\frac{250 \text{ cm}^3}{1000} = 0.25 \text{ dm}^3$.
3. Calculate the moles deposited from this volume: $6.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 0.25 \text{ dm}^3 = 1.5 \text{ mol}$.
4. Convert these moles to mass: $1.5 \text{ mol} \times 101 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 151.5 \text{ g}$.
Option C ($606.0 \text{ g}$) is tempting; the mistake behind it is forgetting to multiply the $6.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ drop in concentration by the actual volume of $0.25 \text{ dm}^3$, incorrectly assuming the sample volume is $1 \text{ dm}^3$ ($6.0 \times 101 = 606.0 \text{ g}$).

**8. According to the JAMB syllabus, blood, milk, fog, and emulsion paints are typical examples of**

- A. true solutions (particle size $< 1 \text{ nm}$)
- B. suspensions
- C. colloids
- D. solvents

**Answer: C.** 
1. Blood is a sol (solid cells in liquid plasma).
2. Milk is an emulsion (liquid fat in liquid water, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
3. Fog is an aerosol (liquid droplets in gas).
4. Emulsion paint is a liquid polymer dispersed in water.
5. All of these fall into the intermediate particle size category ($1 \text{ nm}$ to $1000 \text{ nm}$) and are strictly classified by JAMB as colloids.
Option B is tempting; the mistake behind it is assuming that because these mixtures are cloudy, their particles are large enough ($> 1000 \text{ nm}$) to settle out over time, which defines a suspension, not a colloid.

**9. Which of the following mixtures is strictly classified as a suspension in the JAMB syllabus?**

- A. Fog
- B. Rubber solution
- C. Harmattan haze
- D. Aerosol spray

**Answer: C.** 
1. A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture with particles larger than $1000 \text{ nm}$ that settle over time.
2. The JAMB syllabus strictly classifies Harmattan haze (dust particles heavily suspended in the air) and water paints as suspensions.

**10. The most suitable solvent for removing perspiration (sweat) stains from clothing is**

- A. Kerosene
- B. Turpentine
- C. Petrol
- D. Dilute ammonia solution ($\text{NH}_3$)

**Answer: D.** 
1. Perspiration contains traces of grease alongside organic acidic compounds like lactic acid.
2. A weak alkaline solvent is needed to neutralize the acids.
3. Dilute ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) solution neutralizes these acids and safely dissolves the residues without damaging the fabric.
Option C (Petrol) is tempting; the mistake behind it is assuming that perspiration is purely a heavy non-polar grease (like engine oil) that requires a strong hydrocarbon solvent, ignoring its acidic nature that is better treated with basic $\text{NH}_3$.
