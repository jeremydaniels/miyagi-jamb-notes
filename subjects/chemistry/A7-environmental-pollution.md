## Air and its composition

**Content a: The natural gaseous constituents and their proportion in the air**

The atmosphere is the envelope of gases surrounding the Earth. **Air** is the name given to the natural mixture of these gases. Although we cannot see it, air is matter; it has mass and occupies space. 

Dry, unpolluted air has a remarkably stable composition near the Earth's surface. The natural gaseous constituents and their approximate proportions by volume are:

- **Nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$):** $78.0\%$
- **Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$):** $21.0\%$
- **Argon ($\text{Ar}$) and other noble gases:** $0.9\%$
- **Carbon (IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$):** $0.03\%$
- **Water vapour ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$):** Variable (ranging from near $0\%$ in deserts to about $4\%$ in humid tropical areas)

*Textbook variation:* The global concentration of carbon (IV) oxide has risen slightly above $0.04\%$ in recent years due to human activities. However, $0.03\%$ is the standard value still taught in Nigerian secondary school textbooks and expected in JAMB exams. The noble gases (helium, neon, argon, krypton, and xenon) are present in trace amounts, with argon being by far the most abundant among them.

### Calculations involving the composition of air

Although there is no explicit calculation objective in this section, JAMB often tests your knowledge of air composition using simple volume calculations. These often involve finding the volume of oxygen removed or the residual volume of air after a reaction.

**Worked Example 1:** Calculate the volume of oxygen present in $200 \text{ cm}^3$ of dry air, assuming oxygen makes up $21.0\%$ by volume.
1. State the percentage of oxygen in dry air: $21.0\%$.
2. Write the formula: $\text{Volume of oxygen} = \frac{\text{Percentage of oxygen}}{100} \times \text{Total volume of air}$.
3. Substitute the values: $\text{Volume of oxygen} = \frac{21.0}{100} \times 200 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. Calculate the final answer: $42.0 \text{ cm}^3$.

**Worked Example 2:** A sample of $500 \text{ cm}^3$ of air is passed over repeatedly heated copper until no further change in volume occurs. Calculate the final volume of the residual gas. (Assume air contains $21.0\%$ oxygen by volume).
1. Understand the reaction: Heated copper reacts with oxygen to form solid copper(II) oxide ($\text{2Cu(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{2CuO(s)}$), effectively removing all oxygen gas from the air sample.
2. Calculate the volume of oxygen removed: $\frac{21.0}{100} \times 500 \text{ cm}^3 = 105.0 \text{ cm}^3$.
3. Subtract the volume of oxygen from the total initial volume to find the volume of the residual gas (mostly nitrogen and noble gases): $500 \text{ cm}^3 - 105.0 \text{ cm}^3 = 395.0 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. State the final answer: $395.0 \text{ cm}^3$.

**Worked Example 3:** What volume of carbon (IV) oxide is present in $10.0 \text{ dm}^3$ of unpolluted air? (Assume carbon (IV) oxide makes up $0.03\%$ by volume).
1. State the percentage of $\text{CO}_2$ in air: $0.03\%$.
2. Convert the volume of air from $\text{dm}^3$ to $\text{cm}^3$ to get a more manageable number for the final answer: $10.0 \text{ dm}^3 \times 1000 \text{ cm}^3\text{ dm}^{-3} = 10000 \text{ cm}^3$.
3. Calculate the volume of $\text{CO}_2$: $\frac{0.03}{100} \times 10000 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. Compute the result: $3.0 \text{ cm}^3$.

*Common mistake:* Many students forget that when passing air through an alkaline solution like sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) or potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$), only carbon (IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$) is absorbed, and because it is only $0.03\%$, the change in volume is very tiny. Do not confuse this with passing air over heated copper or phosphorus, which removes oxygen ($21.0\%$), causing a massive change in volume.

### Reasons for the existence of air as a mixture

**Objective i: give reason (s) for the existence of air as a mixture**

Students often wonder why air is classified as a mixture and not a compound. JAMB frequently tests this concept. Air is a mixture for the following reasons:

1. **Separation by physical methods:** The components of air can be separated using purely physical techniques, such as the fractional distillation of liquid air. Compounds require chemical reactions to be separated into their constituent elements.
2. **Variable composition:** The proportion of gases in air is not rigidly fixed. It varies slightly from place to place. For example, air in industrial cities has more carbon (IV) oxide than air in rural areas. Compounds, by contrast, always obey the Law of Definite Proportions, possessing a fixed mass ratio of elements.
3. **Retention of properties:** Each constituent gas in air retains its individual chemical properties. For instance, the oxygen in the air supports combustion and respiration, just as pure oxygen gas does. In a compound, the elements lose their original properties.
4. **No energy change on mixing:** If you mix pure nitrogen, oxygen, argon, and carbon (IV) oxide in the correct proportions, no heat or light is given out or absorbed, and air is formed. Forming a chemical compound usually involves a significant energy change (exothermic or endothermic reaction).
5. **No chemical formula:** Because the proportions vary, air cannot be represented by a single chemical formula like $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or $\text{NaCl}$.

### Principle involved in the separation of air components

**Objective ii: identify the principle involved in the separation of air components**

The industrial separation of air components relies on **fractional distillation of liquid air**. This physical process separates the mixture based on the **different boiling points** of the liquefied gases. It involves several distinct steps:

1. **Purification:** First, atmospheric air is filtered to remove dust particles. It is then passed through an alkali, usually aqueous sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH(aq)}$), to remove carbon (IV) oxide.
   $$2\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
   *Note:* If carbon (IV) oxide is not removed, it will freeze into solid "dry ice" at $-78\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ later in the cooling process and block the pipes. The air is also dried using silica gel or cold chambers to remove water vapour (which would freeze into ice).
2. **Liquefaction:** The purified air is subjected to high pressure (about $200$ atmospheres), which makes it hot. It is cooled by running cold water around the pipes. The compressed, cooled air is then forced to expand rapidly through a small valve. This sudden expansion causes a sharp drop in temperature (a phenomenon known as the Joule-Thomson effect). By repeatedly compressing, cooling, and expanding the air, its temperature drops until it turns into a pale blue liquid at about $-200\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
3. **Fractional distillation:** The liquid air is pumped into a tall fractionating column and allowed to warm up gradually. The gases boil off one by one as the temperature rises, according to their boiling points:
   - **Nitrogen** has the lowest boiling point ($-196\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), so it boils and vaporises first, moving to the top of the column where it is collected.
   - **Argon** boils next at $-186\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
   - **Oxygen** has the highest boiling point among the major components ($-183\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), so it boils last and remains as a liquid at the base of the column the longest.

### Reasons for the variation in the composition of air

**Objective iii: state reasons for the variation in the composition of air in the environment**

While the overall percentages of nitrogen and oxygen remain steady globally, local variations occur constantly due to natural processes and human activities:

- **Photosynthesis and Respiration:** In heavily forested regions during the day, plants take in large amounts of carbon (IV) oxide and release oxygen for photosynthesis, altering local percentages slightly. At night, or in areas with high animal and human populations, respiration increases carbon (IV) oxide levels and decreases oxygen.
- **Weather and Geography:** The amount of water vapour (humidity) varies greatly with location and weather. Coastal regions like Lagos or Port Harcourt have very high humidity (up to $4\%$ water vapour), while regions experiencing the Harmattan have extremely dry air.
- **Industrial and Human Activities:** Urban centers with heavy traffic, factories, and burning of fossil fuels experience increased levels of carbon (IV) oxide, carbon (II) oxide, sulphur (IV) oxide, and dust particles compared to rural farming areas.

### Uses of some of the constituents of air

**Objective iv: specify the uses of some of the constituents of air**

The separated components of air have vital industrial and medical applications:

- **Nitrogen:** Due to its unreactive (inert) nature, nitrogen is used in food packaging (like bags of plantain chips) to prevent oxidation and spoilage. It is the primary raw material for manufacturing ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) in the **Haber process**. Liquid nitrogen is used as a powerful refrigerant to freeze biological samples, such as animal semen for agricultural breeding.
- **Oxygen:** Essential for respiration, oxygen is supplied in cylinders to hospital patients with breathing difficulties. It is used alongside acetylene in the oxy-acetylene flame for welding and cutting metals (achieving temperatures up to $3000\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$). Huge quantities are also used in steel manufacturing to burn off carbon impurities from cast iron.
- **Noble Gases (especially Argon, Neon, and Helium):** 
  - **Argon** is an inert gas used to fill electric light bulbs. It prevents the hot tungsten filament from oxidising and burning away. It is also used to provide an inert atmosphere in welding.
  - **Neon** glows with a bright reddish-orange light when electricity passes through it in a vacuum tube, making it popular for advertising signs. 
  - **Helium** is used to fill weather balloons because it is much less dense than air and non-flammable. It is also mixed with oxygen for deep-sea diving to prevent the "bends".
- **Carbon (IV) oxide:** Solid carbon (IV) oxide (dry ice) is an excellent cooling agent because it sublimes without leaving a liquid residue. The gas is used in carbonated drinks (like sodas) to make them fizzy, and in fire extinguishers because it does not support combustion and is denser than air, allowing it to smother a fire.

## Environmental pollution: types and classification

### Different types of pollution and pollutants

**Objective v: identify the different types of pollution and pollutants**

**Pollution** is the introduction of harmful or poisonous substances into the natural environment in quantities that cause damage to human health, ecosystems, or property. 

A **pollutant** is the specific substance or form of energy that causes pollution. Pollutants can be chemical (like toxic gases), physical (like heat or noise), or biological (like disease-causing bacteria in sewage). 

Environmental pollution is broadly divided into three main types based on the medium affected:
1. **Air pollution:** Contamination of the atmosphere.
2. **Water pollution:** Contamination of water bodies (oceans, rivers, lakes, groundwater).
3. **Soil (Land) pollution:** Contamination and degradation of the earth's surface and soil.

### Classifying pollutants as biodegradable and non-biodegradable

**Objective vii: classify pollutants as biodegradable and non-biodegradable**

Pollutants are classified by how easily they break down in nature:

| Feature | Biodegradable Pollutants | Non-biodegradable Pollutants |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Pollutants that can be broken down into simpler, harmless substances by natural biological processes (usually by microorganisms like bacteria and fungi). | Pollutants that cannot be broken down by biological processes, or break down so slowly that they persist in the environment for decades or centuries. |
| **Danger level** | Usually less dangerous long-term, but can cause severe short-term problems (like depleting oxygen in water) if dumped in large, concentrated quantities. | Highly dangerous because they accumulate in the environment and in the bodies of living organisms over time. |
| **Examples** | Human sewage, animal dung, paper waste, food scraps, agricultural plant residues, wood. | Plastics (polythene bags, pure water sachets), glass, heavy metals (lead, mercury), synthetic pesticides (DDT), chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs). |

## Sources of pollutants

**Objective vi: specify different sources of pollutants**

Pollutants originate from two main categories of sources: natural and anthropogenic (human-made). While natural sources like volcanoes and dust storms contribute, human activities—such as industrialisation, agriculture, transportation, and poor domestic waste management—are the overwhelming sources of environmental pollutants today. 

Below, we detail the specific sources of pollutants for air, water, and soil.

### Air pollution and its examples

**Content b: Air pollution (Examples of air pollutants such as $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{CO}$, $\text{SO}_2$, oxides of nitrogen, chlorofluorocarbons and dust)**

Air pollution occurs when harmful gases and particles are released into the atmosphere. The key examples include:

1. **Carbon (II) oxide ($\text{CO}$):** 
   - *Sources:* The major source is the **incomplete combustion** of fossil fuels (petrol, diesel, coal, wood) where there is a limited supply of oxygen. This happens frequently in car exhaust systems, gas flaring, and small household petrol generators (locally called "I-pass-my-neighbour" in Nigeria).
2. **Sulphur (IV) oxide ($\text{SO}_2$):** 
   - *Sources:* Produced when sulphur-containing fossil fuels, particularly coal and some crude oils, are burned. It is also released during the roasting of sulphide ores (like copper pyrites, $\text{CuFeS}_2$) in extraction metallurgy.
3. **Hydrogen sulphide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$):** 
   - *Sources:* Known for its distinct "rotten egg" smell, it is released naturally by volcanic eruptions and the bacterial decay of organic matter containing sulphur (e.g., in swamps and sewers). Industrial sources include petroleum refineries and natural gas extraction.
4. **Oxides of nitrogen ($\text{NO}$ and $\text{NO}_2$, collectively called $\text{NO}_x$):** 
   - *Sources:* At the extremely high temperatures inside car engines and industrial furnaces, the normally unreactive nitrogen and oxygen in the air are forced to combine:
     $$\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{NO(g)}$$
     The nitrogen (II) oxide ($\text{NO}$) is then rapidly oxidised in the air to nitrogen (IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$), a toxic, reddish-brown gas. Lightning strikes also produce $\text{NO}_x$ naturally.
5. **Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs):** 
   - *Sources:* These are synthetic compounds containing carbon, chlorine, and fluorine. Historically, they were widely used as cooling fluids in refrigerators and air conditioners, and as propellants in aerosol spray cans (like perfumes and insecticides). 
6. **Dust and Particulates:** 
   - *Sources:* Fine solid particles suspended in the air. Natural sources include the seasonal Harmattan winds blowing dust from the Sahara into West Africa. Human sources include cement factories (such as those in Obajana or Ewekoro), mining, construction sites, and open burning of refuse.

### Water pollution: Sewage and oil pollution

**Content c: Water pollution (Sewage and oil pollution should be known)**

Water bodies become polluted when untreated waste is discharged into them.

1. **Sewage:** 
   - *Sources:* Sewage consists of domestic wastewater, human faeces, and urine. In many developing areas, untreated sewage is discharged directly into rivers, lakes, and lagoons. Sewage is a biodegradable pollutant rich in organic matter and nutrients (nitrates and phosphates), but it carries dangerous disease-causing bacteria (pathogens).
2. **Oil Pollution:** 
   - *Sources:* Accidental oil spills from ocean tankers, leaks from underwater pipelines, and illegal oil refining (bunkering). In Nigeria, the Niger Delta region suffers heavily from oil spillage which contaminates creeks, rivers, and the surrounding wetlands.
3. **Agricultural Runoff:** 
   - *Sources:* Rainwater washes artificial fertilizers (rich in nitrogen and phosphorus) and chemical pesticides from farmlands into nearby streams.
4. **Industrial Effluents:** 
   - *Sources:* Factories sometimes dump wastewater containing toxic heavy metals (like lead, mercury, and cadmium) or strongly acidic/alkaline chemicals into water bodies. Discharging extremely hot water from power cooling plants is also a form of pollution called **thermal pollution**, which lowers the solubility of oxygen in the water.

### Soil pollution: Oil spillage and other pollutants

**Content d: Soil pollution (Oil spillage, biodegradable and non-biodegradable pollutants)**

Soil pollution destroys the land's ability to support plant and animal life.

1. **Oil Spillage:** 
   - *Sources:* Just as in water, crude oil spills on land from burst pipelines severely degrade the soil. The thick oil coats soil particles, blocking air and water from reaching plant roots and soil microorganisms.
2. **Non-biodegradable Solid Waste:** 
   - *Sources:* Poor waste disposal leads to landscapes littered with polythene bags, plastic bottles, glass, and metal scraps. Because they do not decay, they choke the soil and prevent plant growth, while also serving as breeding grounds for mosquitoes when they trap water.
3. **Agricultural Chemicals:** 
   - *Sources:* The excessive and repeated application of chemical fertilizers, herbicides, and pesticides. While meant to boost crop yield, over time they can alter the soil pH (often making it too acidic) and kill beneficial soil organisms like earthworms and nitrogen-fixing bacteria.

## Effects of pollution on the environment

**Objective viii: specify the effects of pollution on the environment**

The consequences of releasing these pollutants are severe and affect a global scale. JAMB often tests your understanding of the mechanisms behind these environmental issues.

### 1. Acid Rain
Normal unpolluted rain is slightly acidic (pH $\approx 5.6$) because atmospheric carbon (IV) oxide dissolves in it to form weak trioxocarbonate (IV) acid. However, **sulphur (IV) oxide ($\text{SO}_2$)** and **nitrogen (IV) oxide ($\text{NO}_2$)** cause dangerous acid rain (pH less than $5$).
- **Mechanism:** $\text{SO}_2$ dissolves in rainwater to form tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) in a multi-step process, or weak trioxosulphate (IV) acid directly:
  $$\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3\text{(aq)}$$
  In the presence of sunlight and airborne particles, $\text{SO}_2$ is oxidised to $\text{SO}_3$, which dissolves to form strong acid:
  $$2\text{SO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{SO}_3\text{(g)}$$
  $$\text{SO}_3\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)}$$
- **Effects:** Acid rain corrodes metal structures (like bridges) and limestone/marble buildings. It enters lakes, lowering the pH to a level that kills fish and aquatic eggs. On land, it damages tree leaves and leaches vital nutrients (like calcium and magnesium) out of the soil, making it infertile.

### 2. Global Warming (The Greenhouse Effect)
The Earth receives short-wave radiation from the sun. The Earth's surface absorbs this energy and radiates it back into space as long-wave infrared radiation (heat). **Greenhouse gases**, primarily **carbon (IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$)**, methane ($\text{CH}_4$), and water vapour, trap this outgoing heat like the glass panels of a greenhouse. 
- **Effects:** As human activities release excessive $\text{CO}_2$ (mainly through burning fossil fuels and deforestation), too much heat is trapped. This raises the Earth's average temperature (global warming), leading to the melting of polar ice caps, rising sea levels (which threaten coastal cities like Lagos), and severe climate change.

### 3. Depletion of the Ozone Layer
The ozone layer is a region in the stratosphere containing ozone ($\text{O}_3$) gas. It acts as a protective shield, absorbing dangerous ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun. 
- **Mechanism:** **Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** drift up into the stratosphere. UV light breaks them apart, releasing highly reactive chlorine atoms. A single chlorine atom can catalyse the destruction of thousands of ozone molecules, creating a "hole" in the ozone layer.
- **Effects:** Increased UV radiation reaches the Earth's surface, causing higher rates of skin cancer, eye cataracts, and damage to crops and marine phytoplankton.

### 4. Eutrophication
This is a severe effect of water pollution caused by agricultural runoff and sewage.
- **Mechanism (Step-by-step):**
  1. Rain washes excess fertilizers (nitrates and phosphates) or untreated sewage into a river or lake.
  2. These nutrients act as fertilizer for algae in the water, causing a massive, rapid overgrowth called an **algal bloom**.
  3. The thick green layer of algae blocks sunlight from reaching underwater plants, causing them to die.
  4. The algae soon run out of nutrients and begin to die as well.
  5. Aerobic (oxygen-using) bacteria multiply rapidly as they decompose the massive amount of dead organic matter. In doing so, they consume almost all the dissolved oxygen in the water.
  6. **Effects:** Without dissolved oxygen, fish and other aquatic animals suffocate and die, turning the river into a lifeless, foul-smelling dead zone.

### 5. Health Hazards of Specific Pollutants
- **Carbon (II) oxide ($\text{CO}$):** This gas is silent, colourless, and odourless, making it very dangerous. When inhaled, it binds with haemoglobin in red blood cells much more strongly than oxygen does, forming a stable compound called **carboxyhaemoglobin**. This prevents the blood from carrying oxygen, causing dizziness, suffocation, and death.
- **Dust/Particulates:** Inhaling fine dust causes severe respiratory problems, such as asthma and bronchitis.
- **Hydrogen sulphide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$):** Irritates the eyes and respiratory tract, and is highly toxic in large concentrations.

### 6. Destruction of Ecosystems by Oil Spills
In the Niger Delta, oil spills coat the roots of mangrove trees, suffocating and killing them. Oil floating on water blocks atmospheric oxygen from dissolving into the water, and blocks sunlight from reaching aquatic plants, destroying the entire local aquatic food chain. Furthermore, oil makes farmlands toxic and barren, disrupting local farming economies.

## Measures for control of environmental pollution

**Objective ix: identify measures for control of environmental pollution**

Controlling pollution requires a mix of technology, legislation, and public education.

**Controlling Air Pollution:**
- **Catalytic Converters:** Modern cars are fitted with these devices in their exhaust systems. Using transition metal catalysts (like platinum and rhodium), they convert toxic gases into harmless ones before they leave the tailpipe:
  $$2\text{CO(g)} + 2\text{NO(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + \text{N}_2\text{(g)}$$
- **Unleaded fuel and low-sulphur coal:** Using fuels with lower sulphur content reduces $\text{SO}_2$ emissions. Phasing out leaded petrol prevents lead pollution.
- **Industrial scrubbers:** Factory chimneys can be fitted with chemical scrubbers (like calcium oxide, $\text{CaO}$) that react with and remove acidic gases like $\text{SO}_2$ before they reach the atmosphere.
- **Alternative energy:** Transitioning from fossil fuels to cleaner energy sources like solar, wind, and hydroelectric power.

**Controlling Water and Soil Pollution:**
- **Sewage Treatment:** Domestic waste must pass through treatment plants where organic matter is broken down safely by microorganisms, and pathogens are killed before the water is discharged into rivers.
- **Pipeline Maintenance and Legislation:** Strict laws must enforce standard maintenance of oil pipelines to prevent spills. In Nigeria, agencies like NESREA (National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency) are tasked with enforcing these regulations.
- **Recycling and Waste Management:** Non-biodegradable materials like plastics and glass should be recycled rather than dumped. Moving towards biodegradable alternatives, like paper bags instead of polythene, helps reduce soil pollution.
- **Bioremediation:** Using specific microorganisms that can consume and break down crude oil to clean up oil-polluted soils naturally.

| **Pollution** | The introduction of harmful substances into the environment in quantities that cause damage. |

| **Eutrophication** | The depletion of dissolved oxygen in a water body caused by algal blooms, which are triggered by excess nutrient runoff (fertilizers/sewage). |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Ozone Depletion vs Global Warming:** A very common student mistake is mixing these up. **Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** cause the depletion of the ozone layer. **Carbon (IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$)** causes the greenhouse effect/global warming. They are two different environmental problems.
- **CO vs CO₂ Toxicity:** Do not confuse carbon (II) oxide ($\text{CO}$) with carbon (IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$). $\text{CO}$ is an acute poison because it binds to haemoglobin. $\text{CO}_2$ is not highly toxic to breathe in normal amounts; its danger is its effect on global warming.
- **Boiling Points in Distillation:** Remember that negative temperatures work backwards! Nitrogen boils at $-196\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, which is a *lower* (colder) temperature than Oxygen at $-183\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Therefore, Nitrogen boils first.
- **Air is a Mixture:** JAMB frequently tests why air is not a compound. Remember the key points: no chemical formula, variable composition, and components can be separated by physical methods like fractional distillation.
- **Eutrophication triggers:** The primary culprits for eutrophication are nitrates and phosphates from agricultural runoff and untreated sewage.

## Quick summary

1. Air is a mixture because its components can be separated physically, its composition varies, and no heat is involved in its formation.
2. The main components of unpolluted dry air are nitrogen ($78.0\%$), oxygen ($21.0\%$), argon ($0.9\%$), and carbon (IV) oxide ($0.03\%$).
3. The components of air are separated industrially by the fractional distillation of liquid air, relying on their different boiling points.
4. Biodegradable pollutants (like sewage) decay naturally, while non-biodegradable pollutants (like plastics and heavy metals) accumulate dangerously in the environment.
5. Carbon (II) oxide ($\text{CO}$) from incomplete combustion binds to haemoglobin, causing suffocation.
6. Sulphur (IV) oxide ($\text{SO}_2$) and oxides of nitrogen ($\text{NO}_x$) are the primary causes of acid rain, which damages structures and aquatic ecosystems.
7. Eutrophication occurs when excess fertilizers cause an algal bloom, which eventually leads to the depletion of oxygen and the death of aquatic animals.
8. Catalytic converters in cars reduce air pollution by converting toxic $\text{CO}$ and $\text{NO}$ into safe $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{N}_2$.

## Practice questions

**1. Calculate the volume of oxygen gas present in $500 \text{ cm}^3$ of unpolluted dry air.**
- A. $50.0 \text{ cm}^3$
- B. $105.0 \text{ cm}^3$
- C. $390.0 \text{ cm}^3$
- D. $395.0 \text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: B.** 
1. State the percentage of oxygen in dry air: $21.0\%$.
2. Write the formula: $\text{Volume} = \frac{\text{Percentage}}{100} \times \text{Total volume}$.
3. Substitute the values: $\text{Volume of oxygen} = \frac{21.0}{100} \times 500 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. Calculate the answer: $105.0 \text{ cm}^3$.
The mistake in option D assumes you are calculating the volume of the residual gas ($500 \text{ cm}^3 - 105.0 \text{ cm}^3 = 395.0 \text{ cm}^3$) left after removing oxygen.

**2. A sample of air with a volume of $250 \text{ cm}^3$ is repeatedly passed over heated copper until there is no further change in volume. What is the expected final volume of the residual gas?**
- A. $52.5 \text{ cm}^3$
- B. $197.5 \text{ cm}^3$
- C. $150.0 \text{ cm}^3$
- D. $250.0 \text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Identify that heated copper reacts with and removes the oxygen ($21.0\%$) from the air.
2. Calculate the volume of oxygen removed: $\frac{21.0}{100} \times 250 \text{ cm}^3 = 52.5 \text{ cm}^3$.
3. Subtract the volume of oxygen from the total volume to find the residual gas: $250 \text{ cm}^3 - 52.5 \text{ cm}^3 = 197.5 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. State the answer: $197.5 \text{ cm}^3$.
The mistake in option A is calculating the volume of the oxygen removed, not the residual gas left behind.

**3. If $10.0 \text{ dm}^3$ of air is passed through aqueous sodium hydroxide, what volume of gas is absorbed? (Assume unpolluted air).**
- A. $3.0 \text{ cm}^3$
- B. $210.0 \text{ cm}^3$
- C. $2100.0 \text{ cm}^3$
- D. $300.0 \text{ cm}^3$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Identify that aqueous sodium hydroxide only absorbs carbon (IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$) from the air.
2. State the percentage of $\text{CO}_2$ in air: $0.03\%$.
3. Convert the total volume to $\text{cm}^3$: $10.0 \text{ dm}^3 \times 1000 \text{ cm}^3\text{ dm}^{-3} = 10000 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. Calculate the volume of $\text{CO}_2$: $\frac{0.03}{100} \times 10000 \text{ cm}^3 = 3.0 \text{ cm}^3$.
The mistake in option C is calculating for oxygen ($21.0\%$) instead of carbon (IV) oxide ($0.03\%$).

**4. The industrial separation of the components of air is based on the principle of:**
- A. simple distillation.
- B. fractional distillation of liquid air.
- C. fractional crystallization.
- D. filtration.

**Answer: B.** After air is liquefied by intense cooling and compression, the components are separated by fractional distillation based on their slightly different boiling points. The mistake in option A is choosing simple distillation, which is used for separating a liquid from a solid or liquids with widely different boiling points, not miscible liquefied gases.

**5. Which of the following air pollutants is most strongly associated with the depletion of the ozone layer?**
- A. Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)
- B. Carbon (II) oxide
- C. Sulphur (IV) oxide
- D. Hydrogen sulphide

**Answer: A.** Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) rise to the stratosphere where UV light breaks them down, releasing chlorine atoms that destroy ozone molecules. The mistake behind option B or C is confusing ozone depletion with global warming or acid rain.

**6. A major source of carbon (II) oxide in the atmosphere is:**
- A. complete combustion of fossil fuels.
- B. incomplete combustion of fossil fuels.
- C. photosynthesis in plants.
- D. respiration by animals.

**Answer: B.** Carbon (II) oxide ($\text{CO}$) is produced when fossil fuels are burned in a limited supply of oxygen (incomplete combustion). The mistake behind option A is that complete combustion produces carbon (IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$), not carbon (II) oxide.

**7. Which of the following is a non-biodegradable pollutant?**
- A. Sewage
- B. Plant residues
- C. Animal dung
- D. Polythene bags

**Answer: D.** Non-biodegradable materials cannot be broken down by microorganisms. Polythene bags are synthetic plastics that cannot be broken down naturally. The mistake behind the other options is that sewage, dung, and plant residues are organic and easily broken down by bacteria (biodegradable).

**8. Sulphur (IV) oxide in the atmosphere is harmful primarily because it:**
- A. causes global warming.
- B. causes acid rain.
- C. depletes the ozone layer.
- D. reduces oxygen in the blood.

**Answer: B.** Sulphur (IV) oxide ($\text{SO}_2$) dissolves in rainwater and undergoes oxidation to form strong tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid, causing acid rain. The mistake in option A is confusing it with $\text{CO}_2$, and in option D confusing it with $\text{CO}$.

**9. Sewage pollution in water bodies is dangerous because it:**
- A. increases the dissolved oxygen level.
- B. introduces heavy metals like lead.
- C. causes depletion of dissolved oxygen and death of aquatic life.
- D. makes the water alkaline.

**Answer: C.** Sewage introduces massive amounts of organic matter. Bacteria decomposing the sewage consume the dissolved oxygen, suffocating aquatic life (eutrophication process). The mistake in option B is that heavy metals are typically introduced by industrial effluents, not domestic sewage.

**10. Which of the following represents an effective measure to control air pollution from car exhausts?**
- A. Increasing the use of leaded petrol.
- B. Open burning of refuse.
- C. Installing catalytic converters.
- D. Discharging untreated sewage into rivers.

**Answer: C.** Catalytic converters help control air pollution by catalysing the reaction between toxic exhaust gases ($\text{CO}$ and $\text{NO}$) to form harmless gases ($\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{N}_2$). The mistake in the other options is that they actively cause or worsen environmental pollution.
