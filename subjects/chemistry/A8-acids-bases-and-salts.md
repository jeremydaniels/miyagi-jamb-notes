## General characteristics, properties and uses of acids, bases and salts

Acids, bases, and salts form the foundation of countless chemical reactions in laboratories, industries, and biological systems.

### Properties of acids and bases

Acids and bases are chemical opposites. When they react together, they cancel each other out in a process called neutralization.

| Property | Acids | Bases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Taste** | Sour (e.g., unripe citrus fruits, vinegar) | Bitter (e.g., soap, bitter leaf) |
| **Touch** | Can be corrosive (burns the skin) | Soapy and slippery to the touch |
| **Effect on Litmus** | Turns blue litmus paper red | Turns red litmus paper blue |
| **$\text{pH}$ range** | Less than 7 ($\text{pH} < 7$) | Greater than 7 ($\text{pH} > 7$) |
| **Reaction with metals** | Reacts with active metals (like $\text{Zn}, \text{Mg}$) to liberate hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) | Do not typically react with metals to give hydrogen gas |
| **Reaction with trioxocarbonates(IV)** | Liberates carbon(IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$) gas | No reaction |
| **Reaction with ammonium salts** | No reaction | Liberates ammonia gas ($\text{NH}_3$) when heated |

**Everyday and industrial uses:**
- **Acids:** Tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) is used in car batteries and fertilizers. Hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$) cleans metals and digests food in the stomach. Ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) is used as vinegar.
- **Bases:** Sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$, caustic soda) is heavily used in Nigeria for soap-making. Calcium hydroxide ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$, slaked lime) neutralizes acidic soils. Magnesium hydroxide ($\text{Mg(OH)}_2$) is used in antacids.
- **Salts:** Sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) is common table salt. Calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) ($\text{CaCO}_3$) is limestone, used for cement. Aluminium tetraoxosulphate(VI) ($\text{Al}_2\text{(SO}_4\text{)}_3$) is used for water treatment.

### Types of acids and bases

**Definitions of an Acid**
Scientists have defined acids in slightly different ways over time:
1. **Arrhenius theory:** An acid is a substance which, when dissolved in water, produces the **hydroxonium ion ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$)** or hydrogen ion ($\text{H}^+$) as the only positive ion.
   $$\text{HCl(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{H}_3\text{O}^+\text{(aq)} + \text{Cl}^-\text{(aq)}$$
2. **Brønsted-Lowry theory:** An acid is defined as a **proton donor** (it gives away an $\text{H}^+$ ion), and a base is a proton acceptor. This definition does not require water to be present.

**Inorganic (Mineral) vs. Organic Acids**
- **Inorganic Acids:** Prepared from mineral elements. Examples are hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$), tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), and trioxonitrate(V) acid ($\text{HNO}_3$).
- **Naturally Occurring Organic Acids:** Found in plant and animal materials. Examples include **ethanoic acid** ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) in vinegar, **citric acid** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_8\text{O}_7$) in oranges and lemons, and **tartaric acid** ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_6\text{O}_6$) in grapes, tamarind (tsamiya), and baking powder.

**Types of Bases**
A base neutralizes an acid to form a salt and water only (e.g., metal oxides and hydroxides).
- **Alkalis:** An alkali is a base that is **soluble in water**. Examples include sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) and potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$). All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.

### Basicity of acids

The **basicity** of an acid is the number of replaceable hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) in one molecule of the acid. It dictates how many moles of a base are needed for complete neutralization.

1. **Monobasic acids (Basicity = 1):** Produce one replaceable hydrogen ion per molecule.
   - Hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$)
   - Trioxonitrate(V) acid ($\text{HNO}_3$)
   - **Ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$):** Notice it has four hydrogen atoms, but only the one attached to the oxygen in the carboxyl group ($-\text{COOH}$) is replaceable.
2. **Dibasic acids (Basicity = 2):** Produce two replaceable hydrogen ions.
   - Tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$)
3. **Tribasic acids (Basicity = 3):** Produce three replaceable hydrogen ions.
   - Tetraoxophosphate(V) acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$)

> **Common mistake:** Assuming basicity equals the total number of hydrogen atoms in the formula.
> *Wrong formula:* Ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) has 4 hydrogen atoms, so basicity = 4.
> *Corrected version:* Only the hydrogen atom in the $-\text{COOH}$ group is ionizable, so the basicity = 1.

### Acidity and alkalinity indicators

**Acid-base indicators** are organic dyes that change colour depending on whether they are in an acidic or basic (alkaline) environment.

| Indicator | Colour in Acid | Colour in Base | Colour at Neutral |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Litmus** | Red | Blue | Purple |
| **Methyl orange** | Red / Pink | Yellow | Orange |
| **Phenolphthalein** | Colourless | Pink / Red | Colourless / Pale pink |

### Preparation of salts

The method used to prepare a salt depends on whether it is soluble or insoluble in water.

**1. Preparation of Soluble Salts**
- **Action of dilute acids on metals (Displacement):** 
  Reactive metals react with dilute acids to form a salt and hydrogen gas.
  $$\text{Zn(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$
- **Neutralization (Acid + Alkali):** 
  Titration is used for salts of very active metals (like $\text{Na}, \text{K}$).
  $$\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
- **Oxides and trioxocarbonate (IV) salts:** 
  An acid is warmed with an insoluble base (metal oxide).
  $$\text{CuO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
  Alternatively, an acid reacts with a trioxocarbonate(IV), liberating $\text{CO}_2$ gas.
  $$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} + 2\text{HNO}_3\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Ca(NO}_3\text{)}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$

**2. Preparation of Insoluble Salts**
- **Precipitation (Double Decomposition):**
  Aqueous solutions of two soluble salts are mixed. They exchange ions to form one soluble salt and one insoluble salt (the precipitate).
  $$\text{AgNO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{NaCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{AgCl(s)} + \text{NaNO}_3\text{(aq)}$$

### Classification of salts

A salt is formed when all or part of the replaceable hydrogen ions of an acid are replaced by metallic ions (or ammonium ions).

1. **Normal Salts:** Formed when *all* replaceable hydrogen ions are completely replaced. E.g., $\text{NaCl}, \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$.
2. **Acidic Salts:** Formed when a polybasic acid is only *partially* neutralized. They contain replaceable hydrogen ions. E.g., Sodium hydrogentetraoxosulphate(VI) ($\text{NaHSO}_4$).
3. **Basic Salts:** Formed when there is insufficient acid to neutralize a base. They contain replaceable hydroxide ($\text{OH}^-$) ions. E.g., Zinc hydroxychloride ($\text{Zn(OH)Cl}$).
4. **Double salts and alums:** A **double salt** crystallizes out from a mixed solution of two simple salts and ionizes completely in water. **Alums** are typical double salts. For example, potash alum ($\text{KAl(SO}_4\text{)}_2\cdot 12\text{H}_2\text{O}$) yields $\text{K}^+$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$, and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ions in water.

## Conductance of strong and weak acids and bases

The behaviour of acids and bases in water governs how well they conduct electricity.

### Degree of dissociation and strength

The strength of an acid or base depends on its **degree of dissociation** ($\alpha$)—the fraction of its molecules that break apart into ions in water.

- **Strong acids and strong bases:** They **dissociate completely**. Practically every molecule breaks into ions.
  - *Examples:* $\text{HCl}, \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4, \text{NaOH}, \text{KOH}$.
- **Weak acids and weak bases:** They **dissociate only partially**. Most of the substance remains as whole molecules.
  - *Examples:* Ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$), aqueous ammonia ($\text{NH}_3\text{(aq)}$).

### Degree of dissociation and conductance

Electrical conductance in a solution depends directly on the **amount of ions present**. 

- **High Conductance:** Because a strong acid like $\text{HCl}$ dissociates completely, a $1.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution contains massive amounts of free ions, acting as a **strong electrolyte** and conducting electricity excellently.
- **Low Conductance:** Because a weak acid like ethanoic acid dissociates partially, a $1.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution contains mostly neutral molecules and very few ions, acting as a **weak electrolyte** with poor conductance.

## The pH and pOH scale

The $\text{pH}$ scale measures the acidity or alkalinity of a solution, usually ranging from 0 to 14. 
- $\text{pH} < 7$ is acidic.
- $\text{pH} = 7$ is neutral (e.g., pure water).
- $\text{pH} > 7$ is alkaline (basic).

Formulas you must know:
- $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$
- $\text{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-]$
- $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14$ (at $25^\circ\text{C}$)

### Calculations on pH and pOH

> **Common mistake:** Forgetting to account for basicity when calculating the $\text{pH}$ of a strong dibasic acid like $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
> *Wrong working:* $[\text{H}^+] = 0.05 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$, so $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(0.05) = 1.3$.
> *Corrected version:* $[\text{H}^+] = 2 \times 0.05 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$, so $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(0.1) = 1.0$.

**Worked Example 1: Strong Acid $\text{pH}$**
Calculate the $\text{pH}$ of a $0.05 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution of tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
1. Write the dissociation equation: $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow 2\text{H}^+ + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$.
2. Determine $[\text{H}^+]$: Since one mole of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ gives two moles of $\text{H}^+$, multiply the concentration by $2$.
3. $[\text{H}^+] = 2 \times 0.05 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
4. Calculate $\text{pH}$ using $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$.
5. $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(0.1) = 1.0$ (Note: $\text{pH}$ is a ratio and has no unit).

**Worked Example 2: Strong Base $\text{pOH}$ and $\text{pH}$**
Calculate the $\text{pOH}$ and $\text{pH}$ of a $0.001 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution of sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$).
1. Write the dissociation equation: $\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{OH}^-$.
2. Determine $[\text{OH}^-]$: $[\text{OH}^-] = 0.001 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 10^{-3} \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
3. Calculate $\text{pOH}$ using $\text{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-]$.
4. $\text{pOH} = -\log_{10}(10^{-3}) = 3.0$ (no unit).
5. Calculate $\text{pH}$ using $\text{pH} = 14 - \text{pOH}$.
6. $\text{pH} = 14 - 3.0 = 11.0$.

**Worked Example 3: Finding Concentration from $\text{pH}$**
The $\text{pH}$ of a fruit juice is $4.5$. What is its hydrogen ion concentration?
1. Use the $\text{pH}$ formula: $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$.
2. Rearrange the formula: $[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-\text{pH}}$.
3. Substitute the given $\text{pH}$ value: $[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-4.5} \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
4. Calculate the final answer: $[\text{H}^+] = 3.16 \times 10^{-5} \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.

### Significance of pH in everyday life

The control of $\text{pH}$ is vital in nature and industry:
1. **Agriculture:** Crops grow best within specific $\text{pH}$ ranges. Farmers add slaked lime ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) to neutralize overly acidic soils.
2. **The Human Body:** The stomach produces $\text{HCl}$ to maintain a highly acidic environment ($\text{pH} \approx 2$) for digestion. Human blood must be strictly maintained at a slightly alkaline $\text{pH}$ of about $7.4$.
3. **Tooth Decay:** Bacteria in the mouth ferment sugars to produce acids. When the mouth's $\text{pH}$ drops below 5.5, tooth enamel decays. Toothpaste is alkaline to neutralize this.
4. **Water Treatment:** In public water works, the $\text{pH}$ is adjusted to neutral so it does not corrode metal pipes.

### Applications of buffer solutions

A **buffer solution** is a solution that resists changes in its $\text{pH}$ when small amounts of an acid or a base are added to it.
- **Acidic Buffer:** Made from a weak acid and its salt (e.g., Ethanoic acid + Sodium ethanoate: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ and $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$).
- **Basic Buffer:** Made from a weak base and its salt (e.g., Aqueous ammonia + Ammonium chloride: $\text{NH}_3\text{(aq)}$ and $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$).

**Applications:**
1. **Blood:** Buffered by the $\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 / \text{HCO}_3^-$ system to keep the $\text{pH}$ safely around $7.4$.
2. **Manufacturing:** Crucial in fermentation, brewing, and electroplating industries.
3. **Agriculture:** Soil naturally acts as a buffer, protecting roots from acid rain.

## Acid and base titrations

Titrimetric analysis (titration) is a laboratory method used to determine the unknown concentration of an acid or base by neutralizing it exactly with a standard solution.

### Identifying appropriate acid-base indicators

During a titration, you must select an indicator whose colour change occurs at the exact $\text{pH}$ where the neutralization completes (the equivalence point).

| Titration Type | Equivalent Point $\text{pH}$ | Appropriate Indicator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Strong Acid vs Strong Base** ($\text{HCl}$ vs $\text{NaOH}$) | $\text{pH} = 7$ | Any (Litmus, Methyl orange, Phenolphthalein) |
| **Strong Acid vs Weak Base** ($\text{HCl}$ vs $\text{NH}_3\text{(aq)}$) | Acidic ($\text{pH} < 7$) | **Methyl orange** |
| **Weak Acid vs Strong Base** ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ vs $\text{NaOH}$) | Basic ($\text{pH} > 7$) | **Phenolphthalein** |
| **Weak Acid vs Weak Base** | No sharp change | **None** (use a $\text{pH}$ meter) |

### Graphical representation of titration curves

A titration curve plots the $\text{pH}$ of the solution in the flask against the volume of acid or base added from the burette. The steep, vertical part of the curve shows where the $\text{pH}$ changes drastically with a single drop.

### Calculations based on the mole concept (titration)

Titrations rely on this fundamental formula:
$$\frac{C_A V_A}{C_B V_B} = \frac{n_A}{n_B}$$

Where:
- $C_A, C_B$ = Molar concentration of acid/base ($\text{mol dm}^{-3}$)
- $V_A, V_B$ = Volume of acid/base used ($\text{cm}^3$)
- $n_A, n_B$ = Number of moles from the balanced equation

> **Common mistake:** Ignoring the mole ratio in titrations involving $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ or $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$.
> *Wrong working:* $C_A = \frac{0.100 \times 25.0}{20.0} = 0.125 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ (assuming $1:1$ ratio).
> *Corrected version:* $C_A = \frac{0.100 \times 25.0 \times 2}{20.0 \times 1} = 0.250 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ (using the correct $2:1$ ratio).

**Worked Example 1: Finding concentration**
A student titrates $25.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of $0.100 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ sodium trioxocarbonate(IV) ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) with hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$). If $20.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of the acid is required for complete neutralization, calculate the concentration of the acid.
1. Write the balanced equation:
   $$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\text{(aq)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$
2. Identify the mole ratio from the equation: $n_A = 2$ (for acid), $n_B = 1$ (for base).
3. Identify the given variables: $C_B = 0.100 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$, $V_B = 25.0 \text{ cm}^3$, $V_A = 20.0 \text{ cm}^3$.
4. Apply the titration formula: $\frac{C_A V_A}{C_B V_B} = \frac{n_A}{n_B}$.
5. Rearrange to solve for $C_A$: $C_A = \frac{C_B \times V_B \times n_A}{V_A \times n_B}$.
6. Substitute the values: $C_A = \frac{0.100 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 25.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 2}{20.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 1}$.
7. Calculate the final answer: $C_A = \frac{5.0}{20.0} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.250 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.

**Worked Example 2: Using Mass Concentration**
A $250 \text{ cm}^3$ solution contains $1.00 \text{ g}$ of sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$). $25.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of this solution neutralizes $20.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$). Calculate the molar concentration of the acid. ($\text{H}=1, \text{O}=16, \text{Na}=23$).
1. Find the molar mass of $\text{NaOH}$: $23 + 16 + 1 = 40 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
2. Find the mass concentration of the base in $1 \text{ dm}^3$: $\frac{1.00 \text{ g}}{250 \text{ cm}^3} \times 1000 \text{ cm}^3 = 4.00 \text{ g dm}^{-3}$.
3. Find the molar concentration ($C_B$): $C_B = \frac{4.00 \text{ g dm}^{-3}}{40 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.100 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
4. Write the balanced equation: $2\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$. Here, $n_A = 1$ and $n_B = 2$.
5. Rearrange titration formula for $C_A$: $C_A = \frac{C_B \times V_B \times n_A}{V_A \times n_B}$.
6. Substitute values: $C_A = \frac{0.100 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 25.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 1}{20.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 2}$.
7. Calculate answer: $C_A = \frac{2.5}{40.0} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.0625 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.

**Worked Example 3: Percentage Purity**
$2.00 \text{ g}$ of impure potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$) was dissolved in water and made up to $250 \text{ cm}^3$. $25.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of this solution neutralized $15.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of $0.100 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \text{ HNO}_3$. Calculate the percentage purity of the $\text{KOH}$. ($\text{K}=39, \text{O}=16, \text{H}=1$).
1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{KOH(aq)} + \text{HNO}_3\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{KNO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ ($n_A = 1, n_B = 1$).
2. Find $C_B$: $C_B = \frac{C_A \times V_A \times n_B}{V_B \times n_A} = \frac{0.100 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 15.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 1}{25.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 1} = 0.060 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
3. Find the mass concentration of pure $\text{KOH}$: Molar mass $= 39 + 16 + 1 = 56 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$. Mass conc $= 0.060 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 56 \text{ g mol}^{-1} = 3.36 \text{ g dm}^{-3}$.
4. Find the pure mass in the $250 \text{ cm}^3$ flask: $3.36 \text{ g dm}^{-3} \times \frac{250 \text{ cm}^3}{1000 \text{ cm}^3} = 0.84 \text{ g}$.
5. Calculate percentage purity: $\left(\frac{0.84 \text{ g}}{2.00 \text{ g}}\right) \times 100\% = 42.0\%$.

## Hydrolysis of salts overview

**Salt hydrolysis** is a reaction in which the ions of a salt react with water to produce an acidic or basic solution. Salts from strong acids and strong bases (like $\text{NaCl}$) do not hydrolyse and remain strictly neutral.

### Balancing equations for the hydrolysis of salts

When writing the equations, show the weak ion reacting with water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$):

1. **Ammonium chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$):**
   $$\text{NH}_4^+\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+\text{(aq)}$$
2. **Aluminium chloride ($\text{AlCl}_3$):**
   $$\text{Al}^{3+}\text{(aq)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{Al(OH)}_3\text{(s)} + 3\text{H}^+\text{(aq)}$$
3. **Sodium trioxocarbonate(IV) ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$):**
   $$\text{CO}_3^{2-}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{HCO}_3^-\text{(aq)} + \text{OH}^-\text{(aq)}$$
4. **Sodium ethanoate ($\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$):**
   $$\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COOH(aq)} + \text{OH}^-\text{(aq)}$$

### Properties of resultant solutions from hydrolysis

By looking at the products of the hydrolysis equations, we can deduce if the resulting solution is acidic (extra $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) or basic (extra $\text{OH}^-$). 

| Type of Salt | Example | Hydrolysis Result | Nature of Solution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong Acid + Strong Base | $\text{NaCl}, \text{KNO}_3$ | No hydrolysis | **Neutral** ($\text{pH} = 7$) |
| Strong Acid + Weak Base | $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}, \text{AlCl}_3$ | Produces $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ | **Acidic** ($\text{pH} < 7$) |
| Weak Acid + Strong Base | $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3, \text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$ | Produces $\text{OH}^-$ | **Basic / Alkaline** ($\text{pH} > 7$) |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Acid** | A substance that produces hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) or hydroxonium ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) as the only positive ion in aqueous solution. |
| **Base** | A substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only. |
| **Alkali** | A base that is soluble in water. |
| **Basicity** | The number of replaceable hydrogen ions in one molecule of an acid. |
| **Indicator** | An organic dye that changes colour depending on the $\text{pH}$ (acidity or alkalinity) of a solution. |
| **Salt** | A compound formed when all or part of the replaceable hydrogen ions of an acid are replaced by a metal or ammonium ion. |
| **Double salt** | A salt containing two different positive ions (other than hydrogen) that ionize completely in solution (e.g., alums). |
| **Buffer solution** | A solution that resists changes in $\text{pH}$ when a small amount of acid or base is added. |
| **Salt hydrolysis** | A reaction in which the ions of a salt react with water to form an acidic or basic solution. |
| **Degree of dissociation** | The fraction of molecules of a substance that separate into ions in an aqueous solution. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mistake on basicity:** Assuming ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) has a basicity of $4$. Its basicity is exactly $1$.
- **Mistake in $\text{pH}$ calculations:** For dibasic acids like $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, you must multiply the molar concentration by $2$ to get $[\text{H}^+]$.
- **Mistake in titration formula:** Inverting the mole ratio in the formula. Always ensure $n_A$ (moles of acid) corresponds to $C_A V_A$ in the numerator.
- **Tip on indicators:** Strong Acid + Weak Base = Acidic Salt (use **Methyl orange**). Weak Acid + Strong Base = Basic Salt (use **Phenolphthalein**).
- **Tip on salt types:** Acidic salts always have an 'H' in their formula (e.g., $\text{NaHSO}_4$) showing replaceable hydrogen. Basic salts often have an 'OH' (e.g., $\text{Zn(OH)Cl}$).

## Quick summary

1. Acids are proton donors ($\text{pH} < 7$), whereas bases are proton acceptors ($\text{pH} > 7$). Soluble bases are called alkalis.
2. The basicity of an acid is the number of its replaceable hydrogen ions (e.g., $\text{HCl}$ is monobasic, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is dibasic, $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$ is tribasic).
3. Strong acids and bases dissociate completely in water (giving high electrical conductance), while weak acids and bases dissociate partially (low conductance).
4. Soluble salts are prepared by neutralization or action of acids on metals. Insoluble salts are prepared by double decomposition (precipitation).
5. The $\text{pH}$ scale calculates acidity: $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$. Buffer solutions resist changes to this $\text{pH}$.
6. Salts from a strong acid and weak base hydrolyse to form acidic solutions ($\text{pH} < 7$); salts from a weak acid and strong base form alkaline solutions ($\text{pH} > 7$).
7. In titration calculations, always start with a balanced equation to find the exact mole ratio ($n_A : n_B$) before applying the formula $\frac{C_A V_A}{C_B V_B} = \frac{n_A}{n_B}$.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following is a characteristic property of an acid?**
- A. It turns red litmus paper blue
- B. It reacts with trioxocarbonates(IV) to liberate carbon(IV) oxide gas
- C. It reacts with ammonium salts to liberate ammonia gas
- D. It feels soapy and slippery to the touch

**Answer: B.** 1. Acids react with trioxocarbonates(IV) to form a salt, water, and $\text{CO}_2$ gas. 2. Therefore, option B correctly states an acid property. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, A, is confusing acids with bases; bases turn red litmus blue, while acids turn blue litmus red.

**2. Ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) is classified as a weak acid because it:**
- A. is a naturally occurring organic acid
- B. is highly concentrated in solution
- C. dissociates completely in aqueous solution
- D. partially dissociates into ions in aqueous solution

**Answer: D.** 1. The strength of an acid is determined by its degree of dissociation, $\alpha$. 2. Weak acids like $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ only partially dissociate in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, producing few $\text{H}^+$ ions. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, C, is confusing weak acids with strong acids; strong acids (like $\text{HCl}$) dissociate completely, not weak acids.

**3. What is the basicity of tetraoxophosphate(V) acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$)?**
- A. $1$
- B. $2$
- C. $3$
- D. $4$

**Answer: C.** 1. Basicity is the number of replaceable $\text{H}^+$ ions in one molecule of the acid. 2. One molecule of $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$ yields $3$ replaceable $\text{H}^+$ ions, making it tribasic. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, D, is counting all oxygen atoms ($4$) instead of the ionizable hydrogen atoms.

**4. An insoluble salt like silver chloride ($\text{AgCl}$) is best prepared by:**
- A. neutralizing an acid with a base
- B. the action of an acid on a metal
- C. double decomposition (precipitation)
- D. heating a trioxocarbonate(IV) salt

**Answer: C.** 1. Soluble salts can be prepared by neutralization or action of an acid on a metal. 2. Insoluble salts like $\text{AgCl}$ must be prepared by double decomposition, mixing two soluble salts to form a precipitate. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, A, is applying the method for soluble salts of active metals (neutralization) to an insoluble salt.

**5. Potassium aluminium tetraoxosulphate(VI) dodecahydrate ($\text{KAl(SO}_4\text{)}_2\cdot 12\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is an example of:**
- A. a basic salt
- B. an acidic salt
- C. a double salt
- D. a complex salt

**Answer: C.** 1. A double salt contains two different positive ions (here, $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{Al}^{3+}$) and ionizes completely into simple ions. 2. Alums are classic examples of double salts. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, D, is confusing a double salt with a complex salt; double salts break down completely into simple ions in water, while complex salts do not.

**6. A $1.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution of hydrochloric acid has a higher electrical conductance than a $1.0 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution of ethanoic acid because hydrochloric acid:**
- A. has a higher molar mass
- B. is fully dissociated into ions
- C. is an organic acid
- D. contains more water molecules

**Answer: B.** 1. Electrical conductance depends on the amount of mobile ions in the solution. 2. $\text{HCl}$ is a strong acid and is fully dissociated into $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, providing high conductance. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, A, is assuming that molar mass dictates conductance; conductance is strictly dictated by the concentration of free ions, not the mass of the molecules.

**7. Calculate the $\text{pH}$ of a $0.005 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution of tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid, assuming complete dissociation.**
- A. $1.0$
- B. $2.0$
- C. $2.3$
- D. $3.0$

**Answer: B.** 1. Write the dissociation: $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow 2\text{H}^+ + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$. 2. Calculate $[\text{H}^+]$: $2 \times 0.005 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.010 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$. 3. Calculate $\text{pH}$: $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(0.010) = 2.0$ (no unit for $\text{pH}$). The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, C, is forgetting to multiply the acid concentration by $2$ for a dibasic acid, which gives $-\log_{10}(0.005) = 2.3$.

**8. A solution that resists changes in $\text{pH}$ when a small amount of acid or base is added is known as:**
- A. a normal solution
- B. a buffer solution
- C. a neutral solution
- D. a standardized solution

**Answer: B.** 1. By definition, a buffer solution maintains a relatively constant $\text{pH}$. 2. For example, a mixture of $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ and $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$ forms an acidic buffer. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, C, is confusing a buffer with a neutral solution; a neutral solution ($\text{pH} = 7$) does not resist $\text{pH}$ changes when acid or base is added, whereas a buffer does.

**9. Which of the following salts will dissolve in water to give an acidic solution?**
- A. Ammonium chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$)
- B. Sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$)
- C. Sodium ethanoate ($\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$)
- D. Potassium trioxonitrate(V) ($\text{KNO}_3$)

**Answer: A.** 1. $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ is formed from a strong acid ($\text{HCl}$) and a weak base ($\text{NH}_3$). 2. The $\text{NH}_4^+$ ion hydrolyses in water to produce $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ ions, making the solution acidic ($\text{pH} < 7$). The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, C, is choosing the salt of a weak acid and strong base, which undergoes hydrolysis to produce an alkaline solution, not an acidic one.

**10. $25.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of $0.050 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ sodium trioxocarbonate(IV) requires $20.0 \text{ cm}^3$ of hydrochloric acid for complete neutralization. What is the concentration of the acid?**
- A. $0.031 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- B. $0.063 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- C. $0.125 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$
- D. $0.250 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

**Answer: C.** 1. Write the balanced equation: $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$. 2. Identify moles: $n_A = 2$ (acid), $n_B = 1$ (base). 3. Apply formula: $C_A = \frac{C_B \times V_B \times n_A}{V_A \times n_B} = \frac{0.050 \text{ mol dm}^{-3} \times 25.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 2}{20.0 \text{ cm}^3 \times 1}$. 4. Solve: $C_A = \frac{2.5}{20.0} \text{ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.125 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$. The mistake behind the most tempting wrong option, B, is ignoring the $2:1$ mole ratio and assuming a $1:1$ ratio, which incorrectly yields $0.0625 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ (rounded to $0.063 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$).
