## Identify the various forms of expressing oxidation and reduction

The concepts of **oxidation** and **reduction** are fundamental in chemistry. Historically, chemists defined them by observing the transfer of oxygen and hydrogen in chemical reactions. Today, we understand them more deeply through electron transfer and changes in oxidation numbers. 

Because electrons must go somewhere when they are lost, oxidation and reduction always happen together in a chemical reaction. A reaction that involves both is called a **redox** (reduction-oxidation) reaction.

### Oxidation in terms of the addition of oxygen

The earliest and simplest definition of oxidation is the **addition of oxygen** to a substance. 
For example, when magnesium ribbon burns in the air, it combines with oxygen:
$$2\text{Mg(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{MgO(s)}$$
In this reaction, magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) gains oxygen and is therefore oxidized to magnesium oxide ($\text{MgO}$).

Over time, chemists expanded this definition to include the **addition of any electronegative element** (such as chlorine, fluorine, or sulphur).
For example:
$$2\text{FeCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{FeCl}_3\text{(aq)}$$
Here, iron(II) chloride ($\text{FeCl}_2$) gains chlorine and is oxidized to iron(III) chloride ($\text{FeCl}_3$).

Another early definition states that oxidation is the **removal of hydrogen** from a compound.
$$\text{H}_2\text{S(g)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{HCl(g)} + \text{S(s)}$$
Hydrogen sulphide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) loses its hydrogen atoms to become elemental sulphur ($\text{S}$), meaning it has undergone oxidation.

### Reduction as removal of oxygen with the aid of hydrogen

Reduction is simply the reverse of oxidation. According to the classical definition, reduction is the **removal of oxygen** (or any electronegative element) from a compound. In the laboratory and in metallurgy, hydrogen gas or carbon is often used to aid in removing this oxygen.
$$\text{CuO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{Cu(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
Here, black copper(II) oxide ($\text{CuO}$) loses oxygen to become reddish-brown copper metal ($\text{Cu}$). Thus, $\text{CuO}$ is reduced. Hydrogen gas serves as the aid that removes the oxygen by combining with it to form water.

Similarly, reduction can be defined as the **addition of hydrogen** (or any electropositive element) to a substance.
$$\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_6\text{(g)}$$
Ethene ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$) gains hydrogen to form ethane ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$), meaning it has been reduced.

### Oxidation and reduction in terms of electron transfer

The most universal way to define redox reactions—especially for reactions that do not involve oxygen or hydrogen—is by the transfer of electrons. 

- **Oxidation** is defined as the **Loss of Electrons**.
- **Reduction** is defined as the **Gain of Electrons**.

A popular and helpful memory aid for this is the mnemonic **OILRIG**: **O**xidation **I**s **L**oss, **R**eduction **I**s **G**ain.

For example, when sodium reacts with chlorine to form table salt:
$$2\text{Na(s)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl(s)}$$
The sodium chloride formed is a solid made of $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions.
- Sodium atoms lose electrons: $\text{Na} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{e}^-$ (Oxidation)
- Chlorine molecules gain electrons: $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^-$ (Reduction)

## Deducing the oxidation number of chemical species

In reactions involving covalent compounds, electrons are shared rather than completely transferred. To track "electron shifts" easily, chemists assign an **oxidation number** (or oxidation state) to each atom. 

The oxidation number is the apparent electrical charge that an atom would have if all bonds in the molecule were perfectly ionic. **Note:** Oxidation numbers are dimensionless quantities and have **no units**. However, they must always be written with a sign ($+$ or $-$), except when the value is $0$.

### Use of oxidation numbers

To deduce the oxidation number of any chemical species, apply these standard JAMB rules:

1. **Free elements:** The oxidation number of any atom in its uncombined, free elemental state is $0$. (For example, $\text{Na}$, $\text{O}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$, and $\text{P}_4$ all have an oxidation state of $0$).
2. **Simple ions:** For a monoatomic ion, the oxidation number equals the charge on the ion. (For example, $\text{Na}^+$ is $+1$, $\text{S}^{2-}$ is $-2$, and $\text{Al}^{3+}$ is $+3$).
3. **Hydrogen:** In most compounds, the oxidation state of hydrogen is $+1$. (Exception: in metal hydrides like $\text{NaH}$, it acts as a non-metal and is $-1$).
4. **Oxygen:** In most compounds, the oxidation state of oxygen is $-2$. (Exception: in peroxides like $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, it is $-1$, and in $\text{OF}_2$, it is $+2$ because fluorine is more electronegative).
5. **Neutral molecules:** The sum of all oxidation numbers of the atoms in a neutral molecule is $0$.
6. **Polyatomic ions:** The sum of all oxidation numbers of the atoms in a polyatomic ion equals the overall **charge on the ion**.

**Worked Example 1: Deducing oxidation number in a neutral molecule**
Find the oxidation number of sulphur ($\text{S}$) in tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
1. State the known oxidation numbers: $\text{H} = +1$, $\text{O} = -2$.
2. Note that the molecule is neutral, so the sum of the oxidation states is $0$.
3. Set up the equation using the number of atoms: $2(\text{H}) + 1(\text{S}) + 4(\text{O}) = 0$.
4. Substitute the known values: $2(+1) + \text{S} + 4(-2) = 0$.
5. Solve for S: $+2 + \text{S} - 8 = 0 \implies \text{S} - 6 = 0 \implies \text{S} = +6$.
6. State the final answer: The oxidation number of sulphur is $+6$ (no unit).

**Worked Example 2: Deducing oxidation number in a polyatomic ion**
Find the oxidation number of chromium ($\text{Cr}$) in the heptaoxodichromate(VI) ion ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$).
1. State the known oxidation number: $\text{O} = -2$.
2. Note that the overall charge of the ion is $-2$, so the sum equals $-2$.
3. Set up the equation: $2(\text{Cr}) + 7(\text{O}) = -2$.
4. Substitute the known value: $2\text{Cr} + 7(-2) = -2$.
5. Solve for Cr: $2\text{Cr} - 14 = -2 \implies 2\text{Cr} = +12 \implies \text{Cr} = +6$.
6. State the final answer: The oxidation number of chromium is $+6$ (no unit).

**Worked Example 3: Deducing oxidation number with an exception**
Find the oxidation number of oxygen ($\text{O}$) in hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$).
1. State the known oxidation number: $\text{H} = +1$ (hydrogen is attached to a non-metal).
2. Note the molecule is neutral, so the sum is $0$.
3. Set up the equation: $2(\text{H}) + 2(\text{O}) = 0$.
4. Substitute the known value: $2(+1) + 2\text{O} = 0$.
5. Solve for O: $2 + 2\text{O} = 0 \implies 2\text{O} = -2 \implies \text{O} = -1$.
6. State the final answer: The oxidation number of oxygen is $-1$ (no unit).

By tracking changes in oxidation numbers, we arrive at the most comprehensive definition of redox processes:
- **Oxidation** is an **increase** in oxidation number (becoming more positive due to losing electrons).
- **Reduction** is a **decrease** in oxidation number (becoming more negative due to gaining electrons).

## Classify chemical reactions in terms of oxidation or reduction

Not all chemical reactions are redox reactions. You can use oxidation numbers to classify them accurately.
- If the oxidation number of **any** element changes from the reactant side to the product side, it is a **redox reaction**. (Common examples include single displacement, combustion, and combination reactions).
- If **no** oxidation numbers change, it is **not a redox reaction**. (Common examples include acid-base neutralizations and double decomposition/precipitation reactions).

**Common Mistake:** Assuming that an acid reacting with a base to form salt and water involves oxidation.
*Correction:* Neutralization is strictly a transfer of protons ($\text{H}^+$), not a transfer of electrons. Let's prove it below.

**Worked Example 4: Classifying a reaction**
Is the reaction $\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ a redox reaction?
1. Calculate the oxidation numbers of all elements in the reactants: $\text{Na} = +1$, $\text{O} = -2$, $\text{H} = +1$ (in $\text{NaOH}$), $\text{H} = +1$ (in $\text{HCl}$), $\text{Cl} = -1$.
2. Calculate the oxidation numbers of all elements in the products: $\text{Na} = +1$, $\text{Cl} = -1$, $\text{H} = +1$, $\text{O} = -2$.
3. Compare the two sides: No element changes its oxidation number.
4. Conclusion: This is a neutralization reaction and is **not a redox reaction**.

## Compute the number of electron transfer in redox reactions

JAMB questions frequently ask for the total number of moles of electrons transferred in a specific reaction or half-reaction. 

**Steps to compute electron transfer:**
1. Write down the core half-equation for the species undergoing the change.
2. Deduce the initial and final oxidation numbers for the element that changes state.
3. Find the difference between the two oxidation numbers. This difference equals the number of electrons transferred per single atom.
4. Multiply this difference by the total number of those atoms changing state as indicated by the formula's subscripts and coefficients.

**Worked Example 5: Computing electron transfer**
Calculate the number of moles of electrons transferred when one mole of heptaoxodichromate(VI) ion ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$) is completely reduced to chromium(III) ions ($\text{Cr}^{3+}$).
1. Write the core change, balancing the main atoms first: $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}$.
2. Calculate the oxidation number of Cr in the reactant: We previously found it is $+6$.
3. Calculate the oxidation number of Cr in the product: A simple ion's state is its charge, which is $+3$.
4. Find the difference per Cr atom: A change from $+6$ to $+3$ means each Cr atom gains $3$ electrons (so $3 \text{ mol}$ of electrons per mole of Cr atoms).
5. Multiply by the number of Cr atoms in the formula: $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ contains $2$ Cr atoms, so $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ contains $2 \text{ mol}$ of Cr atoms.
6. Final calculation: $2 \text{ mol of Cr atoms} \times 3 \text{ mol of electrons per mol of Cr atoms} = 6 \text{ mol}$ of electrons.
7. State the final answer: $6 \text{ mol}$ of electrons are transferred.

## Distinguish between oxidizing and reducing agents in redox reactions

In a redox reaction, electrons move from one substance to another. We describe these substances based on their roles, much like how a travel agent helps *you* travel.

- An **Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant)** is a substance that brings about the oxidation of another substance. It does this by taking electrons away from the other substance. By accepting those electrons, the oxidizing agent is itself **reduced**. Consequently, its oxidation number **decreases**.
- A **Reducing Agent (Reductant)** is a substance that brings about the reduction of another substance. It does this by giving electrons to the other substance. By donating those electrons, the reducing agent is itself **oxidized**. Consequently, its oxidation number **increases**.

### Identify the name of redox species in a reaction

When a question asks you to identify the substance oxidized, the substance reduced, the oxidizing agent, or the reducing agent, remember this crucial and unbreakable rule: **The answers are ALWAYS found on the reactant side of the equation, never on the product side.**

**Worked Example 6: Identifying redox species**
Consider the reaction: $\text{H}_2\text{S(g)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{HCl(g)} + \text{S(s)}$. Identify the oxidizing agent and the reducing agent.
1. Assign oxidation numbers to the reactants: S in $\text{H}_2\text{S} = -2$; Cl in $\text{Cl}_2 = 0$.
2. Assign oxidation numbers to the products: Cl in $\text{HCl} = -1$; S in $\text{S} = 0$.
3. Track the changes:
   - S goes from $-2$ to $0$. This is an increase in oxidation number, which means oxidation. Therefore, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ is **oxidized**.
   - Cl goes from $0$ to $-1$. This is a decrease in oxidation number, which means reduction. Therefore, $\text{Cl}_2$ is **reduced**.
4. Apply the definitions to identify the agents:
   - The substance that gets oxidized acts as the reducing agent. So, **$\text{H}_2\text{S}$ is the reducing agent**.
   - The substance that gets reduced acts as the oxidizing agent. So, **$\text{Cl}_2$ is the oxidizing agent**.

## Relate reagents to their oxidizing and reducing abilities

Some chemicals are famous in the laboratory for their strong ability to act as agents. Questions on JAMB will test your ability to recognize these common reagents.

**Common Oxidizing Agents (Electron Acceptors):**
- **Acidified Potassium tetraoxomanganate(VII) ($\text{KMnO}_4$):** An exceptionally powerful oxidizing agent.
- **Acidified Potassium heptaoxodichromate(VI) ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$):** A strong oxidizing agent widely used in organic chemistry and volumetric titrations.
- **Halogens ($\text{F}_2, \text{Cl}_2, \text{Br}_2, \text{I}_2$):** Because they have high electronegativity, they eagerly accept electrons. Fluorine is the most powerful oxidizing agent of all elements.
- **Concentrated trioxonitrate(V) acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) and concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).**

**Common Reducing Agents (Electron Donors):**
- **Hydrogen sulphide gas ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$):** A classic weak reducing agent with a smell of rotten eggs.
- **Sulphur(IV) oxide ($\text{SO}_2$):** Often used in industry to bleach materials like wood pulp by reduction.
- **Potassium iodide ($\text{KI}$):** The iodide ion ($\text{I}^-$) easily loses electrons to become iodine ($\text{I}_2$).
- **Carbon ($\text{C}$) and Carbon(II) oxide ($\text{CO}$):** Highly important in metallurgy, used in blast furnaces to reduce metal ores (like iron ore) to pure metals.
- **Iron(II) salts ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$):** Readily oxidize to the more stable iron(III) ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$) state.

## Apply oxidation number in naming inorganic compounds

### IUPAC nomenclature of inorganic compounds using oxidation number

In older chemistry texts, compounds containing transition metals were named using suffixes like "-ic" (for the higher oxidation state) and "-ous" (for the lower oxidation state). For example, $\text{FeCl}_3$ was ferric chloride, and $\text{FeCl}_2$ was ferrous chloride. 

Today, the standard IUPAC nomenclature uses the **Stock system**. This system uses Roman numerals to explicitly state the oxidation number of the central or transition element in the compound.

**Rules for naming using the Stock system:**
1. Calculate the oxidation number of the key central element (usually a transition metal or the central non-metal in a polyatomic anion).
2. Write the corresponding Roman numeral in parentheses immediately after the element's name, with no space in between (e.g., Iron(II) or Iron(III)).
3. For oxyanions (negative polyatomic ions containing oxygen), the chemical name ends in the suffix **"-ate"**. The number of oxygen atoms is indicated by Greek prefixes (mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, hepta).

**Common JAMB Examples:**
- $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$: Copper(I) oxide (Oxidation number of Cu is $+1$)
- $\text{CuO}$: Copper(II) oxide (Oxidation number of Cu is $+2$)
- $\text{NaClO}$: Sodium oxochlorate(I) (One oxygen, Cl is $+1$)
- $\text{KMnO}_4$: Potassium tetraoxomanganate(VII) (Four oxygens, Mn is $+7$)
- $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$: Tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid (Four oxygens, S is $+6$)

**Worked Example 7: IUPAC naming of an oxyanion salt**
What is the IUPAC name for $\text{KClO}_3$?
1. Identify the formula and its constituent atoms: potassium ($\text{K}$), chlorine ($\text{Cl}$), and $3$ oxygen ($\text{O}$) atoms.
2. State the known oxidation states: $\text{K} = +1$, $\text{O} = -2$.
3. Set up the equation for the neutral compound: $+1 + \text{Cl} + 3(-2) = 0$.
4. Solve for chlorine: $1 + \text{Cl} - 6 = 0 \implies \text{Cl} = +5$.
5. Construct the name: The cation is potassium. The anion has $3$ oxygen atoms ("trioxo"), contains chlorine ("chlorate"), and chlorine is in the $+5$ state ("(V)").
6. Final answer: The IUPAC name is **Potassium trioxochlorate(V)**.

**Worked Example 8: IUPAC naming of a simple covalent oxide**
What is the IUPAC name for $\text{NO}_2$?
1. Identify the formula: nitrogen ($\text{N}$) and $2$ oxygen ($\text{O}$) atoms.
2. State the known oxidation state: $\text{O} = -2$.
3. Set up the equation for the neutral compound: $\text{N} + 2(-2) = 0$.
4. Solve for nitrogen: $\text{N} - 4 = 0 \implies \text{N} = +4$.
5. Construct the name: The elements are nitrogen and oxygen. The oxidation state of nitrogen is $+4$.
6. Final answer: The IUPAC name is **Nitrogen(IV) oxide**. (Older name: nitrogen dioxide).

## Balance redox reaction equations

Simple equations can sometimes be balanced by inspection (guessing). However, complex redox equations, especially those occurring in acidic or basic media, require systematic methods. JAMB expects you to handle these using the **Oxidation Number Method** or the **Ion-Electron (Half-Equation) Method**.

**The Oxidation Number Method:**
This method focuses on ensuring that the total number of electrons lost equals the total number of electrons gained by tracking oxidation numbers.

**Worked Example 9: Balancing by Oxidation Number Method**
Balance the equation: $\text{Cu} + \text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3\text{)}_2 + \text{NO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$
1. **Assign oxidation numbers to find elements that change:**
   - Cu goes from $0$ to $+2$ (an increase of $2 \text{ mol}$ of electrons lost).
   - N goes from $+5$ (in $\text{HNO}_3$) to $+2$ (in $\text{NO}$) (a decrease of $3 \text{ mol}$ of electrons gained).
2. **Cross-multiply to balance the electron transfer:** 
   To make electrons lost equal to electrons gained, multiply Cu by $3$ and NO by $2$ so that the total increase ($3 \times 2 = 6$) equals the total decrease ($2 \times 3 = 6$).
   $$3\text{Cu} + \text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow 3\text{Cu(NO}_3\text{)}_2 + 2\text{NO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
3. **Balance the atoms of the element that changed:**
   There are $3 \times 2 = 6$ nitrogen atoms locked in the copper(II) nitrate products, plus $2$ nitrogen atoms in the NO gas, making $8$ nitrogen atoms in total on the right. Therefore, we must supply $8$ $\text{HNO}_3$ molecules on the left.
   $$3\text{Cu} + 8\text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow 3\text{Cu(NO}_3\text{)}_2 + 2\text{NO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
4. **Balance Hydrogen and Oxygen:**
   There are $8$ H atoms on the left, so we require $4$ $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules on the right to match.
   $$3\text{Cu} + 8\text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow 3\text{Cu(NO}_3\text{)}_2 + 2\text{NO} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
5. **Verify the equation:** 
   Check oxygen: Left side = $8 \times 3 = 24$. Right side = $(3 \times 6) + 2 + 4 = 24$. The equation is correctly balanced! (Note: The stoichiometric coefficients have no unit).

**The Ion-Electron (Half-Equation) Method:**
This technique splits the reaction into two halves and is extremely useful for ionic equations in acidic media.
1. Split the overall equation into an oxidation half and a reduction half.
2. Balance main atoms (elements other than O and H).
3. Balance oxygen atoms by adding $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules to the deficient side.
4. Balance hydrogen atoms by adding $\text{H}^+$ ions to the deficient side.
5. Balance charges by adding electrons ($\text{e}^-$) to the more positive side.
6. Multiply the half-equations by appropriate integers so the number of electrons lost equals the number gained.
7. Add the equations together and cancel out common terms.

**Worked Example 10: Balancing by Ion-Electron Method**
Find the values of $a$ and $b$ in the balanced ionic equation: 
$a\text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ \rightarrow b\text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$
1. **Write the oxidation half-equation:** $\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + 1\text{e}^-$
2. **Write the reduction half-equation:** $\text{MnO}_4^- \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+}$
   - Balance oxygen by adding water: $\text{MnO}_4^- \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$
   - Balance hydrogen by adding protons: $\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$
   - Balance the charge: The left side total charge is $(-1 + 8) = +7$. The right side total charge is $+2$. To equate them, add $5\text{e}^-$ to the left side:
   $\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$
3. **Equate the electrons:** 
   Multiply the entire iron oxidation half-equation by $5$ to supply the $5\text{e}^-$ needed.
   $5\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow 5\text{Fe}^{3+} + 5\text{e}^-$
4. **Add the two half-equations together:**
   $5\text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ \rightarrow 5\text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$
5. **State the final answer:** By comparison with the original algebraic equation, the stoichiometric coefficient $a$ is $5$, and $b$ is $5$.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Oxidation | The addition of oxygen/electronegative element, removal of hydrogen, loss of electrons, or an increase in oxidation number |
| Reduction | The removal of oxygen, addition of hydrogen/electropositive element, gain of electrons, or a decrease in oxidation number |
| Redox reaction | A chemical reaction where both oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously |
| Oxidation number | The apparent electrical charge of an atom in a molecule or ion if all bonds were purely ionic |
| Oxidizing agent | A substance that causes oxidation, gains electrons, and therefore gets reduced itself |
| Reducing agent | A substance that causes reduction, loses electrons, and therefore gets oxidized itself |
| IUPAC Stock system | A standardized naming system for inorganic compounds that utilizes Roman numerals to specify the oxidation state of key elements |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Common Mistake:** Confusing the oxidizing agent with the substance being oxidized. Remember that the **oxidizing agent gets REDUCED** (its oxidation number drops). The **reducing agent gets OXIDIZED** (its oxidation number rises).
- **Common Mistake:** Forgetting that oxidizing and reducing agents must be chosen from the **reactants**, never the products. If a JAMB question asks "What is oxidized?", strictly avoid picking any option that is a product.
- **Exam Tip:** Oxidation numbers can be negative, zero, or positive, and must always be written with their sign ($+2$, not just $2$). However, they are dimensionless and do not have units.
- **Exam Tip:** When calculating the oxidation state of oxygen, always assume it is $-2$ unless you are specifically dealing with a peroxide like $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ (where it is $-1$).
- **Exam Tip:** A neutralization reaction (acid + base $\rightarrow$ salt + water) is never a redox reaction because no electrons are transferred and no oxidation numbers change. Do not be fooled by equations that look complex but are merely proton transfers.

## Quick summary

1. Oxidation is fundamentally the loss of electrons or an increase in oxidation number, while reduction is the gain of electrons or a decrease in oxidation number.
2. A redox reaction is a process where both oxidation and reduction take place simultaneously.
3. Oxidation numbers are assigned to elements using standard rules (e.g., pure uncombined elements are $0$, oxygen is usually $-2$, and hydrogen is usually $+1$).
4. The reducing agent is the substance that gets oxidized, providing electrons; the oxidizing agent is the substance that gets reduced, accepting electrons.
5. Common strong oxidizing agents include acidified $\text{KMnO}_4$ (which changes from purple to colourless) and $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ (which changes from orange to green). Common reducing agents include $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{SO}_2$, and $\text{KI}$.
6. The IUPAC Stock system uses oxidation numbers written in Roman numerals to accurately name inorganic compounds (e.g., Potassium tetraoxomanganate(VII)).
7. Redox equations can be systematically balanced by ensuring the total number of electrons lost in the oxidation half-reaction is exactly equal to the number of electrons gained in the reduction half-reaction.

## Practice questions

**1. What is the oxidation number of chromium in potassium heptaoxodichromate(VI) ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$)?**

- A. +3
- B. +6
- C. +7
- D. +12

**Answer: B.** We find the oxidation number by calculation:
1. State the known oxidation numbers: $\text{K} = +1$, $\text{O} = -2$.
2. Note that the sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral molecule is $0$.
3. Set up the equation using subscripts: $2(+1) + 2(\text{Cr}) + 7(-2) = 0$.
4. Solve for $\text{Cr}$: $2 + 2\text{Cr} - 14 = 0 \implies 2\text{Cr} = +12 \implies \text{Cr} = +6$.
5. The oxidation number of chromium is $+6$ (oxidation numbers have no units).
The mistake in option C ($+7$) is confusing the oxidation state of Cr with the $+7$ state of Mn in potassium tetraoxomanganate(VII).

**2. How many moles of electrons are transferred when one mole of heptaoxodichromate(VI) ion ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$) is completely reduced to chromium(III) ions ($\text{Cr}^{3+}$)?**

- A. 3 mol
- B. 5 mol
- C. 6 mol
- D. 7 mol

**Answer: C.** We determine the electron transfer as follows:
1. Write the core reduction half-equation: $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}$.
2. Determine the oxidation state of Cr in the reactant: $2\text{Cr} + 7(-2) = -2 \implies \text{Cr} = +6$.
3. Determine the oxidation state of Cr in the product: It is the charge on the simple ion, which is $+3$.
4. Calculate the difference per Cr atom: $+6 - (+3) = 3$ electrons gained by each Cr atom, that is $3 \text{ mol}$ of electrons per mole of Cr atoms.
5. Multiply by the moles of Cr atoms in $1 \text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ ($2 \text{ mol}$): $3 \text{ mol} \times 2 = 6 \text{ mol}$ of electrons in total.
The mistake in option A ($3 \text{ mol}$) is forgetting to multiply the electron transfer for one Cr atom by the $2$ chromium atoms in the formula.

**3. In the reaction $3\text{CuO(s)} + 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} \rightarrow 3\text{Cu(s)} + \text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$, which substance acts as the reducing agent?**

- A. $\text{CuO}$
- B. $\text{NH}_3$
- C. $\text{Cu}$
- D. $\text{N}_2$

**Answer: B.** We determine the reducing agent by finding which species is oxidized:
1. Assign oxidation numbers to copper: Cu in $\text{CuO}$ is $+2$, while Cu in solid $\text{Cu}$ is $0$.
2. Assign oxidation numbers to nitrogen: N in $\text{NH}_3$ is $-3$ (since $\text{N} + 3(+1) = 0$), while N in gaseous $\text{N}_2$ is $0$.
3. Identify oxidation: Nitrogen goes from $-3$ to $0$ (an increase), meaning $\text{NH}_3$ loses electrons and is oxidized.
4. Identify reduction: Copper goes from $+2$ to $0$ (a decrease), meaning $\text{CuO}$ gains electrons and is reduced.
5. Apply definitions: The reducing agent is the substance that gets oxidized, which is $\text{NH}_3$.
The mistake in option A ($\text{CuO}$) is picking the oxidizing agent (which undergoes reduction) instead of the reducing agent.

**4. What is the IUPAC name of the compound with the formula $\text{KClO}_3$?**

- A. Potassium oxochlorate(I)
- B. Potassium trioxochlorate(IV)
- C. Potassium chlorate(III)
- D. Potassium trioxochlorate(V)

**Answer: D.** We derive the IUPAC name using the Stock system rules:
1. Identify the constituent elements: potassium ($\text{K}$), chlorine ($\text{Cl}$), and $3$ oxygen ($\text{O}$) atoms.
2. State the known oxidation numbers: $\text{K} = +1$, $\text{O} = -2$.
3. Set the sum for the neutral compound to $0$: $+1 + \text{Cl} + 3(-2) = 0$.
4. Solve for the oxidation state of chlorine: $1 + \text{Cl} - 6 = 0 \implies \text{Cl} = +5$.
5. Construct the name combining the cation, oxygen prefix (trioxo), central element (chlorate), and Roman numeral: Potassium trioxochlorate(V).
The mistake in option B is a simple calculation error, arriving at $+4$ instead of $+5$.

**5. Which of the following equations represents a redox reaction?**

- A. $\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$
- B. $\text{AgNO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{NaCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{AgCl(s)} + \text{NaNO}_3\text{(aq)}$
- C. $\text{Zn(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$
- D. $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaO(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$

**Answer: C.** We check for a redox reaction by finding oxidation number changes:
1. Check option A: Oxidation states remain constant (Na is $+1$, O is $-2$, H is $+1$, Cl is $-1$). This is an acid-base neutralization.
2. Check option B: Oxidation states remain constant (Ag is $+1$, Cl is $-1$). This is precipitation.
3. Check option D: Oxidation states remain constant (Ca is $+2$, C is $+4$, O is $-2$). This is thermal decomposition.
4. Check option C: Zinc ($\text{Zn}$) changes from $0$ to $+2$ (oxidation). Hydrogen ($\text{H}$) changes from $+1$ to $0$ (reduction). 
5. Since electrons are transferred in option C, it is a redox reaction.
The mistake in option A is assuming all vigorous laboratory reactions, like neutralization, involve electron transfer.

**6. Find the value of the coefficient $y$ in the balanced half-equation: $x\text{I}^-\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{I}_2\text{(aq)} + y\text{e}^-$.**

- A. 1
- B. 2
- C. 3
- D. 4

**Answer: B.** We balance the half-equation systematically:
1. Write the initial unbalanced equation: $\text{I}^- \rightarrow \text{I}_2$.
2. Balance the iodine atoms by supplying a coefficient to the reactant: $2\text{I}^- \rightarrow \text{I}_2$. (Thus, the coefficient $x$ is $2$).
3. Calculate the total charge on the left side: $2 \times (-1) = -2$.
4. Calculate the total charge on the right side without electrons: $0$.
5. To balance the charges, we must add $2$ negative electrons to the right side: $2\text{I}^- \rightarrow \text{I}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$. Therefore, $y = 2$.
The mistake in option A ($1$) happens if one balances the charge using a single electron without first balancing the number of iodine atoms.

**7. When sulphur(IV) oxide gas is passed into an acidified solution of potassium heptaoxodichromate(VI), the colour changes from orange to green because:**

- A. The $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ ion reduces the $\text{SO}_2$ to S
- B. The $\text{SO}_2$ acts as an oxidizing agent, changing $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ to $\text{Cr}^{3+}$
- C. The $\text{SO}_2$ acts as a reducing agent, reducing $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ to $\text{Cr}^{3+}$
- D. The $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ ion is oxidized to $\text{Cr}^{2+}$

**Answer: C.** We analyze the redox behaviour of the test reagent:
1. Identify the reagents: Orange $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ reacts with $\text{SO}_2$.
2. Determine the initial oxidation state of Cr in $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$: $2\text{Cr} + 7(-2) = -2 \implies \text{Cr} = +6$.
3. Recall the product responsible for the green colour: It is the $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ ion.
4. Calculate the change: Chromium goes from $+6$ to $+3$, which is a decrease (gain of electrons). Therefore, it undergoes reduction.
5. Because $\text{SO}_2$ causes this reduction, it acts as the reducing agent.
The mistake in option B is claiming $\text{SO}_2$ acts as an oxidizing agent, which would require the oxidation state of chromium to increase, not decrease.

**8. What is the oxidation number of oxygen in hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$)?**

- A. -2
- B. -1
- C. 0
- D. +1

**Answer: B.** We find the oxidation number using the standard rules:
1. State the rule for hydrogen: Hydrogen is usually $+1$ when bonded to non-metals.
2. Note the overall charge: The molecule $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ is neutral, so the sum of oxidation numbers is $0$.
3. Set up the equation using subscripts: $2(\text{H}) + 2(\text{O}) = 0$.
4. Substitute the known hydrogen value: $2(+1) + 2\text{O} = 0 \implies 2 + 2\text{O} = 0$.
5. Solve for oxygen: $2\text{O} = -2 \implies \text{O} = -1$.
The mistake in option A ($-2$) is blindly applying the general rule that oxygen is always $-2$, forgetting that peroxides are a strict exception.

**9. In the balanced redox equation $a\text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ \rightarrow b\text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$, what is the value of the coefficient $a$?**

- A. 2
- B. 3
- C. 5
- D. 8

**Answer: C.** We determine the stoichiometric coefficient by balancing electron transfer:
1. Isolate the reduction half-reaction: $\text{MnO}_4^- \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+}$.
2. Calculate the electron change for manganese: Mn drops from $+7$ to $+2$, meaning $5\text{e}^-$ are gained per formula unit.
3. Isolate the oxidation half-reaction: $\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}$.
4. Calculate the electron change for iron: Fe rises from $+2$ to $+3$, meaning $1\text{e}^-$ is lost per ion.
5. To balance the $5\text{e}^-$ gained by manganese, we must supply $5$ iron(II) ions. Thus, we multiply the iron half-equation by $5$, making $a = 5$.
The mistake in option D ($8$) is confusing the required number of iron ions with the coefficient of the hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$).

**10. Calculate the oxidation number of sulphur in the thiosulphate ion ($\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-}$).**

- A. +2
- B. +3
- C. +4
- D. +6

**Answer: A.** We deduce the oxidation number by solving the algebraic sum:
1. Identify the given ion and its overall charge: The ion is $\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-}$ and its overall charge is $-2$.
2. State the known oxidation number of oxygen: $\text{O} = -2$.
3. Set up the equation for the sum of the oxidation states: $2(\text{S}) + 3(\text{O}) = -2$.
4. Substitute the value for oxygen: $2\text{S} + 3(-2) = -2 \implies 2\text{S} - 6 = -2$.
5. Solve for sulphur: $2\text{S} = -2 + 6 \implies 2\text{S} = +4 \implies \text{S} = +2$. (Note: oxidation numbers have no unit).
The mistake in option D ($+6$) is assuming sulphur is $+6$, which is confusing it with its oxidation state in the more common sulphate ion ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$).
