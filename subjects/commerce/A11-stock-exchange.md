# JAMB Commerce Notes: Stock Exchange

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A, Topic 11 of the JAMB Commerce syllabus: Stock Exchange. It has four content items:

*   (i) Importance and functions
*   (ii) Types of securities (stocks, shares, bonds, debentures, etc)
*   (iii) Procedure of transactions and speculations
*   (iv) Second-Tier Securities Market, (listing requirements, types of companies for the market, advantages and operating regulations of the market.)

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1.  State the importance and functions of the Stock Exchange.
2.  Identify the different securities traded on the Stock Exchange.
3.  Analyse the procedure of transactions and speculations on the Stock Exchange.
4.  Appraise the advantages and operating regulations of the market.

**Note on the syllabus wording:** The syllabus explicitly names the "Second-Tier Securities Market" (SSM). In 2021, the Nigerian Stock Exchange (NSE) was demutualised and became the Nigerian Exchange Limited (NGX). Over the years, the SSM has been restructured and renamed (first to the Alternative Securities Market, ASeM, and later incorporated into a Growth Board). However, JAMB questions frequently test the classic rules and structures of the SSM as they appear in standard textbooks. You must learn the classic SSM rules to pass the exam, but be aware that the real-world market has evolved. 

## Importance and functions of the Stock Exchange

**Objective i: state the importance and functions of the Stock Exchange.**

The **Stock Exchange** is a highly organised and regulated market where existing (second-hand) stocks and shares are bought and sold. It is an essential part of the **capital market**, which deals in long-term loans and investments. 

Unlike a regular market where physical goods like garri or clothes are sold, the stock exchange is a market for financial instruments called **securities**. In Nigeria, this market was historically called the Nigerian Stock Exchange (NSE) and is now known as the Nigerian Exchange Limited (NGX), with its main trading floor in Lagos.

### Functions of the Stock Exchange

1.  **Provision of a secondary market:** The stock exchange provides a platform for investors to buy and sell existing securities. Without it, someone who buys shares in a company would find it very hard to sell them if they suddenly needed cash.
2.  **Enhances liquidity of securities:** Because there is a ready market, investors can easily convert their shares into cash (liquidity) at short notice.
3.  **Capital formation and mobilisation:** The stock exchange helps companies and governments raise long-term capital for expansion and development by encouraging the public to invest their savings.
4.  **Barometer of the economy:** The performance of the stock exchange (whether share prices are generally rising or falling) is a reliable indicator of the health and direction of a country's economy. 
5.  **Evaluation of securities:** The constant buying and selling on the stock exchange determine the true market value of a company's shares based on the forces of demand and supply.
6.  **Protection of investors:** The stock exchange sets strict rules and regulations that companies must follow before and after they are listed. This protects investors from fraudulent or poorly managed companies.

### Importance of the Stock Exchange to the Nigerian Economy

*   **Attracting foreign investment:** A well-regulated stock market attracts foreign investors who bring in foreign exchange to buy shares in Nigerian companies.
*   **Employment generation:** It creates direct and indirect employment for stockbrokers, jobbers, financial analysts, and regulators.
*   **Facilitating government borrowing:** When the Nigerian government needs money for infrastructural projects (like building roads or bridges), it issues government bonds on the stock exchange for the public to buy.
*   **Wealth redistribution:** It gives ordinary citizens the opportunity to own parts of large, profitable companies (like Dangote Cement or MTN Nigeria) and share in their profits through dividends.

> **Common mistakes:** Do not confuse the Stock Exchange with the Commodity Exchange. The Stock Exchange trades financial securities (shares, bonds), whereas the Commodity Exchange (covered in `A15-commodity-exchange.md`) trades physical raw materials and agricultural products like cocoa and cotton.

## Types of securities

**Objective ii: identify the different securities traded on the Stock Exchange.**

**Securities** is a general term for all financial instruments traded on the stock market that represent either ownership in a corporation or a loan given to a corporation or government. The main types of securities are stocks, shares, bonds, and debentures.

### 1. Shares

A **share** is an individual, indivisible unit of the capital of a joint-stock company. When a company's capital is divided into small, equal parts, each part is called a share.
*   **Ordinary Shares (Equities):** The owners of these shares are the true owners of the business. They have voting rights and take the most risk. They receive dividends only after all other claims have been paid, but their dividends can be very high if the company makes a large profit.
*   **Preference Shares:** Owners of these shares have a preferred right to receive a fixed rate of dividend before ordinary shareholders get anything. They also get paid first if the company is liquidated. However, they usually do not have voting rights.

*(Note: The types of capital and detailed classifications of shares are also covered in `A8-financing-business.md`.)*

### 2. Stocks

A **stock** is a bundle or consolidated mass of fully paid-up shares. When a company's shares are fully paid for, they can be merged into a single fund called "stock". The key difference is that stocks can be bought and sold in fractional amounts, whereas shares must be bought and sold in whole numbers.

| Feature | Shares | Stocks |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Divisibility** | Cannot be divided. Must be bought in whole numbers (e.g., 50 shares, not 50.5 shares). | Divisible into any amount. You can buy a fraction of a stock (e.g., ₦150.50 worth of stock). |
| **Payment** | Can be fully paid or partly paid. | Must always be fully paid up. |
| **Numbering** | Each share has a distinct, unique serial number. | Stocks do not have distinct numbers. |
| **Transfer** | Transferred in round numbers (e.g., 10, 100). | Can be transferred in any fraction or amount. |

### 3. Bonds (Government and Corporate)

A **bond** is a certificate of debt issued by a government or a corporation to raise money. The issuer promises to pay back the principal amount on a specific date, plus a fixed rate of interest at regular intervals.
*   **Government Bonds (Development Stocks):** Bonds issued by the federal or state government to finance projects. In Nigeria, Federal Government Bonds are highly traded.
*   **Corporate Bonds:** Bonds issued by large companies.
*   **Gilt-edged securities:** These are government bonds and securities. They are called "gilt-edged" because they are considered completely safe and risk-free—a government will very rarely default on its domestic debt.

### 4. Debentures

A **debenture** is a document issued by a company acknowledging a long-term loan. Unlike shareholders, debenture holders are not owners of the company; they are creditors. They receive a fixed rate of interest whether the company makes a profit or a loss. 
*   **Secured debentures:** Backed by the company's assets. If the company fails to pay, the holders can sell the assets to recover their money.
*   **Unsecured (Naked) debentures:** Not backed by any specific asset.

## Procedure of transactions and speculations

**Objective iii: analyse the procedure of transactions and speculations on the Stock Exchange.**

### The Market Operators: Brokers and Jobbers

To understand how buying and selling happen on the stock exchange, you must know the two traditional types of middlemen:

1.  **Stockbrokers:** A stockbroker is an agent who buys and sells securities on behalf of the public (investors) in exchange for a commission called **brokerage**. A member of the public cannot walk straight into the stock exchange to buy shares; they must hire a stockbroker to do it for them.
2.  **Stockjobbers (Jobbers):** A jobber is a principal dealer who buys and sells securities on their own account. They do not deal with the public; they only deal with stockbrokers. They make their profit from the **jobber's turn**, which is the difference between the price at which they buy securities and the higher price at which they sell them.

*(Note: In the Nigerian market today, stockbroking firms often perform both brokering and dealing functions, but JAMB continues to test the traditional distinction between brokers and jobbers.)*

### Step-by-Step Procedure of Transactions

If an investor in Nigeria wants to buy 1,000 shares in Zenith Bank, the procedure is as follows:

**Step 1: Contact a Stockbroker.**
The investor opens an account with a registered stockbroking firm and gives an order to buy 1,000 Zenith Bank shares.

**Step 2: The broker enters the market.**
The broker goes to the trading floor of the stock exchange (or logs into the Automated Trading System, ATS). 

**Step 3: Finding the price and negotiating.**
The broker approaches a jobber (or another dealer on the ATS) who deals in bank shares and asks for a price quotation. The jobber usually quotes two prices: a lower price (buying price) and a higher price (selling price). The broker negotiates on behalf of the client to get the best deal.

**Step 4: Executing the trade.**
Once they agree on a price, the transaction is struck. On the traditional floor, both the broker and the jobber record the deal in their transaction pads.

**Step 5: Issuance of a Contract Note.**
The stockbroker sends a document called a **Contract Note** to the investor. This document confirms the transaction, showing the number of shares bought, the price, the broker's commission, and any stamp duties or fees.

**Step 6: Settlement and Clearing.**
In Nigeria, settlement is handled electronically by the **Central Securities Clearing System (CSCS)**. The investor's CSCS account is credited with the shares, and the seller's account is debited. Physical share certificates have largely been replaced by these electronic records.

### Speculation on the Stock Exchange

A **speculator** is a person who buys or sells securities on the stock exchange not for long-term investment, but to make a quick profit from short-term fluctuations in prices. 

There are three main types of speculators on the stock exchange, named after animals:

1.  **The Bull:**
    *   A bull is a speculator who **buys** securities now with the expectation that prices will **rise** in the future.
    *   They hope to sell the securities later at a higher price to make a profit.
    *   If many speculators are buying because they expect prices to go up, the market is described as a **Bull Market** (optimistic and rising).

2.  **The Bear:**
    *   A bear is a speculator who **sells** securities now with the expectation that prices will **fall** in the future.
    *   Often, a bear sells shares they do not even own yet (a practice called *short selling*), hoping to buy them back later at a much lower price before they have to deliver them to the buyer.
    *   If many speculators are selling because they expect prices to drop, the market is described as a **Bear Market** (pessimistic and falling).

3.  **The Stag:**
    *   A stag is a speculator who applies for newly issued shares (during an Initial Public Offering, or IPO) hoping that the shares will be in high demand.
    *   They expect that when trading officially begins, the shares will open at a **premium** (a price higher than the issue price). 
    *   The stag intends to sell the newly allocated shares immediately for a quick profit.

| Speculator | Action taken | Market expectation | Goal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bull** | Buys now | Prices will **rise** | Sell later at a higher price |
| **Bear** | Sells now | Prices will **fall** | Buy back later at a lower price |
| **Stag** | Applies for new shares | New shares will open at a **premium** | Sell new shares immediately for a profit |

## The Second-Tier Securities Market (SSM)

**Objective iv: appraise the advantages and operating regulations of the market.**

### What is the Second-Tier Securities Market (SSM)?

The main board (First-Tier Market) of the Nigerian Stock Exchange had very strict and expensive requirements that made it difficult for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) to join and raise capital. 

To solve this, the stock exchange established the **Second-Tier Securities Market (SSM)** in 1985. It is a market specifically created to accommodate small, indigenous, and growing companies by offering them more relaxed listing requirements compared to the main market.

*(Note: Today, this market has evolved into the ASeM / Growth Board, but the historical SSM rules below are heavily tested by JAMB.)*

### Listing Requirements for the SSM

To be admitted into the Second-Tier Securities Market, a company must meet the following operating regulations and requirements:

1.  **Public Limited Liability Company:** The company must be registered as a Public Limited Liability Company (Plc) under the Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA). Private companies cannot be listed.
2.  **Track Record (Trading History):** The company must submit its financial records (audited accounts) for at least **3 years**. *(Contrast: The First-Tier market requires 5 years).*
3.  **Public Equity:** The company must offer at least **10%** of its equity share capital to the public. *(Contrast: The First-Tier market requires 25%).*
4.  **Number of Shareholders:** The company must have a minimum of **100 shareholders** after the shares are issued. *(Contrast: The First-Tier market requires a minimum of 300).*
5.  **Exclusive Listing:** A company cannot be listed on both the First-Tier Market and the Second-Tier Securities Market at the same time.
6.  **No Minimum Capital Requirement:** The SSM originally had no strict minimum capital limit, making it flexible for smaller firms, but the amount raised could not exceed ₦20 million (this limit was later revised upward).

### Types of Companies for the Market

*   **Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs):** Businesses that have good growth potential but are not yet large enough to meet First-Tier requirements.
*   **Indigenous Companies:** Wholly Nigerian-owned businesses needing capital to expand.
*   **Growing Companies:** Firms that are relatively new (between 3 to 5 years old) and want to establish a public track record.

### Advantages of the Second-Tier Securities Market

1.  **Access to cheaper capital:** It allows small and medium indigenous companies to raise long-term capital from the public rather than relying on expensive bank loans with high interest rates.
2.  **Relaxed listing requirements:** The less strict rules (e.g., only 3 years of accounts needed) make it much easier for younger companies to be admitted.
3.  **Survival and expansion:** By gaining access to public funds, SMEs can survive economic downturns, expand their operations, and generate more employment.
4.  **Stepping stone to the First-Tier:** It serves as a training ground. Once a company on the SSM grows large enough, it can upgrade and be admitted to the First-Tier market.
5.  **Investment opportunities for the public:** It provides an avenue for the public to invest in smaller, fast-growing local companies at a low entry price, offering the potential for high returns.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Bulls vs. Bears:** JAMB frequently tests these definitions. Remember: **Bull = Buys** (expects price to rise). **Bear = Sells** (expects price to drop). A bull tosses you *up* with its horns; a bear swats you *down* with its paws.
*   **Broker vs. Jobber:** A **broker** is an *agent* for the public. A **jobber** is a *dealer* for himself. The public cannot deal directly with a jobber.
*   **SSM requirements:** The most heavily tested differences between the First-Tier and Second-Tier markets are the track record (**5 years vs. 3 years**) and the percentage offered to the public (**25% vs. 10%**).
*   **Stocks vs. Shares:** If a question asks which security can be transferred in fractions or divided into any amount, the answer is **Stocks**. Shares are indivisible.

## Quick summary

1.  The Stock Exchange is a capital market where second-hand (existing) securities are traded, providing liquidity, evaluating company performance, and mobilizing long-term capital.
2.  Shares are indivisible units of a company's capital, while stocks are bundles of fully paid shares that can be divided into fractions. Bonds and debentures represent debt (loans) rather than ownership.
3.  Government bonds are known as gilt-edged securities because they are considered risk-free.
4.  Trading on the traditional stock exchange involves stockbrokers (agents of the public) and jobbers (dealers who trade for their own profit). 
5.  When a transaction is completed, the broker issues a Contract Note to the investor as proof of the trade.
6.  Speculators try to make quick profits from price changes: Bulls buy expecting a price rise, Bears sell expecting a price fall, and Stags buy new issues hoping to sell at a premium immediately.
7.  The Second-Tier Securities Market (SSM) was created for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) to raise capital.
8.  To list on the SSM, a company must be a Plc, provide 3 years of financial records, offer at least 10% of its equity to the public, and have at least 100 shareholders.

## Practice questions

**1. A speculator who anticipates a fall in the prices of shares and sells them now is known as a**
- A. Bull
- B. Bear
- C. Stag
- D. Broker

**Answer: B.** A bear expects prices to fall, so they sell securities now in the hope of buying them back later at a cheaper rate. A bull expects prices to rise, and a stag deals with newly issued shares.

**2. Which of the following is a listing requirement for the Second-Tier Securities Market?**
- A. The company must offer at least 25% of its equity to the public.
- B. The company must have submitted financial records for 5 years.
- C. The company must be a private limited liability company.
- D. The company must have submitted financial records for at least 3 years.

**Answer: D.** A company on the SSM needs to present an audited track record of at least 3 years. Option A and B are requirements for the First-Tier (main) market, and Option C is wrong because only Public Limited Companies (Plc) can be listed.

**3. Government bonds traded on the stock exchange are generally referred to as**
- A. blue-chip shares
- B. equities
- C. debentures
- D. gilt-edged securities

**Answer: D.** Government securities are called gilt-edged securities because they are extremely safe and have no risk of default. Equities refer to ordinary shares.

**4. A principal dealer on the stock exchange who buys and sells shares in their own name and does not deal directly with the public is a**
- A. stockbroker
- B. promoter
- C. stockjobber
- D. stag

**Answer: C.** A stockjobber acts as a principal dealing on his own account and deals only with brokers, making profit from the jobber's turn. A stockbroker acts as an agent for the public.

**5. The main function of the Stock Exchange is to**
- A. provide a market for short-term loans
- B. provide a secondary market for existing securities
- C. produce goods and services for the economy
- D. determine the exchange rate of the local currency

**Answer: B.** The stock exchange is a secondary capital market where existing (second-hand) securities are traded, thereby providing liquidity for investors. It deals with long-term, not short-term loans (which is the money market).

**6. The document sent by a stockbroker to an investor confirming that shares have been bought or sold is the**
- A. prospectus
- B. share certificate
- C. contract note
- D. letter of allotment

**Answer: C.** A contract note is prepared by the broker and sent to the client immediately after a transaction, detailing the price, number of shares, and commission. A prospectus is an invitation to buy new shares.

**7. An investor who buys new shares at an initial public offering (IPO) with the intention of selling them as soon as trading begins at a premium is a**
- A. bull
- B. bear
- C. jobber
- D. stag

**Answer: D.** A stag is a speculator who applies for new issues of shares hoping they will be oversubscribed so they can sell them for a quick profit right after the market opens.

**8. Which of the following is a key difference between shares and stocks?**
- A. Shares can be divided into fractions, but stocks must be bought in whole units.
- B. Stocks can be divided into fractional amounts, while shares must be in whole numbers.
- C. Stocks carry voting rights, but shares do not.
- D. Shares are issued by the government, while stocks are issued by companies.

**Answer: B.** Shares are indivisible units (you can buy 10 shares, but not 10.5 shares). Stocks are consolidations of fully paid shares that can be transferred in fractions or any money amount.

**9. The Second-Tier Securities Market (SSM) was established mainly to**
- A. assist large multinational companies in avoiding taxes
- B. encourage small and medium indigenous companies to raise capital
- C. allow the government to sell treasury bills
- D. replace the primary market

**Answer: B.** The SSM was created to relax the strict listing requirements of the main board so that small and medium-sized indigenous companies could raise long-term capital for growth.

**10. One of the reasons companies issue ordinary shares on the stock exchange is to**
- A. pay off all their workers
- B. mobilise long-term capital for expansion
- C. increase the number of their directors
- D. borrow money for a few months

**Answer: B.** Companies issue shares (equity) to raise long-term capital from the public to finance business expansion and development. Short-term loans (a few months) are usually sourced from commercial banks or the money market.
