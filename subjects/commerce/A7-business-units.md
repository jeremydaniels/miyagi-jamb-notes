# JAMB Commerce Notes: Business Units

## Forms and features of business units

**Objective i: identify the forms and features of business units.**  
**Content i: Forms and features.**

A business unit (or business organisation) is an enterprise set up by an individual, a group of people, or the government to produce goods or provide services, usually to make a profit or provide welfare. 

In Nigeria, business units are generally divided into **private enterprises** (owned by individuals or groups) and **public enterprises** (owned by the government).

### 1. Sole Proprietorship (One-man business)
A sole proprietorship is a business owned, managed, and controlled by a single individual. It is the oldest, most common, and simplest form of business in Nigeria. Examples include the local tailor, the market woman selling garri, the vulcanizer by the roadside, and the barber in your neighbourhood.

**Features of a sole proprietorship:**
- **Ownership:** It is owned by one person.
- **Source of capital:** Capital is provided by the owner from personal savings, loans from friends and family, or trade credit.
- **Liability:** The owner has **unlimited liability**. This means if the business fails and owes debts, the owner's personal properties (like their house or car) can be seized and sold to pay the business debts.
- **Legal status:** It is not a separate legal entity from its owner. In the eyes of the law, the owner and the business are exactly the same person.
- **Management:** The owner is entirely in charge of decision-making.
- **Lifespan:** The business does not have perpetual succession. If the owner dies, goes bankrupt, or becomes insane, the business usually comes to an end.

### 2. Partnership
A partnership is a voluntary association of two to twenty persons carrying on a legal business together with the aim of making a profit. Examples include law firms, accounting firms, and medical clinics run by a group of doctors. 

> **Note on syllabus and current laws:** Textbooks and JAMB historically state that a partnership is formed by **2 to 20 people** (and up to 10 for banks). However, under the current Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA) 2020, professional firms like lawyers or accountants are no longer restricted to 20 members. For exam purposes, stick to the traditional **2 to 20 members** rule, as JAMB questions are often based on older textbook definitions.

**Features of a partnership:**
- **Ownership:** Formed by 2 to 20 people.
- **Source of capital:** Capital is contributed by the partners according to their agreement.
- **Liability:** In an ordinary (general) partnership, partners have **unlimited liability**. 
- **Legal status:** Like a sole proprietorship, an ordinary partnership is not a separate legal entity. 
- **Lifespan:** The death or withdrawal of a major partner can end the partnership unless the agreement states otherwise.

**Types of partners:**
- **Active partner:** Contributes capital and takes an active part in managing the business.
- **Sleeping (dormant) partner:** Contributes capital and shares in profits/losses but does not take part in the daily management of the business.
- **Nominal partner:** Does not contribute capital or manage the business, but allows the partnership to use their name for prestige or reputation.
- **Limited partner:** Liability is limited only to the capital they contributed. They cannot participate in management.

**The Deed of Partnership:**
This is a written legal document or agreement drawn up by partners stating the rules and regulations guiding the partnership. It prevents future disputes. It usually contains:
- The name of the business and the partners.
- The amount of capital contributed by each partner.
- The ratio for sharing profits and losses.
- Salaries to be paid to active partners.
- The duration of the partnership.
- Procedures for admitting new partners or dissolving the firm.

*If there is no deed of partnership*, the provisions of the Partnership Act of 1890 apply (e.g., profits and losses are shared equally, no partner gets a salary, and no interest is paid on capital).

### 3. Limited Liability Companies
A limited liability company is a business organisation recognised by law as a separate legal entity, formed by shareholders whose liability is limited to the shares they hold. In Nigeria, they are regulated by the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC). 

There are two main types: **Private Limited Liability Companies (Ltd.)** and **Public Limited Liability Companies (Plc.)**.

| Feature | Private Limited Company (Ltd.) | Public Limited Company (Plc.) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Membership size** | 2 to 50 shareholders. | 7 to an unlimited number of shareholders. |
| **Sale of shares** | Cannot sell shares to the general public. | Can invite the general public to buy shares. |
| **Transferability of shares** | Shares cannot be transferred without the consent of other shareholders. | Shares are freely bought and sold on the Stock Exchange. |
| **Publication of accounts** | Not required by law to publish annual financial reports in public newspapers. | Must publish annual financial reports in newspapers for the public to see. |
| **Commencement of business** | Can start trading immediately after receiving the Certificate of Incorporation. | Must receive a Trading Certificate before it can start business. |

> **Note on CAMA 2020:** The new CAMA 2020 law allows a single person to form a private limited liability company. However, standard JAMB textbooks define private companies as having 2 to 50 members. Be aware of both, but answer based on the traditional 2-50 rule if the question doesn't specifically mention the new CAMA.

**General features of Limited Liability Companies:**
- **Legal Entity:** The company is an "artificial person" in law. It can sue and be sued in its own name, separate from its owners.
- **Limited Liability:** If the company fails, the shareholders only lose the money they paid for their shares. Their personal assets cannot be touched.
- **Perpetual Succession:** The death or madness of a shareholder does not end the company.
- **Management:** Owned by shareholders, but managed by a Board of Directors elected by the shareholders.

### 4. Cooperative Societies
A cooperative society is a voluntary association of individuals with common interests who pool their resources together to promote their mutual economic well-being. Examples are market women cooperatives, farmers' cooperatives, and civil service cooperative societies.

**Features of a cooperative society:**
- **Ownership and Membership:** Voluntary and open to anyone who shares the common interest.
- **Democratic control:** It operates on the principle of **"one man, one vote"**, regardless of how many shares a member holds.
- **Motive:** The primary aim is to protect and improve the welfare of members, not strictly to maximize profit.
- **Dividend:** Profits are shared as a "patronage dividend" based on how much a member patronised or traded with the society, rather than just how much capital they contributed.

**Types of Cooperative Societies:**
- **Consumer Cooperative Society:** Members pool money to buy consumer goods in bulk from manufacturers at cheaper rates and sell them to members at fair prices.
- **Producer Cooperative Society:** Formed by small-scale producers or farmers who pool resources to buy equipment, raw materials, or tractors to boost production.
- **Thrift and Credit Cooperative Society:** Members contribute money regularly into a common fund (similar to *esusu* or *ajo*). Members can borrow from this fund at very low interest rates.
- **Wholesale Cooperative Society:** Formed by retail cooperatives to buy goods in large quantities from manufacturers.

### 5. Public Corporations
A public corporation is a large-scale business organisation set up, owned, and fully funded by the government through an Act of Parliament or military decree. The aim is to provide essential services to the public. Examples in Nigeria include the Nigerian Ports Authority (NPA), the Nigerian Railway Corporation (NRC), and the Federal Radio Corporation of Nigeria (FRCN). 

**Features of a Public Corporation:**
- **Ownership:** Owned entirely by the government.
- **Establishment:** Created by a special Act of Parliament which states its powers and functions.
- **Motive:** Formed mainly to provide essential services and improve public welfare, not to maximize profit.
- **Management:** Managed by a Board of Directors appointed by the government (usually a minister or the president).
- **Legal Entity:** It can sue and be sued in its own name.
- **Finance:** Capital is provided by the government through taxes, allocations, and grants.

> **Note on NNPC:** The Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation was historically a prime example of a public corporation. Under the Petroleum Industry Act (2021), it transitioned into NNPC Limited, a commercial limited liability company. 

## Merits and demerits of business units

**Objective v: state the merits and demerits of business units.**  
**Content v: Merits and demerits.**

### 1. Sole Proprietorship
**Merits (Advantages):**
- Easy and cheap to establish; few legal requirements.
- Quick decision-making because the owner does not have to consult anyone.
- The owner takes all the profits.
- Close personal relationship with customers and employees.
- Complete privacy; no obligation to publish business accounts.

**Demerits (Disadvantages):**
- **Unlimited liability:** Personal property is at risk if the business incurs debt.
- Inadequate capital for expansion since it relies on one person's savings.
- Lack of continuity (no perpetual succession); the business might die with the owner.
- The owner bears all the risks and losses alone.
- May suffer from the limited management ability or poor ideas of one person.

### 2. Partnership
**Merits:**
- More capital can be raised compared to a sole proprietorship.
- Better decision-making (two heads are better than one).
- Sharing of risks and losses among partners.
- Privacy of business affairs is maintained.
- Division of labour and specialization is possible among partners.

**Demerits:**
- Unlimited liability for general partners.
- Disagreement among partners can delay decision-making or destroy the business.
- Profits are shared, unlike the sole proprietor who takes all.
- A mistake or fraudulent act by one partner binds and affects all other partners.
- Limited lifespan; death or withdrawal of a partner can lead to dissolution.

### 3. Limited Liability Companies
**Merits:**
- **Limited liability:** Shareholders' personal assets are safe.
- Can raise massive amounts of capital, especially Public Limited Companies (by selling shares to the public).
- **Perpetual succession:** The company continues to exist even if shareholders die.
- Separation of ownership and management allows the hiring of expert managers (Directors).
- Economies of scale because they can operate on a very large scale.

**Demerits:**
- Very complex, difficult, and expensive to set up (requires legal documents and CAC registration).
- For Public Companies, there is no privacy; financial accounts must be published.
- Decision-making can be slow due to bureaucracy and the need to call board meetings.
- Lack of personal touch with employees and customers.
- Double taxation (the company pays corporate tax, and shareholders pay tax on dividends).

### 4. Cooperative Societies
**Merits:**
- Promotes unity, cooperation, and protects the interest of members.
- Democratic management prevents any single rich member from dominating (one man, one vote).
- Goods are provided at cheaper rates to members.
- Educates members in business management.
- Low interest loans are easily available to members (in thrift and credit societies).

**Demerits:**
- Inadequate capital, as members are usually low-income earners.
- Poor management because the elected committee members may lack professional business skills.
- Embezzlement and mismanagement of funds by dishonest leaders.
- Heavy reliance on members' loyalty, which can fluctuate.

### 5. Public Corporations
**Merits:**
- Prevents exploitation of consumers by providing essential services (like water and electricity) cheaply.
- Requires huge capital which only the government can provide (e.g., building railways).
- Avoids wasteful duplication of resources by creating a state monopoly in essential sectors.
- Creates massive employment opportunities for citizens.
- Ensures wealth remains in the country rather than going to foreign private owners.

**Demerits:**
- Prone to gross inefficiency and poor management.
- High levels of corruption, embezzlement, and nepotism.
- Political interference in the appointment of board members and daily operations.
- Lack of competition leads to poor service delivery.
- Red tapism and bureaucracy cause severe delays in decision-making.

## Reasons for government ownership of public enterprises

**Objective vi: state the reasons for government ownership of public enterprises.**  
**Content vi: Reasons for government ownership of public enterprises.**

Why doesn't the government just leave all businesses to private individuals? The government establishes public corporations and state-owned enterprises for several crucial reasons:

1. **Provision of essential services:** Private businesses focus on profit and might ignore poor areas. The government steps in to provide vital services (like water, mass transit, and electricity) at affordable rates for citizens.
2. **Huge capital requirement:** Some projects, like building railways, seaports, and massive dams, require billions of naira and take years to yield returns. Private individuals often cannot or will not risk such huge amounts.
3. **National security:** Strategic sectors like the minting of the national currency, production of arms and ammunition, and national broadcasting are too sensitive to be left in the hands of private businesspeople.
4. **Prevention of monopoly exploitation:** If a single private company controlled all electricity or water, they could charge extremely high prices. The government runs these to prevent citizens from being exploited.
5. **Employment generation:** The government uses large state-owned enterprises to create jobs and reduce unemployment in the country.
6. **Even development:** Private companies locate factories only where it is profitable (usually big cities). Government enterprises are often spread across the country to ensure all regions develop evenly.

## Registration of businesses

**Objective ii: analyse the procedures for registering businesses.**  
**Content ii: Registration of businesses.**

In Nigeria, the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)** is the government body responsible for the registration and regulation of business names and companies, as governed by the **Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA)**.

The procedure for registering a limited liability company involves the following steps:

**Step 1: Availability check and reservation of name.**
The promoters must submit their proposed company names to the CAC. The CAC checks its database to ensure no other company is using the name. If the name is available, it is reserved for the promoters.

**Step 2: Preparation of incorporation documents.**
The promoters must prepare the statutory documents required by law. The most important ones are:
- **Memorandum of Association:** This is the constitution of the company. It governs the relationship between the company and the outside world. It contains clauses like the Name clause, Registered Office clause, Objects clause (what the business will do), Liability clause, and Share Capital clause.
- **Articles of Association:** This document contains the internal rules and regulations guiding the day-to-day management of the company and the relationship between the shareholders and directors. It covers voting rights, payment of dividends, and how meetings are conducted.
- **Statement of Share Capital:** Details of the authorized share capital.
- **Particulars of Directors:** Names, addresses, and consent of the first directors.

**Step 3: Payment of Stamp Duties and Filing Fees.**
The documents must be stamped by the Federal Inland Revenue Service (FIRS), and the required registration fees must be paid to the CAC.

**Step 4: Submission of documents to CAC.**
The stamped documents, along with a statutory declaration of compliance by a lawyer, are submitted to the CAC.

**Step 5: Issuance of Certificate of Incorporation.**
If the CAC is satisfied that all legal requirements have been met, it issues a **Certificate of Incorporation**. This certificate is the "birth certificate" of the company. It brings the company into legal existence. 
* A private company can start business immediately upon receiving this certificate.

**Step 6: Issuance of Trading Certificate (For Public Companies only).**
A public limited company (Plc) cannot start trading just because it has a Certificate of Incorporation. It must issue a **Prospectus** (a document inviting the general public to buy its shares) and raise a minimum amount of capital. Once successful, the CAC issues a **Certificate of Trading**, allowing it to commence business operations.

## Determination of choice of business units

**Objective iii: examine the factors which determine the choice of business units.**  
**Content iii: Determination of choice of business units.**

Before starting a business, an entrepreneur must decide which form of business unit to adopt. The choice is influenced by several factors:

1. **Amount of capital required:** If the business requires small capital, a sole proprietorship is best. If it requires massive capital (like oil refining or telecom), a public limited liability company is the only realistic choice.
2. **Desire for control and independence:** If the owner wants total control over decisions and does not want to share profits or secrets, they will choose a sole proprietorship. If they don't mind sharing control, a partnership or company is fine.
3. **Degree of risk and liability:** If the business involves high risks and the owners want to protect their personal property, they will opt for a limited liability company. If the risk is low, a sole proprietorship might suffice.
4. **Legal requirements and cost of formation:** Forming a limited liability company is expensive, requires legal documents, and involves strict CAC rules. A sole proprietorship is cheap, fast, and has almost no legal formalities.
5. **Size of the market:** A business aiming to serve a small, local community can be a sole proprietorship. A business intending to operate nationwide or internationally will need the structure and capital of a limited liability company.
6. **Management ability:** A single person may lack the skills needed for a complex business. Forming a partnership or a company allows for pooling of skills, ideas, and expert management.
7. **Need for continuity:** If the founders want the business to outlive them and exist perpetually, they must incorporate it as a limited liability company.

## Dissolution and liquidation

**Objective iv: differentiate between dissolution and liquidation of business.**  
**Content iv: Dissolution and liquidation.**

Although people often use "dissolution" and "liquidation" to mean the same thing, in business law, they are two distinct concepts.

### Liquidation (Winding up)
**Liquidation** is the *process* of bringing a business entity's operations to an end. It involves appointing a liquidator to gather all the company’s assets, sell (realise) them, pay off the company's debts to creditors, and distribute any remaining money to the shareholders. 
- A company goes into liquidation when it is bankrupt (cannot pay its debts) or when members voluntarily decide to close it.

### Dissolution
**Dissolution** is the final *legal event* or official declaration that a business unit no longer exists. 
- For a **partnership**, dissolution means the partnership agreement is terminated (e.g., due to the death of a partner, mutual agreement, or court order).
- For a **company**, dissolution is the final step *after* the liquidation process is complete. The CAC strikes the company's name off its register, officially ending its life as a legal entity.

### Comparison Table

| Feature | Liquidation (Winding up) | Dissolution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | The process of selling assets to pay debts and close operations. | The legal termination or "death" of the business entity. |
| **Timing** | It is a step-by-step process that happens first (often taking months or years). | It is the final event that happens after liquidation is fully completed. |
| **Operations** | The business may still operate temporarily to complete existing contracts while being liquidated. | The business completely ceases to exist in the eyes of the law. |
| **Applicability** | Applies mostly to registered companies. | Applies to partnerships (ending the agreement) and companies (striking off the register). |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **"Unlimited liability"** is the biggest disadvantage of sole proprietorships and ordinary partnerships. Make sure you understand it: the owner's personal property can be sold to settle business debts.
- **"Separate legal entity"** applies ONLY to registered companies (Ltd and Plc) and public corporations. Sole proprietorships and partnerships are NOT separate legal entities from their owners.
- **Do not confuse the Memorandum with the Articles:** The *Memorandum* looks outward (relationship with the outside world, objects, powers), while the *Articles* look inward (internal management, voting, directors).
- **Public Companies vs Public Corporations:** A *Public Limited Company (Plc)* is a private enterprise owned by individual shareholders who bought shares. A *Public Corporation* is owned entirely by the government to provide essential services. Do not mix them up!
- **Cooperative Voting:** Remember the golden rule for cooperative societies: **"One man, one vote"**, irrespective of capital contributed. 
- **Dissolution vs Liquidation:** Liquidation is the *process* of gathering assets and paying debts. Dissolution is the *event* of legal death.

## Quick summary

1. A sole proprietorship is owned by one person, requires small capital, but suffers from unlimited liability and lack of continuity.
2. A partnership involves 2 to 20 people pooling resources; they use a Deed of Partnership to guide their relationship, but general partners also face unlimited liability.
3. Limited Liability Companies (Private and Public) are separate legal entities with perpetual succession and limited liability for owners, but they are expensive and complex to form.
4. Cooperative societies are voluntary groups that pool resources for members' welfare, sharing profits based on patronage and voting on a "one man, one vote" basis.
5. Public Corporations are government-owned businesses created by Acts of Parliament to provide essential services, avoid monopoly exploitation, and generate employment.
6. The Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) registers companies. Key documents include the Memorandum of Association and the Articles of Association.
7. Liquidation is the process of selling business assets to pay debts, while dissolution is the legal termination of the business's existence.

## Practice questions

**1. The major disadvantage of a sole proprietorship is**
- A. slow decision making
- B. unlimited liability
- C. payment of corporate tax
- D. sharing of profits

**Answer: B.** A sole proprietor has unlimited liability, meaning their personal assets can be seized to pay off business debts if the business fails. Options A and D apply to partnerships/companies. Option C applies to companies.

**2. In an ordinary partnership, the maximum number of partners allowed by traditional law is**
- A. 2
- B. 7
- C. 20
- D. 50

**Answer: C.** Historically and in most standard JAMB textbooks, an ordinary partnership is formed by a minimum of 2 and a maximum of 20 persons. 50 is the traditional maximum for a private limited company.

**3. The document that governs the internal management of a limited liability company is the**
- A. Memorandum of Association
- B. Articles of Association
- C. Certificate of Incorporation
- D. Prospectus

**Answer: B.** The Articles of Association contain the rules for internal management (e.g., meetings, directors, voting). The Memorandum governs external relationships.

**4. A business unit where voting at meetings is based on "one man, one vote" regardless of capital contributed is a**
- A. public limited company
- B. public corporation
- C. partnership
- D. cooperative society

**Answer: D.** Cooperative societies operate on democratic principles where each member has only one vote, unlike companies where voting power depends on the number of shares held.

**5. Which of the following brings a limited liability company into legal existence?**
- A. Issuance of a Trading Certificate
- B. Signing of the Memorandum of Association
- C. Issuance of the Certificate of Incorporation
- D. Publication of a Prospectus

**Answer: C.** The Certificate of Incorporation acts as the company's "birth certificate," legally creating it as a separate artificial person. A trading certificate is only required for public companies to *start trading*, not for legal existence.

**6. The process of gathering and selling the assets of a company to pay off its debts is known as**
- A. dissolution
- B. liquidation
- C. incorporation
- D. amalgamation

**Answer: B.** Liquidation (or winding up) is the process of realising assets and paying debts. Dissolution is the final legal termination that happens after liquidation is complete.

**7. A public corporation is primarily established to**
- A. maximize profit for the government
- B. compete with private limited companies
- C. provide essential services to the public
- D. sell shares to the general public

**Answer: C.** The main motive of a public corporation (like the water board or railway corporation) is to provide essential services to citizens at affordable rates, not to maximize profit.

**8. Which of the following factors strongly influences an entrepreneur's choice to form a sole proprietorship?**
- A. The need to raise massive capital
- B. The desire for total control and privacy
- C. The requirement for perpetual succession
- D. The desire to share business risks

**Answer: B.** A person choosing a sole proprietorship usually values being their own boss, making quick independent decisions, and keeping business affairs completely private.

**9. The liability of a shareholder in a public limited company is limited to**
- A. the personal assets owned by the shareholder
- B. the value of the shares held by the shareholder
- C. the total assets of the company
- D. the amount of dividends received

**Answer: B.** Limited liability means that if the company collapses, the shareholder only loses the money they invested in buying their shares.

**10. A partner who contributes capital but takes no active part in the management of the business is a**
- A. nominal partner
- B. active partner
- C. general partner
- D. sleeping partner

**Answer: D.** A sleeping (or dormant) partner contributes capital and shares in the profits but does not participate in the daily running of the business. A nominal partner merely lends their name but does not contribute capital.
