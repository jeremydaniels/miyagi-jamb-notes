## The Pre-Computing Age (Early Counting)

Before mechanical calculators and electronic computers were invented, human beings still needed to count, trade, and keep track of time and property. This era, stretching from ancient times up to the start of the mechanical era, is known as the **Pre-Computing Age**.

In the beginning, early humans used their own bodies and simple objects around them.
* **Fingers and Toes:** The earliest counting tools were human fingers (digits). This is why our modern number system is based on 10 (the decimal system).
* **Stones and Pebbles:** Used to count livestock. A shepherd would place one pebble in a pouch for every sheep that went out to graze, and remove one for every sheep that returned.
* **Sticks and Tally Marks:** Notches were carved into bones or pieces of wood (tally sticks) to keep a permanent record of numbers, debts, or days.
* **Cowry Shells and Seeds:** In many parts of Africa, including pre-colonial Nigeria, cowry shells and palm kernels were used both as money and as counting aids for trade in the markets.

While these methods were fine for small numbers, they became completely useless as populations grew, trade expanded, and complex mathematics, engineering, and astronomy developed. This need for faster, more accurate calculation led to the invention of mechanical computing devices.

## Early Computing Devices (Up to the 19th Century)

**Objective i: Identify the various computing devices since the beginning of counting/computing.**
**Content a: Pre-Computing Age - 19th century Features and components early computing devices.**
**Content c: The history behind each device.**

As mathematics grew more complex, inventors created mechanical devices to help humans calculate. The devices below are listed in historical order, tracing the evolution from simple manual tools to complex 19th-century mechanical machines.

### 1. The Abacus

**History:** The abacus is one of the first true computing devices. It was invented in ancient Babylon (around 2400 BC) and later perfected in China (around 500 BC), where it is known as the *suanpan*. 

**Features and Components:** 
* It consists of a rectangular wooden frame holding several vertical metal rods or wires.
* **Beads** are strung on these rods and can slide up and down.
* A horizontal crossbar divides the frame into two sections: an upper deck (often with one or two beads per rod, each representing a value of 5) and a lower deck (often with four or five beads per rod, each representing a value of 1).

**Uses:** It is manually operated to perform basic arithmetic: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. A skilled abacus user can calculate totals as fast as someone using a modern electronic calculator. It is still used today in parts of Asia and Africa for teaching mathematics to children.

### 2. Napier's Bones

**History:** Invented in 1617 by a Scottish mathematician named **John Napier**. Napier is also famous in mathematics for inventing logarithms.

**Features and Components:**
* It consists of a set of rectangular rods (originally made from animal bones, ivory, or wood).
* Each rod has multiplication tables written on it, divided by diagonal lines.

**Uses:** Napier's Bones were used to make **multiplication and division** much easier. By placing the rods side by side to match the digits of a large number, a user could multiply large numbers simply by adding the single digits shown in the diagonal rows, and divide by subtracting. 

### 3. The Slide Rule

**History:** Invented in 1622 by an English mathematician, **William Oughtred**. He based his invention on John Napier's concept of logarithms.

**Features and Components:**
* It looks like a ruler but has a sliding central strip.
* Both the fixed outer frame and the sliding central strip have logarithmic scales printed on them. 
* A transparent sliding cursor with a hairline helps line up the numbers.

**Uses:** By sliding the scales back and forth to add or subtract physical distances, the slide rule performs multiplication, division, and calculates roots, exponents, and trigonometry (sine, cosine, tangent). It cannot do standard addition and subtraction well. Before pocket calculators became cheap in the 1970s, the slide rule was the main calculating tool used by engineers and scientists to design everything from bridges to airplanes.

### 4. Pascal Calculator (Pascaline)

**History:** Invented in 1642 by a 19-year-old French mathematician named **Blaise Pascal** to help his father, who was a tax collector, handle huge amounts of money.

**Features and Components:**
* It was a rectangular brass box filled with a series of interlocking **gears and toothed wheels**.
* The top of the box had a row of dials numbered from 0 to 9.
* It featured an automatic **carry mechanism**: when one wheel turned past 9 to 0, a tooth on that wheel would catch the next wheel to its left and push it forward by one digit (exactly how the mileage meter on an old car dashboard works).

**Uses:** The Pascaline could perform **addition and subtraction** quickly and accurately. It could not easily multiply or divide. It is recognised as one of the first mechanical calculators.

### 5. Leibnitz Multiplier (Stepped Reckoner)

**History:** Invented in 1673 by the German mathematician and philosopher **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (JAMB syllabus spells it *Leibnitz*). He wanted to improve Pascal's machine so it could multiply automatically.

**Features and Components:**
* It used a special type of gear called a **stepped drum** (or Leibniz wheel), which is a cylinder with nine teeth of increasing lengths.
* It had moving carriages and hand cranks.

**Uses:** Unlike the Pascaline, the Leibnitz Multiplier could perform all four basic mathematical operations: **addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division**. It was highly advanced for its time, though the mechanical engineering of the 17th century was not precise enough to make it work perfectly without jamming. 

### 6. Jacquard Loom

**History:** Invented in 1801 by the French weaver **Joseph Marie Jacquard**. While it was a machine for making cloth rather than a calculator, it is one of the most important ancestors of the modern computer.

**Features and Components:**
* It was a mechanical loom for weaving fabric.
* The huge breakthrough was that the pattern to be woven was controlled by a series of heavy cardboard **punch cards** attached together in a continuous chain. 
* A hole in the card allowed a hook to pass through and lift a thread; solid cardboard stopped the hook.

**Uses:** It wove complex patterns (like flowers and leaves) into cloth automatically. 
**Contribution to Computing:** The Jacquard Loom proved that **instructions (a program) could be stored on punch cards** and fed into a machine to control its actions. This idea of storing data and instructions on punched cards was later used in almost all early computers.

[IMAGE: Illustration of the Jacquard Loom. A large wooden weaving frame. At the top, a continuous loop of heavy cardboard punch cards is feeding into the machine's mechanism, controlling the lifting of warp threads below to weave a pattern. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### 7. Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine

**History:** In the 1820s, the English mathematician **Charles Babbage** designed a massive, steam-powered mechanical calculator called the **Difference Engine** to print mathematical tables. Later, in the 1830s, he designed a much more ambitious machine called the **Analytical Engine**. Because of his work, Babbage is famously known as the **"Father of the Computer"**.

**Features and Components:**
The Analytical Engine was completely mechanical, using thousands of brass gears, but its design was identical to the architecture of modern electronic computers. It had four main parts:
1. **The Mill:** The calculating unit (equivalent to the modern CPU/ALU).
2. **The Store:** The memory, where numbers were held (equivalent to modern RAM).
3. **The Reader:** The input device, which used punch cards inspired by the Jacquard Loom.
4. **The Printer:** The output device, which printed the results on paper.

**Uses:** It was designed to be the world's first **general-purpose, programmable computer**, capable of solving any mathematical problem. 

*Note:* Babbage never finished building the Analytical Engine because it was too expensive and the engineering tools of the 1800s could not make the parts precisely enough. A mathematician named **Ada Lovelace** wrote notes explaining how the machine could calculate a sequence of numbers, making her the world's first computer programmer.

### 8. Hollerith Census Machine

**History:** In 1890, the United States population was growing so fast that it was going to take 10 years to count the census data by hand. An American inventor named **Herman Hollerith** built an electromechanical machine to speed up the process.

**Features and Components:**
* It used **punch cards** to store data about each person (e.g., age, gender, occupation). A hole punched in a specific spot meant a specific answer.
* The cards were fed into a **Tabulating Machine**. 
* Metal pins dropped onto the card. Where there was a hole, the pin passed through, touched a pool of mercury below, and completed an electrical circuit. This caused a dial to advance by one, counting the data.

**Uses:** It successfully processed the 1890 US Census in just a fraction of the time manual counting would have taken. Hollerith's invention started the data processing industry. He founded the Tabulating Machine Company, which in 1924 was renamed **International Business Machines (IBM)**, one of the biggest computer companies in history.

### 9. Burrough's Machine

**History:** Invented in the late 1880s by **William S. Burroughs** (JAMB syllabus spells it *Burrough's*). He was a bank clerk who was tired of making mistakes when adding up long lists of money.

**Features and Components:**
* It was a heavy, mechanical **adding machine** with a full keyboard of number keys.
* It had a crank handle and a built-in printer that printed the numbers and the final total on a continuous roll of **paper tape**.

**Uses:** It was primarily used by banks and accountants for reliable, fast addition of large sums of money. Because it printed the numbers, clerks could check the paper tape to ensure they hadn't made a typing mistake.

## Computing Devices of the 20th Century

**Objective ii: Discuss the contributions and uses of each of the founders of these devices: ENIAC, EDVAC, UNIVAC 1, Desktop Personal Computers, etc.**
**Content b: Computing Devices - 20th Century.**

The 20th century saw the leap from mechanical gears to electricity. World War II drove governments to fund the creation of massive, high-speed electronic calculating machines. 

### 1. ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer)

**The Founders:** ENIAC was designed and built by two American engineers, **John Mauchly** and **J. Presper Eckert**, at the University of Pennsylvania.

**The Device (Features & Uses):** 
* **Completed in:** 1945.
* **Features:** ENIAC is considered the world's first **general-purpose electronic digital computer**. It was massive, weighing 30 tons and taking up an entire large room. Instead of physical gears, it used over 18,000 **vacuum tubes** (glass bulbs that control the flow of electrons) to process calculations. It processed numbers in decimal (base 10).
* **Programming problem:** It did not store programs inside itself. To give it a new set of instructions, human operators (many of whom were brilliant women) had to physically unplug and replug hundreds of thick cables on giant switchboards, which took days.
* **Uses:** It was funded by the US military to calculate complex artillery firing tables (trajectories of bombs and shells) and to help design the hydrogen bomb.

A massive room filled with towering metal cabinets covered in dials, switches, and thick black cables plugged into various ports like an old telephone exchange. Two operators in 1940s clothing are plugging in patch cables. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### 2. EDVAC (Electronic Discrete Variable Automatic Computer)

**The Founders:** Designed by **John Mauchly**, **J. Presper Eckert**, and the brilliant Hungarian-American mathematician **John von Neumann**. 

**The Device (Features & Uses):**
* **Completed in:** 1949 (design started while ENIAC was still being built).
* **Features:** EDVAC solved the two biggest problems of ENIAC. 
    1. Instead of the decimal system, EDVAC used the **binary number system** (0s and 1s), which is much faster and simpler for electronic switches.
    2. **Stored-Program Concept:** This was John von Neumann's greatest contribution. EDVAC was designed to store its program (its instructions) electronically in its own memory alongside the data. You no longer had to rewire the machine to change the program; you just fed it new code. This architecture (the **von Neumann architecture**) is still used in computers today.
* **Uses:** Like ENIAC, it was used by the military for complex scientific and ballistics calculations, but it was much faster, smaller, and easier to reprogram.

### 3. UNIVAC 1 (Universal Automatic Computer)

**The Founders:** Once again, **John Mauchly** and **J. Presper Eckert**. After the war, they left the university and started their own business, the Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corporation, to sell computers to the public.

**The Device (Features & Uses):**
* **Completed in:** 1951.
* **Features:** UNIVAC 1 was a milestone in computing history. It was the first computer to use **magnetic tape** for storing data instead of punch cards. It was also the first computer capable of processing both alphabetical characters and numbers (text and data).
* **Uses:** It was the **first commercial computer** produced in the United States. Instead of just doing military maths, it was used for business and government administration. Its first customer was the US Census Bureau. It became famous in 1952 when it was used on live television to correctly predict that Dwight D. Eisenhower would win the US Presidential election, based on just a tiny percentage of early votes.

### 4. Desktop Personal Computers (PCs)

**The Founders:** The PC revolution was not the work of one team, but several visionary founders and companies in the 1970s and 1980s.
* **Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs:** Founded Apple Computer. Wozniak built the Apple I and Apple II. Jobs championed the idea of computers being user-friendly for everyday people.
* **Don Estridge and IBM:** Led the team that created the **IBM PC** in 1981, which became the standard design for modern personal computers.
* **Bill Gates and Paul Allen:** Founded Microsoft to create software (Operating Systems like MS-DOS and Windows) for these new machines.

**The Device (Features & Uses):**
* **Features:** Earlier computers took up whole rooms and cost millions of naira. The invention of the **microprocessor** in 1971 allowed the entire CPU to be placed on a single tiny silicon chip. This made computers small enough to fit on a desktop, cheap enough for individuals to buy, and reliable. PCs came with a keyboard for input, a monitor (screen) for output, and later featured Graphical User Interfaces (GUIs) with mice.
* **Uses:** PCs brought computing out of government laboratories and into offices, schools, and homes. They are used for word processing, accounting, education, gaming, internet browsing, and managing small businesses.

## The History Behind Each Device: An Evolutionary Summary

To master this topic for JAMB, you need to understand the *progression* of these devices. Technology evolved in clear stages:

1. **Manual Era:** Abacus, Napier's Bones, Slide Rule. People did the physical work to move parts; the device just kept track of the numbers.
2. **Mechanical Era:** Pascaline, Leibnitz Multiplier, Babbage's Analytical Engine. Precision gears and wheels automatically processed numbers. Jacquard's punch cards introduced the concept of giving a machine programmed instructions.
3. **Electromechanical Era:** Hollerith Census Machine, early IBM tabulators. Electricity was used to power the moving parts and read punch cards via electrical contacts. 
4. **Electronic Era:** ENIAC, EDVAC, UNIVAC 1, PCs. Moving gears were completely replaced by electronic switches (first vacuum tubes, then transistors, then microprocessors). The stored-program concept (EDVAC) meant the software could be changed without rebuilding the hardware.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Abacus** | An ancient calculating tool with beads on rods, used for manual arithmetic. |
| **Logarithms** | A mathematical concept used by John Napier to simplify complex multiplication into addition. |
| **Punch Cards** | Stiff pieces of paper where holes represent data or instructions. First used by Jacquard for weaving, later by Hollerith and early computers. |
| **Pascaline** | An early mechanical calculator with gears, capable of adding and subtracting. |
| **Vacuum Tubes** | Glass bulbs used in early electronic computers (like ENIAC) to control electrical signals. They were large and generated a lot of heat. |
| **General-purpose computer** | A computer designed to solve any problem if programmed to do so, unlike a calculator built for one specific task. |
| **Stored-program concept** | The idea (pioneered in EDVAC) of storing both data and instructions (the program) electronically in the computer's memory. |
| **Microprocessor** | A silicon chip that contains a computer's entire central processing unit (CPU). This invention made Desktop PCs possible. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Father of the Computer vs First Programmer:** Remember that **Charles Babbage** is the Father of the Computer (for designing the Analytical Engine), while **Ada Lovelace** is credited as the first computer programmer.
* **ENIAC vs EDVAC vs UNIVAC 1:** This is heavily tested. 
  * **ENIAC:** First general-purpose electronic computer; decimal; programmed by cables.
  * **EDVAC:** Introduced the stored-program concept and binary processing (von Neumann).
  * **UNIVAC 1:** First *commercial* computer; used magnetic tape; predicted an election.
* **Jacquard vs Hollerith:** Both used punch cards. Jacquard used them to control a weaving loom (manufacturing); Hollerith used them to process census data (data processing).
* **Leibnitz Multiplier spelling:** Do not get confused if JAMB uses "Leibnitz" instead of the standard "Leibniz". It refers to the inventor of the Stepped Reckoner.
* **Who invented what?** Know your pairs. Napier = Bones/Logarithms. Oughtred = Slide Rule. Pascal = Pascaline (addition only). Leibniz = Stepped Reckoner (add, subtract, multiply, divide).

## Quick summary

1. Early humans used fingers, stones, sticks, and cowry shells for basic counting, leading to the invention of the Abacus.
2. 17th-century inventors like Napier (Bones), Oughtred (Slide Rule), Pascal (Pascaline), and Leibniz (Multiplier) created manual and mechanical tools to speed up mathematics.
3. Joseph Marie Jacquard invented punch cards in 1801 to program weaving looms, introducing the concept of data storage.
4. Charles Babbage designed the Analytical Engine in the 1830s, featuring an input, processor, memory, and output, earning him the title "Father of the Computer".
5. Herman Hollerith used punch cards and electricity in 1890 to rapidly count the US Census, birthing the data processing industry (and IBM).
6. The 20th century saw electronic giants: ENIAC (first electronic general-purpose), EDVAC (first stored-program binary machine), and UNIVAC 1 (first commercial computer).
7. The invention of the microprocessor in the 1970s allowed pioneers like Wozniak, Jobs, and Gates to bring computing to the public through Desktop Personal Computers.

## Practice questions

**1. The computing device credited with being the first to use the concept of punched cards for storing instructions is the:**
- A. Pascaline
- B. Jacquard Loom
- C. Hollerith Census Machine
- D. Analytical Engine

**Answer: B.** Joseph Marie Jacquard used punch cards in 1801 to control patterns on his weaving loom. Hollerith later adapted this idea for census data processing.

**2. Who is known as the "Father of the Computer"?**
- A. John von Neumann
- B. Herman Hollerith
- C. Blaise Pascal
- D. Charles Babbage

**Answer: D.** Charles Babbage earned this title for designing the Analytical Engine, which had the basic architecture (input, memory, processing unit, output) of modern computers.

**3. Which of the following devices is correctly paired with its inventor?**
- A. Slide Rule - John Napier
- B. Pascaline - Gottfried Leibniz
- C. Stepped Reckoner - William Oughtred
- D. Logarithms and Bones - John Napier

**Answer: D.** John Napier invented Napier's Bones and the concept of logarithms. The Slide Rule was Oughtred, Pascaline was Pascal, and Stepped Reckoner was Leibniz.

**4. The fundamental difference between ENIAC and EDVAC was that EDVAC:**
- A. used transistors instead of vacuum tubes.
- B. was used for the US census.
- C. utilised the stored-program concept and binary arithmetic.
- D. was completely mechanical.

**Answer: C.** EDVAC improved upon ENIAC by using binary (instead of decimal) and storing its program in internal memory (the von Neumann architecture). Neither used transistors; they both used vacuum tubes.

**5. The first commercially produced electronic computer sold to businesses and government was the:**
- A. UNIVAC 1
- B. ENIAC
- C. IBM PC
- D. Analytical Engine

**Answer: A.** UNIVAC 1, created by Eckert and Mauchly in 1951, was the first commercial computer in the US, used by the Census Bureau and for predicting elections.

**6. Which 19th-century electromechanical machine was built specifically to process population data rapidly?**
- A. Babbage's Difference Engine
- B. Hollerith Census Machine
- C. Burrough's Adding Machine
- D. Leibnitz Multiplier

**Answer: B.** Herman Hollerith built his punch-card tabulating machine to process the 1890 US Census data.

**7. The mathematical concept that formed the basis for the invention of the Slide Rule is:**
- A. Algebra
- B. Boolean logic
- C. Binary arithmetic
- D. Logarithms

**Answer: D.** The Slide Rule uses printed logarithmic scales, a concept developed by John Napier, to perform multiplication and division by sliding the scales to add physical distances.

**8. John von Neumann's major contribution to the development of computers was the:**
- A. invention of vacuum tubes.
- B. stored-program architecture.
- C. creation of the first high-level language.
- D. invention of the microprocessor.

**Answer: B.** Von Neumann proposed the architecture where a computer stores both its instructions (programs) and its data in the same electronic memory, which was first implemented in designs like EDVAC.

**9. The Pascal Calculator (Pascaline) was limited mainly to which mathematical operations?**
- A. Multiplication and division
- B. Finding square roots
- C. Addition and subtraction
- D. Logarithmic equations

**Answer: C.** The Pascaline, with its interlocking gears, was built to add and subtract. The later Leibnitz Multiplier added the ability to easily multiply and divide.

**10. What major technological component made the development of Desktop Personal Computers possible in the 1970s?**
- A. The vacuum tube
- B. The punched card
- C. The magnetic tape
- D. The microprocessor

**Answer: D.** The microprocessor put the entire CPU onto a single tiny silicon chip, vastly reducing the size and cost of computers, allowing them to fit on a desktop.
