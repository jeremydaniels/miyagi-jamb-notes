# JAMB Crk Notes: Opposition to the Gospel Message

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section C, Topic 18 of the JAMB CRK syllabus: Opposition to the Gospel Message. It covers the various ways the early Christians faced resistance, violence, and imprisonment from both Jewish and Roman authorities as they spread the teachings of Jesus Christ. 

The syllabus covers four main content areas:
- **a.** The arrest and imprisonment of Peter and John (Acts 3; 4:1-22; 5:17-42; 12:1-24)
- **b.** The martyrdom of Stephen (Acts 6:8-15; 7)
- **c.** Persecution by Saul (Acts 8:1-3; 9:1-2 cf. Gal. 1:11-17)
- **d.** Persecution of Paul (Acts 16:11-40; 19:23-41; 21:27-36 cf. 2 Cor. 11:23-33)

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
1. Trace the story of the arrest and imprisonment of Peter and John.
2. Trace the events that led to the martyrdom of Stephen.
3. Describe the role of Saul in the persecution of the Church.
4. Evaluate the importance of persecution to the growth of the Church.
5. Account for the persecution of Paul.

*(Note on the syllabus wording: The printed syllabus misses a semicolon between Acts 5:17-42 and 12:1-24 in content item (a), and uses a colon instead of a period in 2 Cor:11:23-33 in content item (d). These are minor typographical errors, and the correct passages are taught in these notes.)*

## The arrest and imprisonment of Peter and John

**Objective i: trace the story of the arrest and imprisonment of Peter and John.**
**Content a: The arrest and imprisonment of Peter and John**

After the coming of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, the apostles began to preach boldly in Jerusalem. This immediately brought them into conflict with the Jewish religious leaders (the Sanhedrin), who wanted to stop the spread of Christianity. The early chapters of Acts record three major instances of the apostles being arrested and imprisoned.

### 1. The first arrest (Acts 3 and 4:1-22)

The first opposition was triggered by a miracle. 
- **The healing:** Peter and John went to the temple at the "hour of prayer" (the ninth hour, or 3:00 PM). At the gate called Beautiful, they met a man who had been lame from birth, begging for alms. Peter told him, "Silver and gold have I none; but such as I have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk" (Acts 3:6). The man was instantly healed and began walking, leaping, and praising God.
- **The sermon and arrest:** The crowd was amazed. Peter used the opportunity to preach that Jesus, whom the Jews had crucified, was the source of this healing. As they spoke, the priests, the captain of the temple, and the Sadducees came upon them. The Sadducees were particularly angry because Peter and John were teaching the resurrection of the dead (a doctrine the Sadducees did not believe in). They arrested Peter and John and put them in custody until the next day. By this time, the number of male believers had grown to about five thousand.
- **The trial:** The next day, the Jewish council (including Annas the high priest, Caiaphas, John, and Alexander) asked the apostles by what power or name they had done this. Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, boldly declared that the healing was done by the name of Jesus Christ, emphasizing that there is salvation in no other name. 
- **The outcome:** The council marvelled at the boldness of Peter and John, noting that they were "unlearned and ignorant men," but recognized they had been with Jesus. Because the healed man was standing right there, the council could not deny the miracle. They strictly commanded the apostles not to speak or teach in the name of Jesus. Peter and John replied, "Whether it be right in the sight of God to hearken unto you more than unto God, judge ye." Finding no way to punish them because the people were praising God, the council let them go.

### 2. The second arrest (Acts 5:17-42)

Despite the warnings, the apostles continued to perform many signs and wonders, and the church grew rapidly.
- **The arrest and angelic rescue:** The high priest and the Sadducees, filled with jealousy (indignation), arrested the apostles and put them in the common prison. But during the night, an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors, brought them out, and commanded them to go stand in the temple and speak "all the words of this life."
- **The second trial:** The next morning, the council met and sent officers to the prison, but found it empty, though the doors were locked and guards were standing outside. Someone then reported that the apostles were back in the temple teaching. The captain and officers brought them back to the council, but without violence, because they feared the people would stone them.
- **Peter's defense:** The high priest accused them of filling Jerusalem with their doctrine and intending to bring Jesus' blood upon the council. Peter and the other apostles famously replied, "We ought to obey God rather than men."
- **Gamaliel's advice:** The council was furious and wanted to kill them. However, a respected Pharisee and teacher of the law named **Gamaliel** stood up. He reminded the council of previous rebel leaders, Theudas and Judas of Galilee, who had gathered followers but eventually died, causing their movements to scatter. Gamaliel advised: "Refrain from these men, and let them alone: for if this counsel or this work be of men, it will come to nought: But if it be of God, ye cannot overthrow it; lest haply ye be found even to fight against God."
- **The outcome:** The council agreed with Gamaliel. They beat the apostles, commanded them again not to speak in the name of Jesus, and released them. The apostles left the council rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for Jesus' name, and they continued teaching daily.

### 3. Persecution by King Herod Agrippa I (Acts 12:1-24)

Years later, political authorities also began persecuting the church.
- **The death of James and arrest of Peter:** King Herod Agrippa I stretched out his hands to harass the church. He killed **James the brother of John** with the sword. Seeing that this pleased the Jews, he proceeded to arrest Peter during the days of Unleavened Bread, intending to bring him before the people after Passover.
- **The miraculous escape:** Peter was kept in prison, guarded by four squads of soldiers, and bound with two chains between two soldiers. Meanwhile, the church prayed constantly for him. On the night before Herod was to bring him out, an angel of the Lord appeared. A light shone, the angel struck Peter on the side to wake him, and the chains fell off his hands. The angel led him past the guards and through the iron city gate, which opened on its own. 
- **Rhoda and the believers:** Peter went to the house of Mary (the mother of John Mark), where many were praying. A servant girl named **Rhoda** answered his knock. Recognizing his voice, she was so overjoyed she forgot to open the gate and ran to tell the others. They thought she was mad or that it was his angel, but Peter kept knocking. When they opened the door, they were astonished. 
- **The aftermath:** In the morning, there was a great stir among the soldiers. Herod examined the guards and ordered them to be executed. Shortly after, Herod went to Caesarea. While delivering an oration, the people shouted, "It is the voice of a god, and not of a man." Because Herod did not give the glory to God, an angel of the Lord struck him; he was eaten by worms and died. Despite this severe opposition, "the word of God grew and multiplied."

## The martyrdom of Stephen

**Objective ii: trace the events that led to the martyrdom of Stephen.**
**Content b: The martyrdom of Stephen**

Stephen was one of the seven men (often called deacons) chosen by the early church to handle the daily distribution of food to widows (Acts 6). However, Stephen's ministry went beyond food distribution. He was described as a man "full of faith and power," who did great wonders and miracles among the people. 

### Opposition from the Synagogue
Stephen's powerful preaching attracted fierce opposition from members of the **Synagogue of the Freedmen** (comprising Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia, and Asia). They argued with Stephen but "were not able to resist the wisdom and the spirit by which he spake." 

Because they could not win the debate fairly, they secretly bribed (suborned) men to serve as false witnesses. These men stirred up the people, the elders, and the scribes, who then seized Stephen and brought him before the Sanhedrin.

### The False Accusations
The false witnesses brought two main charges against Stephen:
1. **Blasphemy against God and Moses:** They claimed he spoke against the Law of Moses.
2. **Blasphemy against the Temple:** They claimed he said that Jesus of Nazareth would destroy the temple and change the customs handed down by Moses.

As the council looked at Stephen, they saw that his face was "as it had been the face of an angel."

### Stephen's Defense (Acts 7)
The high priest asked Stephen if the charges were true. Stephen responded with a long, brilliant speech summarizing the history of Israel. He traced God's dealings with Abraham, Joseph, Moses, and the Israelites in the wilderness. 

Stephen's main arguments were:
- God does not dwell only in man-made temples (proving he respected God, but showing that the Jews placed too much emphasis on the physical building).
- Throughout history, the Israelites had a habit of rejecting the deliverers God sent them (like Joseph and Moses).
- He concluded with a sharp rebuke: "Ye stiffnecked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, ye do always resist the Holy Ghost: as your fathers did, so do ye." He accused them of murdering the prophets and, most recently, betraying and murdering the "Just One" (Jesus Christ).

### The Stoning of Stephen
When the council heard this, they were "cut to the heart" and gnashed their teeth in extreme anger. 
- Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, looked up to heaven and said, "Behold, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing on the right hand of God."
- This claim to see Jesus at God's right hand was considered the ultimate blasphemy by the council. They cried out with a loud voice, stopped their ears, and rushed at him.
- They cast him out of the city and began to **stone** him. The witnesses who started the stoning laid their outer garments at the feet of a young man named **Saul** (who later became Paul).
- As he was dying, Stephen made two final prayers, similar to Jesus on the cross: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit," and "Lord, lay not this sin to their charge." He then "fell asleep" (died). 

Stephen became the first Christian **martyr** (someone killed for their faith).

## Persecution by Saul

**Objective iii: describe the role of Saul in the persecution of the Church.**
**Content c: Persecution by Saul**

Saul of Tarsus (a Pharisee and a student of Gamaliel) was present at Stephen's death and "was consenting unto his death" (Acts 8:1). Following Stephen's martyrdom, a great wave of persecution broke out against the church in Jerusalem.

### Saul's actions in Jerusalem (Acts 8:1-3)
Saul became the chief persecutor of the early Christians. The Bible says he "made havoc of the church." He aggressively hunted believers, entering house after house, and dragging out both men and women to throw them into prison. Because of this severe persecution, all the believers—except the apostles—were scattered throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria.

### Expanding the persecution to Damascus (Acts 9:1-2)
Saul's hatred for Christianity was so intense that he was not satisfied with destroying the church in Jerusalem. He was "breathing out threatenings and slaughter against the disciples of the Lord." 
He went to the high priest and requested official letters addressed to the synagogues in **Damascus** (a city in Syria). Saul's goal was to find anyone belonging to "the Way" (as Christianity was initially called), whether men or women, and bring them bound as prisoners back to Jerusalem. It was on this journey to Damascus that Saul dramatically encountered the risen Jesus and was converted.

### Paul's own reflection on his past (Galatians 1:11-17)
Years later, writing as the Apostle Paul, he reflected on his former life. He admitted that he "persecuted the church of God beyond measure, and wasted it." He explained that his actions were driven by extreme religious zeal: he was highly advanced in the Jewish religion (Judaism) and fiercely passionate about the traditions of his fathers. 

Paul emphasized that his transformation was entirely due to God's grace. When God "revealed his Son in me," Paul did not immediately consult with "flesh and blood" (other human beings, including the apostles in Jerusalem). Instead, he went away to **Arabia**, and later returned to Damascus. This proved that his new message (the gospel) came directly by revelation from Jesus Christ, not from any human teacher.

## Persecution of Paul

**Objective v: account for the persecution of Paul.**
**Content d: Persecution of Paul**

After his conversion, Paul became the greatest missionary of the early church, taking the gospel to the Gentile world. However, the persecutor became the persecuted. Paul faced immense opposition from Jewish traditionalists, Roman authorities, and pagan idol worshippers. 

The syllabus highlights three specific instances of Paul's persecution in different cities, followed by his own summary of his sufferings.

### Paul's summary of his sufferings (2 Corinthians 11:23-33)
In his letter to the Corinthians, Paul was forced to defend his apostleship against "false apostles." To prove his dedication, he listed a catalogue of the intense physical and emotional suffering he had endured for the gospel:
- **Beatings:** Five times he received from the Jews the "forty stripes save one" (39 lashes); three times he was beaten with rods (a Roman punishment).
- **Near-death experiences:** He was stoned once (at Lystra) and was often in danger of death.
- **Shipwrecks:** He suffered shipwreck three times, and spent a night and a day floating in the deep sea.
- **Constant perils:** He faced dangers from rivers, robbers, his own countrymen (Jews), the heathen (Gentiles), in the city, in the wilderness, in the sea, and among false brethren.
- **Hardships:** He endured weariness, pain, many sleepless nights (watchings), hunger, thirst, frequent fasting, cold, and nakedness.
- **Escape from Damascus:** He recalled how the governor under King Aretas guarded Damascus to arrest him, but he escaped by being let down in a basket through a window in the city wall.
- **Emotional burden:** On top of all this physical suffering, he carried the daily stress and "care of all the churches."

## Importance of persecution to the growth of the Church

**Objective iv: evaluate the importance of persecution to the growth of the Church.**

At first glance, persecution seems like it would destroy a religion. The Sanhedrin, King Herod, and the Roman authorities certainly intended to wipe out Christianity. However, persecution actually fuelled the growth of the early church in several vital ways:

1. **Geographical expansion of the Gospel:** Jesus had commanded the apostles to preach in Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, and the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). However, the early believers initially stayed in Jerusalem. It was the intense persecution following Stephen's death that forced them out. Acts 8:4 states, "Therefore they that were scattered abroad went every where preaching the word." For example, Philip went to Samaria, breaking cultural barriers.
2. **Strengthening of faith and boldness:** The arrests of Peter, John, and Paul did not silence them; it made them bolder. When Peter and John were beaten, they rejoiced that they were counted worthy to suffer for Christ. This courage inspired other believers to stand firm.
3. **Demonstration of God's power:** The imprisonments provided opportunities for miraculous deliverances (e.g., angels releasing Peter and John; the earthquake in Philippi). These miracles proved that God was with the church, leading to more conversions (like the Philippian jailer).
4. **Church unity and prayer:** Persecution forced the believers to rely entirely on God and each other. When Peter was in prison awaiting execution, the church gathered for earnest, unbroken prayer, demonstrating deep spiritual unity.
5. **Transformation of enemies:** The zeal that made Saul the greatest persecutor of the church was redirected by God's grace to make him the greatest missionary (Paul) to the Gentiles. His persecution of the church ultimately led him to Damascus, where he met Christ.
6. **Relevance today:** In modern times, including in parts of Nigeria, Christians sometimes face opposition, mockery, or even violence for their faith. The history of the early church teaches believers to remain steadfast, avoid retaliating with violence, rely on prayer, and trust that God's purpose cannot be overthrown by human anger.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Differentiate the arrests of Peter and John:** 
  - **First arrest:** Caused by healing the lame man. Ended with a warning.
  - **Second arrest:** Arrested by Sadducees, freed by an angel, recaptured, Gamaliel advised the council, ended with a beating.
  - **Third arrest (Peter alone):** Arrested by King Herod Agrippa I, freed by an angel, went to Rhoda's house.
- **Know Gamaliel's argument:** He cited past failed rebellions (Theudas, Judas of Galilee) to argue that if Christianity was just a human invention, it would die out on its own.
- **Know the exact charges against Stephen:** Blasphemy against Moses, God, the law, and the temple. He was *not* accused of tax evasion or political treason against Rome.
- **Match Paul's persecutions to the correct city:**
  - *Philippi:* Slave girl with a spirit of divination; jailer converted.
  - *Ephesus:* Demetrius the silversmith; riot in the theatre.
  - *Jerusalem:* Accused of bringing a Greek into the temple; rescued by Roman soldiers.
- **Paul's immediate action after conversion:** According to Galatians, he did *not* go to Jerusalem to meet the apostles right away; he went to Arabia.

## Quick summary

1. Peter and John were arrested by Jewish authorities for healing a lame man and preaching the resurrection. Despite threats and beatings, they refused to stop preaching.
2. King Herod Agrippa I killed James and arrested Peter, but God miraculously delivered Peter through an angel in response to the church's prayers.
3. Stephen, a deacon full of faith, was falsely accused of blasphemy. After a bold historical defense, he became the first Christian martyr by stoning.
4. Saul of Tarsus aggressively persecuted the early church, dragging believers to prison in Jerusalem and seeking letters to arrest Christians in Damascus.
5. After his conversion, Paul faced severe persecution himself, including imprisonment in Philippi, a massive riot by silversmiths in Ephesus, and mob violence in Jerusalem.
6. In 2 Corinthians 11, Paul summarized his physical sufferings for the gospel, which included being whipped 39 times on five occasions, beaten with rods, stoned, and shipwrecked.
7. Persecution did not destroy the church; rather, it forced believers to scatter to new regions, spreading the gospel geographically and strengthening their reliance on God.

## Practice questions

**1. The immediate cause of the first arrest of Peter and John by the temple authorities was their**
- A. refusal to pay taxes to the Roman government
- B. healing of a lame man and teaching the resurrection of Jesus
- C. attempt to rebuild the temple walls
- D. violent protest against the Sadducees

**Answer: B.** They were arrested because they healed the lame man at the Beautiful Gate and used the miracle to preach Jesus and the resurrection of the dead, which angered the Sadducees (Acts 4:1-2).

**2. Who advised the Sanhedrin to leave the apostles alone, arguing that if their work was of God, it could not be overthrown?**
- A. Caiaphas
- B. Annas
- C. Gamaliel
- D. Nicodemus

**Answer: C.** Gamaliel, a respected Pharisee, gave this advice during the second trial of the apostles, saving them from execution (Acts 5:34-39).

**3. The false witnesses set up against Stephen accused him of speaking blasphemous words against**
- A. the Roman Emperor and the governor
- B. Moses and God
- C. the Pharisees and the Sadducees
- D. the synagogue and the high priest

**Answer: B.** The members of the Synagogue of the Freedmen bribed men to say, "We have heard him speak blasphemous words against Moses, and against God" (Acts 6:11).

**4. According to Acts 9, Saul requested letters from the high priest to the synagogues in which city, so that he could arrest believers?**
- A. Antioch
- B. Ephesus
- C. Damascus
- D. Tarsus

**Answer: C.** Saul was on his way to Damascus with letters from the high priest to arrest Christians when he encountered Jesus (Acts 9:1-2).

**5. In Galatians 1, Paul states that after God revealed His Son to him, he did not immediately go to Jerusalem, but went to**
- A. Arabia
- B. Syria
- C. Cilicia
- D. Cyprus

**Answer: A.** Paul wrote that he did not confer with flesh and blood, nor go up to Jerusalem, but went into Arabia before returning to Damascus (Galatians 1:16-17).

**6. The uproar against Paul in Ephesus was instigated by Demetrius because Paul's preaching**
- A. caused a slave girl to lose her ability to tell the future
- B. threatened the trade of craftsmen making shrines of the goddess Diana
- C. encouraged the Jews to stop paying tribute to Caesar
- D. defiled the Jewish temple

**Answer: B.** Demetrius, a silversmith, started the riot in Ephesus because Paul's teaching against idolatry was hurting his business of making silver shrines of Diana (Acts 19:24-27).

**7. During Paul's final visit to Jerusalem, Jews from Asia stirred up the crowd and tried to kill him, falsely accusing him of**
- A. claiming to be the Messiah
- B. stealing gold from the temple treasury
- C. bringing Greeks into the temple and defiling the holy place
- D. organizing a rebellion against the Roman chief captain

**Answer: C.** The mob attacked Paul because they wrongly assumed he had brought Trophimus, an Ephesian Gentile, into the restricted area of the temple (Acts 21:28-29).

**8. King Herod Agrippa I arrested Peter during the days of Unleavened Bread immediately after he had**
- A. exiled John to the island of Patmos
- B. executed Stephen by stoning
- C. killed James the brother of John with the sword
- D. been eaten by worms

**Answer: C.** Acts 12:1-3 records that Herod killed James the brother of John with the sword, and because it pleased the Jews, he proceeded to arrest Peter.

**9. A major result of the intense persecution of the early church following the death of Stephen was that**
- A. the apostles fled to Rome to seek the Emperor's protection
- B. believers were scattered and went everywhere preaching the word
- C. the church decided to stop preaching in the name of Jesus
- D. the Sanhedrin officially accepted Christianity

**Answer: B.** The persecution forced the believers out of Jerusalem, causing them to scatter throughout Judea and Samaria, where they preached the gospel wherever they went (Acts 8:4).

**10. In Paul's catalogue of his sufferings in 2 Corinthians 11, which of the following is NOT mentioned?**
- A. Being beaten with rods three times
- B. Being crucified upside down
- C. Being stoned once
- D. Suffering shipwreck three times

**Answer: B.** In 2 Corinthians 11:24-25, Paul mentions beatings, rods, stoning, and shipwrecks. Crucifixion upside down is the traditional account of Peter's death, not mentioned in Paul's biblical catalogue of sufferings.
