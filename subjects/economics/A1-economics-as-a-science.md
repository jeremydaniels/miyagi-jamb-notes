## Why Economics is a Science

Before diving into the concepts, it is important to understand why economics is called a science. Economics is a **social science**. Unlike physical sciences (like physics or chemistry) that study matter in a laboratory, economics studies **human behaviour** in society. 

It is considered a science because it adopts the **scientific method** to study how people make decisions. Economists observe reality, collect data, formulate hypotheses, test them, and establish economic laws or theories (for example, the Law of Demand). 

### Definitions of Economics
JAMB frequently tests your knowledge of the historical definitions of economics and the economists who proposed them:
1. **Adam Smith (Classical, 1776):** Defined economics as "an inquiry into the nature and causes of the wealth of nations." He is widely regarded as the father of modern economics.
2. **Alfred Marshall (Neo-classical, 1890):** Defined economics as "the study of mankind in the ordinary business of life."
3. **Lionel Robbins (Modern, 1932):** Defined economics as *"the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses."* 

**Lionel Robbins' definition** is the most widely accepted today because it touches on the three core pillars of economics: **wants** (ends), **scarcity** (scarce means), and **choice** (alternative uses).

## Basic Concepts in Economics

The following concepts form the building blocks of all economic theory.

### Wants (Ends)
**Wants** are the desires of human beings for goods and services that give satisfaction. Examples include the desire for food, clothing, shelter, smartphones, and education. 
- **Characteristics of wants:** Wants are **insatiable** (unlimited) because human desires are endless. When one want is satisfied, another arises. Wants are also **competitive**, meaning they compete for the limited resources in our pockets. 

### Scarcity (Scarce Means)
**Scarcity** refers to the limited supply of resources relative to the unlimited human wants. Resources (such as land, money, time, and raw materials) are never enough to satisfy all human desires. 
- **The Fundamental Economic Problem:** Scarcity is the central problem of economics. If resources were infinite, there would be no need for economics. Every society, rich or poor, faces scarcity.
- **Economic goods vs Free goods:** Because of scarcity, most goods are **economic goods** (they have a price, like garri, shoes, or petrol). **Free goods** (like the air we breathe or sunshine) are abundant and have no price, though environmental changes are making pure free goods rarer.

### Choice
Because resources are scarce and wants are unlimited, an individual, firm, or government cannot have everything they want. Therefore, they must make a **choice**. Choice is the act of selecting one alternative out of a number of available alternatives. Choice arises directly because of scarcity.

### Scale of Preference
A **scale of preference** is a list of an individual's unsatisfied wants arranged in descending order of their relative importance. 
- The most pressing or urgent want is placed at the top of the list, while the least urgent is placed at the bottom. 
- **Importance:** It helps consumers make rational choices and maximize their satisfaction within the limits of their scarce resources (income). 

*Example:* A secondary school student in Lagos has ₦10,000. Her wants are: a JAMB past questions textbook (₦6,000), a new school bag (₦4,000), and a pair of sandals (₦5,000). Her scale of preference might look like this:
1. JAMB Textbook (₦6,000)
2. School bag (₦4,000)
3. Sandals (₦5,000)
Since she only has ₦10,000, she will choose items 1 and 2, and leave the sandals for later.

### Opportunity Cost
**Opportunity cost** is defined as the real cost of a commodity in terms of the **next best alternative forgone**. When you make a choice, you sacrifice another option. The item you sacrificed is the opportunity cost. It is also known as the *real cost* or *true cost*.

*Example:* In the scale of preference example above, if the student had only ₦6,000 and had to choose between the JAMB Textbook (₦6,000) and a slightly more expensive combination of the bag and something else, or let's say she must choose between the Textbook (₦6,000) and the Sandals (which we assume she negotiated down to ₦6,000). If she chooses the Textbook, the opportunity cost is the **Sandals** (the alternative she had to give up).

Opportunity cost applies to everyone:
- **Individuals:** Choosing to study for three hours instead of going to a party. The opportunity cost is the enjoyment of the party.
- **Firms:** A farmer choosing to plant cassava instead of maize on his plot of land. The opportunity cost is the maize forgone.
- **Government:** The Nigerian government choosing to spend ₦50 billion on building a new bridge instead of upgrading hospitals. The opportunity cost is the hospital upgrade forgone.

### Rationality
In economics, human beings are assumed to be **rational** (often referred to as *Homo economicus* or "economic man"). Rationality means that decision-makers act purposefully to achieve the maximum possible benefit.
- A **rational consumer** will spend their money in a way that maximizes their satisfaction (utility).
- A **rational producer (firm)** will allocate resources in a way that maximizes their profit.
- A **rational government** will implement policies that maximize the welfare of its citizens.

### Production, Distribution and Consumption
These are the three major economic activities that happen in every society.
1. **Production:** This is the creation of goods and services (wealth) to satisfy human wants. In economics, production is not complete until the goods reach the final consumer. It involves adding *utility* (usefulness) to raw materials. 
2. **Distribution:** This is the process of moving goods and services from the point of production to the final consumers. It involves wholesalers, retailers, transportation, and logistics. 
3. **Consumption:** This is the final use of goods and services to satisfy human wants. When you eat a plate of jollof rice or use a pen to write an exam, you are engaged in consumption.

## Comparing Various Concepts and Their Applications

**Objective i: compare various concepts in economics and their applications.**

Understanding the differences between closely related economic terms is crucial for the UTME. 

| Concept | Concept | Comparison and Differences |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scarcity** | **Choice** | **Scarcity** is the reality that resources are limited. **Choice** is the action taken as a *result* of scarcity. Without scarcity, choice would not be necessary. |
| **Wants** | **Demand** | A **want** is merely a desire (e.g., wanting a private jet without having money). **Demand** is a desire that is backed by the ability and willingness to pay for it at a specific price. |
| **Money Cost** | **Opportunity Cost** | **Money cost** (or financial cost) is the amount of money spent to acquire a good (e.g., spending ₦500 on a loaf of bread). **Opportunity cost** is the *next best alternative forgone* (e.g., the packet of biscuits you could have bought instead of the bread). |
| **Scale of Preference** | **Opportunity Cost** | A **scale of preference** lists all alternatives in order of importance, guiding the choice. **Opportunity cost** is the specific item that ranks directly below the chosen item (the immediate sacrifice). |

## The Fundamental Economic Problems

**Content b.(i): Economic problems of what, how and for whom to produce and efficiency of resource use.**

Every society—whether capitalist like the USA, socialist like Cuba, or mixed like Nigeria—faces fundamental economic problems because of scarcity. Society must decide how to allocate its scarce resources. These problems are grouped into four main questions:

### What to produce?
Since resources are scarce, society cannot produce everything. It must decide which goods and services to produce and in what quantities. 
- Should the country produce more consumer goods (like clothing and food) or more capital goods (like tractors and factory machines)? 
- Should Nigeria invest its land in growing food crops (yam, cassava) or cash crops (cocoa, rubber)? 
This decision depends on the needs of the society and the resources available.

### How to produce?
This is the problem of choosing the **technique of production**. Goods can be produced in different ways. The two main techniques are:
1. **Labour-intensive technique:** Using more human labour and fewer machines. This is highly applicable in developing countries like Nigeria where the population is high, labour is cheap, and capital (machinery) is scarce. It helps reduce unemployment.
2. **Capital-intensive technique:** Using more machinery and advanced technology with less human labour. This is common in developed countries or specific industries like crude oil extraction in the Niger Delta. It ensures high speed and mass production.

### For whom to produce?
This problem relates to the **distribution of national output (wealth)**. Once goods are produced, who gets to consume them? 
- Should goods be distributed equally among all citizens?
- Should they be distributed based on people's needs?
- Or should they be distributed based on purchasing power (only to those who can afford them)? 
In most modern economies, purchasing power determines who gets what, which often leads to inequality between the rich and the poor.

### Efficiency of resource use
This relates to making sure that resources are not wasted. Efficiency is divided into two parts:
1. **Productive efficiency:** Producing goods at the lowest possible cost, without wasting raw materials, time, or labour.
2. **Allocative efficiency:** Producing the specific combination of goods and services that society actually wants and values most.

## Identifying Economic Problems in Practice

**Objective iii: identify economic problems.**

Economists identify these economic problems by observing societal conditions and economic indicators:
- When a country experiences **severe shortages** of essential goods and massive queues at petrol stations or food markets, it is identifying a problem with **"What to produce"** and **Efficiency**.
- When a country has a very high **unemployment rate** among its youth, it points to a problem with **"How to produce"** (perhaps relying too heavily on imported technology instead of local labour-intensive methods).
- When a nation has extreme wealth inequality (a few billionaires and millions living in extreme poverty), economists identify a severe problem with **"For whom to produce"** (the distribution of wealth).

## Proffering Solutions to Economic Problems

**Objective iv: proffer solutions to economic problems.**

How a country solves these fundamental economic problems depends on its **economic system** (which is covered in detail in `A2-economic-systems.md`). However, as a quick overview:

1. **In a Capitalist (Free Market) Economy:** The problems are solved by the **price mechanism** (the invisible hand of demand and supply).
   - *What to produce?* Whatever consumers demand and what brings the highest profit.
   - *How to produce?* The cheapest, most efficient method to maximize profit.
   - *For whom to produce?* Those who have the money to pay for it.
2. **In a Socialist (Command) Economy:** The problems are solved by **government planning**. The state dictates what is produced based on citizens' needs, uses methods that ensure full employment, and distributes goods equitably.
3. **In a Mixed Economy:** Both the price mechanism and the government work together. For instance, in Nigeria, the private sector decides what consumer goods to produce, while the government steps in to provide public goods (like roads and defence) and passes laws to ensure fair distribution.

## The Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)

**Content b.(ii): Application of PPF to solution of economic problems.**

### What is the PPF?
The **Production Possibility Frontier** (also known as the Production Possibility Curve - PPC, or Transformation Curve) is a graph that shows the various combinations of two goods or services that a country can produce using its available resources and technology efficiently.

**Assumptions of the PPF:**
To draw the PPF, economists make several simplifying assumptions:
1. The country produces only **two commodities** (e.g., Yam and Cassava, or Consumer goods and Capital goods).
2. The quantity of resources (land, labour, capital) is **fixed**.
3. The state of technology is **constant** (no new inventions happen during the period).
4. All resources are **fully and efficiently employed** (no wasted resources or unemployment).

### PPF Schedule and Opportunity Cost

A schedule is a table showing data. Let us look at a hypothetical PPF schedule for a country producing Yam and Cassava.

| Production Point | Yam (Million Tonnes) | Cassava (Million Tonnes) | Opportunity Cost (Sacrifice) for additional Yam |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A | 0 | 100 | - |
| B | 10 | 90 | 10m Tonnes of Cassava |
| C | 20 | 70 | 20m Tonnes of Cassava |
| D | 30 | 40 | 30m Tonnes of Cassava |
| E | 40 | 0 | 40m Tonnes of Cassava |

**Interpreting the Schedule:**
- At **Point A**, the country uses all its resources to produce 100m tonnes of Cassava and 0 Yam.
- To produce the first 10m tonnes of Yam (moving to **Point B**), it must move resources away from Cassava farming. Cassava production falls from 100 to 90. The **opportunity cost** is 10m tonnes of Cassava.
- Notice that as we want more and more Yam, the opportunity cost (the amount of Cassava we must give up) keeps increasing: 10, then 20, then 30, then 40. 
- This is called the **Law of Increasing Opportunity Cost**. It happens because resources are not perfectly adaptable. Land that is excellent for Cassava might be poor for Yam. As you force more unsuitable land into Yam production, you lose large amounts of Cassava for only small gains in Yam.

### Interpreting Graphs and Schedules in Economics

**Objective ii: interpret graphs/schedules in relation to the concepts.**

If we plot the schedule above on a graph, with Yam on the horizontal (X) axis and Cassava on the vertical (Y) axis, we get a curve that bows outward from the origin.

**How to Read the PPF Graph:**
1. **The Downward Slope:** The curve slopes downwards from left to right. This negative slope illustrates **opportunity cost**. To get more of one good on the X-axis, you must give up some of the good on the Y-axis.
2. **The Concave Shape (Bowed-out):** The curve is bowed outward from the origin because of **increasing opportunity cost**, as explained by the imperfect adaptability of resources. 

### Application of PPF to Solution of Economic Problems

The PPF is a powerful tool because it visually demonstrates the basic economic concepts and problems:

1. **Scarcity:** Illustrated by the boundary of the curve itself and the unattainable region outside it. Point **F** (outside the curve) represents a combination of goods the country desires but cannot currently produce because resources are scarce. 
2. **Choice:** Illustrated by the need to select a single point **on** the curve. A country cannot be at Point B and Point C at the same time; it must choose.
3. **Opportunity Cost:** Illustrated by the movement along the curve (the negative slope). Moving from B to C means sacrificing Cassava for Yam. 
4. **Efficiency:** Any point strictly **on** the curve (Points A, B, C, D, E) represents productive efficiency and full employment of resources.
5. **Inefficiency and Unemployment:** Point **U** (inside the curve) shows that the country is not using all its resources. People are unemployed, or factories are idle. The economy can produce more of both goods without sacrificing anything if it simply employs its idle resources to reach the curve.
6. **Economic Growth:** If the country discovers new resources (e.g., Nigeria discovering oil) or invents new technology (better farming methods), the entire PPF curve will **shift outwards to the right**. This solves the problem of scarcity temporarily by making previously unattainable points (like Point F) attainable.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Money cost vs Opportunity cost:** A very common JAMB trap. If a question gives you monetary values and asks for the *opportunity cost*, the answer is **never** the money amount. It is the *item* that was given up. 
- **The Core Problem:** Whenever a question asks for the "fundamental" or "central" economic problem, the answer is always **Scarcity**, not poverty, lack of money, or inflation.
- **Inside vs Outside the PPF:** Memorize this: 
  - Points *on* the curve = Efficiency / Full employment.
  - Points *inside* the curve = Inefficiency / Unemployment.
  - Points *outside* the curve = Unattainable (given current resources).
- **Outward Shift:** An outward shift of the entire PPF represents **economic growth** (caused by technological progress or increased resources), not merely an increase in demand.
- **Lionel Robbins:** Commit Lionel Robbins' definition to memory. Key words to look for in options: "ends", "scarce means", "alternative uses".
- **Calculating from a Schedule:** When calculating marginal opportunity cost from a table, look at the change (difference) in the alternative good's quantity, not the total quantity itself. 

## Quick summary

1. Economics is a social science that uses scientific methods to study how humans allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants. Lionel Robbins' definition is the most accepted.
2. The fundamental concepts of economics are wants (unlimited desires), scarcity (limited resources), choice (selecting alternatives), and opportunity cost (the forgone alternative).
3. A scale of preference helps consumers rank their wants rationally to maximize satisfaction.
4. Every society faces fundamental economic problems: What to produce, How to produce, For whom to produce, and Efficiency of resource use.
5. "How to produce" refers to the choice of technique (labour-intensive vs capital-intensive). "For whom" deals with the distribution of wealth.
6. The Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) is a curve showing combinations of two goods that can be produced if resources are fully and efficiently utilized.
7. The PPF illustrates scarcity (unattainable points outside), choice (points on the curve), opportunity cost (downward slope), and economic growth (outward shift). 

## Practice questions

**1. Lionel Robbins defined economics as a science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between:**
- A. wealth accumulation and national development
- B. the ordinary business of life and human welfare
- C. ends and scarce means which have alternative uses
- D. production, distribution, and consumption
**Answer: C.** Robbins' definition focuses on "ends" (wants) and "scarce means" (scarcity) having alternative uses (choice). Option A relates to Adam Smith, and B relates to Alfred Marshall.

**2. The fundamental economic problem that necessitates the study of economics is:**
- A. inflation
- B. unemployment
- C. overpopulation
- D. scarcity
**Answer: D.** Scarcity is the fundamental economic problem. Because resources are limited relative to human wants, individuals and societies are forced to make choices.

**3. A student has ₦8,000 and needs to buy either a pair of shoes (₦8,000) or a textbook (₦8,000). If he chooses to buy the shoes, what is the opportunity cost?**
- A. ₦8,000
- B. the textbook
- C. the shoes
- D. zero, because he bought what he wanted
**Answer: B.** The opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone, which is the textbook. Option A is the money cost, which is a common mistake.

**4. The economic problem of "how to produce" primarily refers to:**
- A. deciding which goods the consumers want the most
- B. choosing the appropriate technique of production
- C. determining who will consume the final products
- D. ensuring resources are allocated fairly
**Answer: B.** "How to produce" deals with the choice of production technique, such as deciding whether to use labour-intensive or capital-intensive methods. 

**5. On a Production Possibility Frontier (PPF), a point located entirely inside the curve indicates:**
- A. economic growth and technological advancement
- B. unattainable levels of production
- C. full and efficient employment of all resources
- D. inefficiency or unemployment of resources
**Answer: D.** A point inside the PPF means the economy is not utilizing all its resources efficiently (idle factories, unemployed labour). Option B represents a point outside the curve, and Option C represents points exactly on the curve.

**6. The primary purpose of drawing a scale of preference is to:**
- A. calculate the total money cost of goods
- B. help consumers make rational choices based on the urgency of their wants
- C. increase the supply of scarce resources
- D. eliminate the need to make a choice
**Answer: B.** A scale of preference ranks wants in descending order of importance, guiding the rational consumer to satisfy the most pressing needs first within their limited budget.

**7. An outward rightward shift of the entire Production Possibility Frontier can be caused by:**
- A. a high level of unemployment
- B. a decrease in the demand for goods
- C. technological improvements or the discovery of new resources
- D. a reallocation of resources from one good to another
**Answer: C.** An outward shift represents economic growth, which occurs when an economy acquires more resources or invents better technology, increasing its productive capacity. Mistake: Option A causes production to fall inside the curve; it does not shift the curve inward or outward.

**8. In economics, the assumption of rationality implies that:**
- A. producers always lower prices to help consumers
- B. consumers always buy the most expensive goods
- C. decision-makers act to maximize their own satisfaction or profit
- D. human wants are fully satisfiable
**Answer: C.** A rational economic agent aims to maximize their benefit (utility for consumers, profit for producers).

**9. A country is producing 100 cars and 50 tractors. If it decides to reallocate its resources to produce 120 cars and 40 tractors, what is the opportunity cost of producing the additional 20 cars?**
- A. 10 tractors
- B. 40 tractors
- C. 50 tractors
- D. 120 cars
**Answer: A.** The opportunity cost is the amount of the other good that must be given up. Tractor production fell from 50 to 40. The difference (50 - 40) is 10 tractors.

**10. Which of the following is NOT considered a fundamental economic problem?**
- A. What to produce
- B. How to produce
- C. For whom to produce
- D. Where to store produced goods
**Answer: D.** "Where to store produced goods" is a logistical issue, not one of the fundamental societal economic problems (What, How, For whom, and Efficiency).
