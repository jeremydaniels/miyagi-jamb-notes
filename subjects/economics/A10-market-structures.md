## Introduction to Market Structures

In Economics, a **market** is not necessarily a physical place like Mile 12 in Lagos or Onitsha Main Market. A market is any arrangement or mechanism that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange goods or services at an agreed price. 

**Market structure** refers to the characteristics of a market that determine the behaviour of firms within it, especially how prices and output are set. Economists classify market structures based on the number of buyers and sellers, the nature of the product, barriers to entry, and the degree of control over the price. The two main categories are **Perfect Competition** and **Imperfect Markets**.

---

## The Perfectly Competitive Market

**Objective i: analyse the assumptions and characteristics of a perfectly competitive market.**

Perfect competition is an ideal (theoretical) market structure. While no market in the real world is 100% perfectly competitive, the markets for standard agricultural products (like yams, garri, or rice in a large open market) come very close.

### Assumptions and characteristics of a perfectly competitive market

For a market to be considered perfectly competitive, it must possess the following characteristics:

1. **Large number of buyers and sellers:** There are so many buyers and sellers that no single individual can influence the market price by changing their demand or supply. Therefore, every firm in perfect competition is a **price taker**.
2. **Homogeneous (identical) products:** All firms produce goods that are exactly the same in quality, size, and appearance. A buyer has no preference for the garri from Seller A over the garri from Seller B. There is no branding or packaging to differentiate them.
3. **Free entry and exit:** There are no barriers (legal, financial, or technical) preventing new firms from entering the industry if it is profitable, or preventing existing firms from leaving if they are making losses.
4. **Perfect knowledge of market conditions:** All buyers and sellers have complete information about prices, costs, and availability. A seller cannot secretly charge a higher price because buyers will immediately know and go elsewhere.
5. **Perfect mobility of factors of production:** Resources like labour and capital can move freely and instantly from one firm or industry to another without any cost or hindrance.
6. **No transport costs:** It is assumed that there are no transport costs, ensuring that the price of the good remains exactly the same everywhere in the market.

## Short-run and long-run equilibrium of a perfect competitor

**Objective ii: differentiate between short-run and long-run equilibrium of a perfectly competitive firm.**

Because the firm is a price taker, it must accept the price determined by the interaction of the whole **industry** (the total market supply and demand). 

If the market price is ₦8, the firm can sell 1 unit, 10 units, or 100 units at ₦8 each. Therefore, its Price (P) is equal to its Average Revenue (AR) and its Marginal Revenue (MR). The firm's demand curve is a perfectly horizontal (perfectly elastic) line.
$$P = AR = MR$$

### The profit maximization rule
For any firm to maximize profit (or minimize loss), it must produce at the output level where **Marginal Revenue equals Marginal Cost**.
$$MR = MC$$

There are two conditions for equilibrium:
1. **Necessary condition:** $MR = MC$.
2. **Sufficient condition:** The MC curve must cut the MR curve from below. This means Marginal Cost must be rising.

Here is a schedule showing a perfectly competitive firm selling at ₦8:

| Output (Q) | Price (P) | Total Revenue (TR) | Marginal Revenue (MR) | Total Cost (TC) | Marginal Cost (MC) | Profit (TR - TC) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | ₦8 | ₦0 | - | ₦10 | - | -₦10 |
| 1 | ₦8 | ₦8 | ₦8 | ₦15 | ₦5 | -₦7 |
| 2 | ₦8 | ₦16 | ₦8 | ₦18 | ₦3 | -₦2 |
| 3 | ₦8 | ₦24 | ₦8 | ₦20 | ₦2 | ₦4 |
| 4 | ₦8 | ₦32 | ₦8 | ₦24 | ₦4 | ₦8 |
| 5 | ₦8 | ₦40 | ₦8 | ₦30 | ₦6 | ₦10 |
| 6 | ₦8 | ₦48 | ₦8 | ₦38 | ₦8 | **₦10** |
| 7 | ₦8 | ₦56 | ₦8 | ₦48 | ₦10 | ₦8 |

At an output of **6 units**, the firm's $MR$ is ₦8 and its $MC$ is ₦8 ($MR = MC$). The $MC$ is rising (from 6 to 8). This is the equilibrium output where profit is maximized at ₦10.

### Short-run equilibrium
In the short run, a perfectly competitive firm can experience one of three situations, depending on where its Average Total Cost (ATC) curve lies relative to the price:

1. **Supernormal profit (Abnormal or Economic profit):** This occurs if the market price is higher than the firm's average total cost at the equilibrium output ($P > ATC$).
2. **Normal profit (Zero economic profit):** This occurs if the market price is exactly equal to the firm's average total cost at the equilibrium output ($P = ATC$). The firm's total revenue exactly covers all its explicit and implicit costs.
3. **Subnormal profit (Economic loss):** This occurs if the market price is lower than the firm's average total cost at the equilibrium output ($P < ATC$).

Graph 2 (Normal profit): ATC curve is tangent to the MR line at the intersection. Graph 3 (Loss): ATC curve is above the MR line at the intersection. A shaded rectangle above the MR line shows loss. ### Long-run equilibrium
In the long run, all factors of production are variable, and firms can freely enter or exit the industry.
* If existing firms are making **supernormal profits**, new firms will be attracted into the industry. This increases the total market supply (shifting the industry supply curve to the right), which forces the market price down until supernormal profits are wiped out.
* If existing firms are making **losses**, some firms will shut down and leave the industry. This decreases market supply (shifting the industry supply curve to the left), which pushes the market price up until losses are eliminated.

Therefore, in the long run, a perfectly competitive firm can only make **normal profit**. Long-run equilibrium occurs at the lowest point of the Long-Run Average Cost (LAC) curve.
$$P = AR = MR = LMC = LAC \text{ (at minimum LAC)}$$

[IMAGE: A graph showing the long-run equilibrium of a perfectly competitive firm. A horizontal P=AR=MR line. A U-shaped Long-Run Average Cost (LAC) curve rests its lowest point exactly on the AR line. A Long-Run Marginal Cost (LMC) curve cuts through this lowest tangent point from below. Point of intersection is the long-run equilibrium point. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

## Imperfect Markets: Monopoly and Monopolistic Competition

An imperfect market is one where one or more of the assumptions of perfect competition are absent. The firms in these markets have some degree of control over the prices they charge (they are price makers). The syllabus focuses on Pure Monopoly, Discriminatory Monopoly, and Monopolistic Competition.

### Pure monopoly, discriminatory monopoly and monopolistic competition

#### 1. Pure Monopoly
A pure monopoly exists when there is only **one producer or seller** of a product that has no close substitutes. Because the monopolist is the only seller, the firm *is* the industry. A classic Nigerian example is the Nigerian Railway Corporation (NRC), which has historically been the sole provider of rail transport services.

#### 2. Discriminatory Monopoly
This is a monopoly that practices **price discrimination**, which means selling the exact same product or service to different consumers at different prices, not due to differences in cost, but based on their willingness or ability to pay. 
For price discrimination to be possible, the following conditions must be met:
* The firm must have monopoly power (ability to set prices).
* The market must be easily divided into different segments (e.g., adult vs child, business class vs economy class).
* There must be different price elasticities of demand in each segment.
* There must be no possibility of "resale" or arbitrage (a consumer buying in the cheap market and selling in the expensive market). 

#### 3. Monopolistic Competition
This market structure combines elements of both monopoly and perfect competition. It is very common in the real world. Think of the market for toilet soaps (Lux, Joy, Dettol, Premier) or fast food (Mr Bigg's, Chicken Republic). 

### Assumptions and characteristics of imperfect markets

**Objective iii: analyse the assumptions and characteristics of imperfect markets.**

**Characteristics of a Pure Monopoly:**
* **Single seller:** One firm controls the entire market supply.
* **No close substitutes:** The consumer has no alternative product to switch to.
* **Barriers to entry:** Strong obstacles prevent new firms from entering the market. These can be legal (patents, copyrights, government licenses), natural (control of a scarce resource, like a single water spring), or economic (massive capital required).
* **Price maker:** The monopolist can set the price of the product OR the quantity to be sold, but not both at the same time. If they set a high price, consumers will buy less.

**Characteristics of Monopolistic Competition:**
* **Large number of buyers and sellers:** Like perfect competition, there are many firms, but each has a small share of the market.
* **Differentiated products:** Products are similar but not identical. They are differentiated by branding, packaging, scent, or quality. Because consumers develop brand loyalty, each firm acts like a "mini-monopoly" over its own brand.
* **Free entry and exit:** New firms can easily enter the market if there are profits to be made.
* **Non-price competition:** Firms compete heavily through advertising, promotions, and customer service rather than just lowering prices.

## Short-run and long-run equilibria of imperfectly competitive firms

**Objective iv: differentiate between the short-run and long-run equilibria of imperfectly competitive firms.**

Unlike a perfect competitor, an imperfectly competitive firm faces a **downward-sloping demand curve (Average Revenue curve)**. To sell more units, the firm must lower its price. Because it lowers the price for all units sold, its **Marginal Revenue (MR) is always lower than its Average Revenue (AR)**.

Here is a schedule for a Monopolist maximizing profit:

| Output (Q) | Price/AR (P) | Total Revenue (TR) | Marginal Revenue (MR) | Total Cost (TC) | Marginal Cost (MC) | Profit (TR - TC) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | ₦17 | ₦0 | - | ₦10 | - | -₦10 |
| 1 | ₦16 | ₦16 | ₦16 | ₦16 | ₦6 | ₦0 |
| 2 | ₦15 | ₦30 | ₦14 | ₦20 | ₦4 | ₦10 |
| 3 | ₦14 | ₦42 | ₦12 | ₦24 | ₦4 | ₦18 |
| 4 | ₦13 | ₦52 | ₦10 | ₦30 | ₦6 | ₦22 |
| 5 | ₦12 | ₦60 | ₦8 | ₦38 | ₦8 | **₦22** |
| 6 | ₦11 | ₦66 | ₦6 | ₦48 | ₦10 | ₦18 |

At **5 units**, $MR = MC$ (both are ₦8). The firm sets the price at ₦12 (read from the AR column) and makes a maximum profit of ₦22.

### Equilibrium of a Monopoly
* **Short-run equilibrium:** The monopolist produces where $MR = MC$. The price is read off the AR curve at that quantity. Usually, a monopolist makes **supernormal profit**, though in rare cases it could make normal profit or even a loss if costs are too high.
* **Long-run equilibrium:** Because there are **barriers to entry**, new firms cannot enter to compete. Therefore, the monopolist continues to earn **supernormal profit in the long run**.

A vertical line goes up from Q to the AR curve to determine price P. An ATC curve sits below the price point. A shaded rectangle indicates supernormal profit. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### Equilibrium of Monopolistic Competition
* **Short-run equilibrium:** Similar to a monopoly, the firm produces where $MR = MC$ and faces a downward-sloping AR curve. It can make supernormal profit, normal profit, or a loss in the short run.
* **Long-run equilibrium:** Unlike a monopoly, there is **free entry**. If firms are making supernormal profit, new brands will enter the market (e.g., more fast-food chains open). This takes customers away from existing firms, shifting their demand (AR) curves to the left until all supernormal profits are wiped out. 
In the long run, a monopolistically competitive firm only makes **normal profit**. Its AR curve becomes exactly tangent to its Long-Run Average Cost (LAC) curve.

[IMAGE: Long-run equilibrium of a firm under monopolistic competition. Downward sloping AR and MR curves. A U-shaped LAC curve is exactly tangent to the downward-sloping AR curve at the equilibrium price and quantity. LMC cuts MR exactly below the tangency point. No shaded profit area (normal profit only). Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### Summary Comparison Table

| Feature | Perfect Competition | Monopolistic Competition | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of sellers** | Very many | Many | One |
| **Type of product** | Homogeneous | Differentiated | Unique (no close substitutes) |
| **Control over price** | None (Price taker) | Some control | High (Price maker) |
| **Demand curve (AR)** | Perfectly horizontal | Downward sloping (elastic) | Downward sloping (relatively inelastic) |
| **Barriers to entry** | None | None | High |
| **Long-run profit** | Normal profit only | Normal profit only | Supernormal profit |

## Break-even and Shut-down Analysis in Various Markets

**Objective v: establish the conditions for the break-even/shut down of firms.**

Whether a firm is in perfect competition or a monopoly, it uses its cost curves to decide whether it is safe to keep producing. Recall that Total Cost ($TC$) is made up of Total Fixed Cost ($TFC$) and Total Variable Cost ($TVC$). 
* **Fixed costs** (like rent or a generator loan) must be paid even if output is zero.
* **Variable costs** (like raw materials or daily wages) only occur when production happens.

Dividing by quantity gives: $ATC = AFC + AVC$.

### The Break-even Point
The break-even point is the level of output where total revenue exactly equals total cost ($TR = TC$), meaning the firm is making exactly zero economic profit (which is **normal profit**).
* **Condition:** $Price (AR) = ATC$
* At this point, the firm is doing just well enough to cover all explicit costs and the entrepreneur's opportunity cost. It will comfortably remain in business.

### The Shut-down Point
What happens if the price falls below the ATC, causing the firm to make a loss? Should it close down immediately? Not necessarily in the short run.

As long as the price is high enough to cover the **Average Variable Cost (AVC)**, the firm should continue producing. Any extra money earned above the variable cost goes towards paying off the fixed costs. 

However, if the price falls so low that it cannot even cover the day-to-day running costs (the variable costs), the firm must stop production to minimize its losses. If it shuts down, its loss will be exactly equal to its Total Fixed Cost.
* **Condition for shut down:** $Price (AR) \leq AVC$

**Example scenario (A Nigerian Pure Water Factory):**
Suppose a factory's Average Total Cost ($ATC$) to produce a bag of pure water is ₦50. This is made up of ₦20 average fixed cost per bag (factory rent shared over the bags produced) and ₦30 average variable cost per bag (nylon, water treatment, daily labour).
* If the market price is **₦50**, it is at the **break-even point**.
* If the price falls to **₦40**, the factory makes a loss of ₦10 per bag. However, it should **continue producing in the short run** because the ₦40 covers the ₦30 variable cost, and leaves ₦10 per bag to help pay the fixed costs. If they close, they still have to pay all their fixed costs (the rent) from their pocket.
* If the price falls to **₦25**, it does not even cover the ₦30 variable cost. For every bag produced, they are losing an extra ₦5 per bag on top of their fixed costs. At this price, they must **shut down**.

A horizontal line drawn at the minimum of ATC is labelled "Break-even point". A horizontal line drawn at the minimum of AVC is labelled "Shut-down point". ## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Market structure | The characteristics of a market that determine firm behaviour, pricing, and output |
| Price taker | A firm that has no influence over market price and must accept the industry price |
| Price maker | A firm that has the power to set the price of its product |
| Homogeneous product | Goods that are exactly identical, with no branding or differences |
| Differentiated product | Goods that are similar but made distinct by branding, packaging, or quality |
| Normal profit | The minimum profit required to keep a firm in business; zero economic profit ($TR = TC$) |
| Supernormal profit | Profit earned above normal profit; also called abnormal or economic profit ($TR > TC$) |
| Price discrimination | Charging different prices to different consumers for the exact same product |
| Break-even point | The output level where Total Revenue equals Total Cost ($P = ATC$) |
| Shut-down point | The price level at which a firm cannot cover its variable costs and must stop production ($P = AVC$) |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Mixing up necessary and sufficient conditions:** To maximize profit, it is not enough for $MR = MC$. The MC curve must also cut the MR curve from *below*.
* **Assuming monopolies can set both price and quantity:** A monopolist can set a high price, but they cannot force consumers to buy a large quantity at that price. They set one, and the demand curve dictates the other.
* **Firm vs Industry:** In perfect competition, the *industry* demand curve slopes downward, but the *individual firm's* demand curve is perfectly horizontal.
* **The Long-Run Monopoly myth:** Many students think a monopolist *must* make supernormal profit. While they usually do because of entry barriers, if their costs are too high or demand too low, they could make normal profit.
* **Shutting down at a loss:** A common mistake is thinking a firm must shut down as soon as it makes a loss ($P < ATC$). It only shuts down if the price falls below Average Variable Cost ($P < AVC$).

## Quick summary

1. A perfectly competitive market features many buyers/sellers, homogeneous products, free entry/exit, perfect knowledge, and no transport costs. The firm is a price taker ($P = AR = MR$).
2. To maximize profit, every firm (regardless of market structure) must produce at the output where Marginal Revenue equals Marginal Cost ($MR = MC$), with MC rising.
3. In the short run, a perfect competitor can make supernormal profit, normal profit, or a loss. In the long run, free entry and exit ensure it makes only normal profit at the lowest point of the LAC curve.
4. A pure monopoly has one seller, no close substitutes, and high entry barriers. The firm is a price maker and can maintain supernormal profits in the long run.
5. Discriminatory monopoly involves selling the same good at different prices to different markets, which requires market segmentation and differing elasticities.
6. Monopolistic competition features many sellers with differentiated products and free entry. Firms earn normal profit in the long run due to the entry of new competitors.
7. The break-even point occurs when Price equals Average Total Cost ($P = ATC$), meaning the firm makes exactly zero economic profit (normal profit).
8. The shut-down point occurs when Price falls to or below Average Variable Cost ($P \leq AVC$), as the firm cannot even cover its daily running costs.

## Practice questions

**1. A market structure characterised by a large number of buyers and sellers of a homogeneous product is**
- A. an oligopoly.
- B. a pure monopoly.
- C. perfect competition.
- D. monopolistic competition.

**Answer: C.** Perfect competition requires many buyers and sellers trading an identical (homogeneous) product, meaning no single participant can influence the market price.

**2. Which of the following is the sufficient condition for profit maximization?**
- A. Marginal revenue is greater than marginal cost.
- B. Marginal cost curve cuts the marginal revenue curve from above.
- C. Average revenue equals average cost.
- D. Marginal cost curve cuts the marginal revenue curve from below.

**Answer: D.** While $MR = MC$ is the necessary condition, the sufficient condition to ensure it is the maximum profit (and not minimum) is that MC must be rising, thereby cutting MR from below. Option B represents a point of minimum profit/maximum loss.

**3. In the long run, a firm in a perfectly competitive market earns**
- A. supernormal profit.
- B. normal profit only.
- C. economic loss.
- D. monopoly profit.

**Answer: B.** Because there is free entry and exit, any supernormal profit made in the short run attracts new firms, which increases supply and drives the price down until only normal profit ($P = ATC$) is made.

**4. A monopolist can continue to earn supernormal profit in the long run primarily because**
- A. of the absence of barriers to entry.
- B. it faces a perfectly elastic demand curve.
- C. there are strict barriers preventing new firms from entering.
- D. it produces a homogeneous product.

**Answer: C.** High barriers to entry (like patents, high capital costs, or government licenses) prevent competing firms from entering the market to share the profits, allowing the monopoly to maintain its supernormal profit indefinitely.

**5. A major feature that distinguishes monopolistic competition from perfect competition is**
- A. a large number of buyers and sellers.
- B. product differentiation.
- C. free entry and exit.
- D. the presence of a single seller.

**Answer: B.** While both markets have many buyers/sellers and free entry/exit, products in perfect competition are identical (homogeneous), whereas products in monopolistic competition are similar but distinct (differentiated by brand or quality).

**6. Price discrimination is most likely to be successful if**
- A. the firm is a price taker.
- B. consumers can easily buy in the cheap market and resell in the expensive market.
- C. the product is homogeneous and sold in a single location.
- D. the market can be segmented and elasticities of demand differ.

**Answer: D.** A discriminatory monopolist must be able to separate consumers into groups with different willingness to pay (elasticities) and prevent them from reselling the product to each other. Option B is a common misunderstanding; if arbitrage (resale) is possible, price discrimination fails.

**7. A firm's average total cost is ₦60, its average variable cost is ₦45, and the market price for its product is ₦50. In the short run, the firm should**
- A. shut down immediately to avoid further losses.
- B. continue producing because the price covers its average variable cost.
- C. continue producing because it is making a supernormal profit.
- D. raise its price to ₦60 to break even.

**Answer: B.** Since the price (₦50) is greater than the average variable cost (₦45) but less than the average total cost (₦60), the firm is making a loss. However, it should continue producing in the short run because the ₦50 price covers all variable costs and contributes ₦5 towards paying off the fixed costs. Shutting down (Option A) would force the firm to pay all fixed costs out of pocket with zero revenue.

**8. For a perfectly competitive firm, the demand curve is**
- A. perfectly inelastic.
- B. perfectly elastic.
- C. downward sloping.
- D. upward sloping.

**Answer: B.** A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker. It can sell any quantity at the prevailing market price, so its demand curve ($P = AR = MR$) is perfectly horizontal (perfectly elastic). 

**9. The break-even point for a firm occurs where**
- A. Total Revenue is at its maximum.
- B. Price equals Average Variable Cost.
- C. Total Revenue equals Total Cost.
- D. Marginal Revenue equals Average Revenue.

**Answer: C.** The break-even point is where the firm makes exactly zero economic profit (normal profit), which happens when Total Revenue equals Total Cost ($TR = TC$), or equivalently, Price equals Average Total Cost ($P = ATC$).

**10. In a long-run equilibrium under monopolistic competition, the firm's demand curve (AR) is**
- A. perfectly horizontal and equal to MC.
- B. tangent to the lowest point of the Long-Run Average Cost curve.
- C. tangent to the downward-sloping section of the Long-Run Average Cost curve.
- D. above the Long-Run Average Cost curve.

**Answer: C.** In the long run, free entry wipes out supernormal profits, pushing the AR curve to the left until it just touches (is tangent to) the LAC curve. Because AR is downward sloping, the tangency must occur on the downward-sloping part of the U-shaped LAC curve, not at its lowest point (which is the case for perfect competition).
