## Meaning and scope: Economic Growth vs. Development

**Content a: Meaning and scope**
**Objective i: distinguish between economic growth and development**

In everyday English, "growth" and "development" mean almost the same thing. But in Economics, they are two very different concepts. JAMB frequently tests your ability to tell them apart.

### What is Economic Growth?
**Economic growth** is a sustained increase in the volume of goods and services produced in a country over a specific period (usually a year). It is a purely **quantitative** change. 

We measure economic growth by looking at the **Real Gross Domestic Product (Real GDP)** or the **National Income**. If Nigeria produces more bags of rice, barrels of oil, and banking services this year than it did last year, the economy has grown. 

### What is Economic Development?
**Economic development** is economic growth accompanied by positive, **qualitative** changes in the standard of living of the people and the structure of the economy. 

For a country to be developing, not only must the national income increase, but poverty, unemployment, and inequality must decrease. The citizens must have better access to healthcare, education, clean water, and infrastructure. Furthermore, the economy's structure usually shifts from relying mostly on agriculture (primary sector) to manufacturing and services (secondary and tertiary sectors).

### "Growth without Development"
A country can experience economic growth without economic development. For example, if a country's oil revenue doubles because world oil prices go up, its GDP will increase (economic growth). However, if that money is stolen by corrupt officials or only benefits a few rich people, while the masses remain jobless, hungry, and without good roads or hospitals, there is **no economic development**. 

*Economic growth is necessary for economic development, but economic growth alone is not sufficient to guarantee economic development.*

### Comparison: Economic Growth vs. Economic Development

| Feature | Economic Growth | Economic Development |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | An increase in the real national income or output over time. | Economic growth plus improvements in living standards and structural changes. |
| **Nature of change** | **Quantitative** (deals with numbers and volumes). | **Qualitative** and quantitative (deals with the quality of life). |
| **Scope** | Narrow concept. | Broad concept (it includes economic growth). |
| **Indicators** | Real GDP, GNP, and Per Capita Income. | Human Development Index (HDI), life expectancy, literacy rates, poverty levels. |
| **Focus** | Focuses on increased production of goods and services. | Focuses on poverty reduction, wealth distribution, and social welfare. |
| **Occurrence** | Can happen in a short time (e.g., due to a boom in oil prices). | Is a long-term, gradual process. |

## Indicators of growth and development

**Content b: Indicators of growth and development**
**Objective ii: highlight the indicators of growth and development**

How do economists know if a country is growing or developing? They use specific tools called **indicators**.

### Indicators of Economic Growth

1. **Real Gross Domestic Product (Real GDP):** The total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year, adjusted for inflation. If Real GDP rises, there is growth.
2. **Real Gross National Product (Real GNP):** The total value of goods and services produced by the citizens of a country, regardless of where they live, adjusted for inflation.
3. **Per Capita Income:** This is the national income divided by the total population. 
   Formula: $\text{Per Capita Income} = \frac{\text{National Income}}{\text{Total Population}}$
   If the national income is growing faster than the population, per capita income rises, indicating economic growth.

### Indicators of Economic Development

Because development is about human welfare, its indicators measure the quality of life.

1. **Human Development Index (HDI):** Created by the United Nations, this is the most widely used indicator of economic development. It combines three things into one score (from 0 to 1):
   * **Health:** Measured by life expectancy at birth.
   * **Education:** Measured by adult literacy rates and years of schooling.
   * **Standard of Living:** Measured by real Per Capita Income.
   * *A country with an HDI close to 1 (like Norway) is highly developed, while a country with a low HDI is less developed.*
2. **Life Expectancy:** The average number of years a newborn is expected to live. High life expectancy indicates a developed healthcare system, good nutrition, and sanitation.
3. **Literacy Rate:** The percentage of the population aged 15 and above who can read and write.
4. **Poverty and Inequality Indices:** A reduction in the percentage of people living below the poverty line, and a shrinking gap between the rich and the poor, are strong signs of development.
5. **Infant Mortality Rate:** The number of babies out of every 1000 born who die before their first birthday. A falling infant mortality rate indicates development.
6. **Structural Transformation:** The shift of the workforce from subsistence agriculture to modern manufacturing and service industries.

## Factors affecting growth and development

**Content c: Factors affecting growth and development**
**Objective iii: identify the factors affecting growth and development**

The speed at which a country grows and develops depends on several economic and non-economic factors.

### 1. Natural Resources (Land)
A country richly endowed with fertile soil, minerals, forests, and water bodies has a foundation for growth. For example, Nigeria's crude oil, natural gas, tin, and fertile agricultural land provide raw materials and export revenue. However, resources alone do not guarantee development if they are mismanaged.

### 2. Capital Accumulation
Capital includes machinery, factories, roads, and tools used to produce other goods. Capital accumulation means increasing the stock of these physical assets. A country that saves and invests in building modern factories, power plants, and railways will grow faster than one that consumes all its income.

### 3. Human Resources (Human Capital)
The quality and quantity of a country's labour force are crucial. A healthy, educated, and skilled workforce is highly productive. If a country invests in schools, universities, and hospitals, it improves its human capital. Conversely, the "brain drain" (where skilled doctors and engineers leave Nigeria for the US or UK) slows down development.

### 4. Technological Progress
Technology refers to new and better ways of doing things. Improvements in technology lead to higher efficiency, meaning more output can be produced from the same amount of inputs. Examples include the use of tractors instead of hoes in agriculture, or using software to speed up banking.

### 5. Entrepreneurship
Entrepreneurs are the risk-takers who combine land, labour, and capital to create businesses. A country with innovative and active entrepreneurs (like those in Aba, Nnewi, or Lagos tech hubs) will experience faster economic expansion.

### 6. Political Stability and Governance
Economic development requires long-term planning. Wars, riots, terrorism, and frequent changes in government scare away foreign and local investors. Good governance, rule of law, and protection of property rights encourage businesses to invest.

### 7. Social and Cultural Factors
Certain cultural attitudes can either promote or hinder development. A culture that values hard work, saving, and education promotes development. Conversely, customs that restrict women from working or acquiring property, or cultural resistance to modern medicine and technology, can delay development.

## Problems of development in Nigeria

**Content d: Problems of development in Nigeria**
**Objective iv: examine the problems of development in Nigeria**

Despite vast natural and human resources, Nigeria remains a developing country. Several severe bottlenecks hinder its economic development.

1. **Mono-Product Economy (Over-reliance on Oil):** 
   Nigeria relies heavily on crude oil exports for government revenue and foreign exchange. This makes the economy highly vulnerable. When global oil prices crash, the Nigerian economy suffers heavily, leading to budget deficits and currency devaluation.
   
2. **Corruption and Mismanagement of Funds:**
   Large sums of public money meant for building roads, schools, and hospitals are often diverted or stolen by corrupt officials. This starves the economy of the capital needed for development.

3. **Inadequate Infrastructure:**
   Economic activities rely on good infrastructure. Nigeria suffers from erratic power supply (electricity), dilapidated road networks, and an inefficient railway system. Poor infrastructure increases the cost of doing business, forcing many factories to close down or relocate to other countries.

4. **High Population Growth Rate:**
   Nigeria’s population grows very fast (over 2.5% per year). When the population grows faster than the economy (GDP), the per capita income drops. The government struggles to provide enough schools, hospitals, and jobs for the rapidly increasing population, leading to high unemployment and poverty.

5. **Heavy Debt Burden:**
   Nigeria spends a huge percentage of its national revenue servicing (paying interest on) domestic and external debts. Money that should have been used for development projects is used to pay off debts.

6. **Insecurity:**
   Issues such as the Boko Haram insurgency in the North-East, banditry, and farmer-herder clashes have severely disrupted agricultural production and displaced millions of people. This leads to food insecurity and discourages foreign investment.

7. **Low Level of Technology and Weak Manufacturing Base:**
   Nigeria relies mostly on imported manufactured goods and exports raw, unrefined materials (like crude oil and cocoa). Because the manufacturing sector is weak, the country loses out on the jobs and extra value that come from processing goods locally.

## Development planning in Nigeria

**Content e: Development planning in Nigeria**
**Objective v: examine the role of planning in development**

### What is Development Planning?
**Development planning** is the conscious and deliberate effort by the government to manage and direct the resources of a country towards achieving specific economic and social goals over a given period of time.

Plans are typically categorised by time:
* **Short-term plans:** Usually 1 to 2 years (e.g., the annual government budget).
* **Medium-term plans:** Usually 3 to 5 years (e.g., National Development Plans).
* **Long-term (Perspective) plans:** Usually 10 to 25 years, setting a broad vision for the future (e.g., Vision 20:2020).

### The Role and Importance of Planning in Development
Why does a country like Nigeria need to plan?
1. **Efficient Allocation of Resources:** Resources (money, land, skilled labour) are scarce. Planning ensures that these scarce resources are directed to the most important sectors (like education, agriculture, and infrastructure) rather than being wasted.
2. **Economic Diversification:** Planning helps a country design policies to stop relying on just one sector (like oil) and boost other sectors like agriculture, solid minerals, and manufacturing.
3. **Reduction of Unemployment and Poverty:** A good plan sets targets for job creation and outlines specific projects (like building railways or setting up tech hubs) that will employ the youth.
4. **Attracting Foreign Aid and Investment:** International organizations (like the World Bank) and foreign investors are more willing to lend money or invest in a country that has a clear, well-documented economic plan.
5. **Measuring Progress:** A plan provides a benchmark. At the end of the plan period, the government can compare actual achievements with the set targets to know if the economy is succeeding or failing.

### Examples of Development Plans in Nigeria
*You do not need to memorise all the dates, but you should recognise these names as examples of Nigerian development plans.*
* **Pre-Independence:** The Ten-Year Plan of Development and Welfare (1946).
* **Post-Independence National Development Plans:** Nigeria had four of these (1st: 1962–1968, 2nd: 1970–1974, 3rd: 1975–1980, 4th: 1981–1985).
* **Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP):** Introduced in 1986 to restructure the economy, promote exports, and reduce reliance on imports.
* **NEEDS (National Economic Empowerment and Development Strategy):** Launched in 2004 to reform government institutions, grow the private sector, and implement a social charter for the people.
* **Vision 20:2020:** A long-term plan aiming to make Nigeria one of the top 20 economies in the world by the year 2020.
* **Economic Recovery and Growth Plan (ERGP):** Launched in 2017 to pull the country out of recession and drive diversification.

### Problems of Development Planning in Nigeria
Despite having many well-written plans over the decades, Nigeria has struggled to achieve the goals set within them. The major problems include:

1. **Poor Implementation:** Plans are often well-written on paper but poorly executed in reality due to lack of commitment and administrative bottlenecks.
2. **Lack of Accurate Data:** Effective planning requires knowing the exact population, unemployment rate, and resource availability. Nigeria suffers from poor statistical data, making plans unreliable.
3. **Political Instability and Policy Somersault:** When a new government takes power, it often abandons the development plans of the previous administration and starts its own. This lack of continuity is called policy somersault.
4. **Over-reliance on Oil Revenue:** Most Nigerian plans are budgeted based on expected crude oil earnings. When global oil prices crash, the government runs out of money to fund the plan.
5. **Corruption:** Funds allocated for specific projects in the development plan are often embezzled, leaving projects abandoned (white elephant projects).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Economic Growth** | A quantitative increase in the volume of goods and services produced in a country over time (measured by Real GDP). |
| **Economic Development** | Economic growth accompanied by qualitative improvements in living standards, infrastructure, and poverty reduction. |
| **Real GDP** | The total value of goods and services produced within a country in a year, adjusted for inflation. |
| **Per Capita Income** | The total national income divided by the total population. |
| **Human Development Index (HDI)** | A tool developed by the UN to measure development using life expectancy, education, and per capita income. |
| **Capital Accumulation** | The process of increasing a country's stock of physical assets, like machinery and infrastructure, to boost production. |
| **Mono-economy** | An economy that relies overwhelmingly on a single product (like crude oil in Nigeria) for its foreign exchange and government revenue. |
| **Human Capital** | The economic value of a worker's experience, skills, and education. |
| **Development Planning** | A deliberate government effort to manage resources to achieve specific socio-economic goals over a given timeframe. |
| **Policy Somersault** | The frequent change or abandonment of government policies and plans, usually when a new administration takes over. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Growth vs. Development:** This is the most common JAMB question on this topic. Remember: **Growth** is *quantitative* (numbers, GDP). **Development** is *qualitative* and *quantitative* (standard of living, structural changes).
* **Growth without Development:** Know that economic growth is a necessary condition for economic development, but you can have growth without development (if the wealth is not distributed and poverty remains high).
* **Indicators:** If JAMB asks for the best indicator of economic *growth*, look for **Real GDP** or **Per Capita Income**. If they ask for the best indicator of economic *development*, look for **HDI (Human Development Index)**.
* **Mono-economy:** Nigeria is a classic example of a mono-product economy. When asked what poses the biggest threat to Nigeria's economic stability, over-reliance on crude oil is the standard answer.
* **Planning problems:** Lack of accurate data and political instability (lack of continuity) are frequently tested as the major reasons development plans fail in West Africa.

## Quick summary

1. Economic growth is the quantitative increase in the real national income or output over a given period.
2. Economic development involves economic growth plus qualitative improvements in the citizens' standard of living and structural changes in the economy.
3. Economic growth is measured using Real GDP and Per Capita Income, while economic development is best measured by the Human Development Index (HDI), which includes health, education, and income.
4. Factors that speed up growth and development include natural resource endowment, capital accumulation, human capital development, technological progress, and political stability.
5. Nigeria's economic development is held back by over-reliance on oil (mono-economy), corruption, poor infrastructure, insecurity, and a high population growth rate.
6. Development planning is used by governments to efficiently allocate scarce resources, diversify the economy, and reduce unemployment.
7. Development plans in Nigeria often fail due to lack of accurate statistical data, poor implementation, corruption, and frequent changes in government policy (policy somersault).

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following is the most important distinction between economic growth and economic development?**
- A. Growth involves structural changes, while development involves increased output.
- B. Growth is qualitative, while development is quantitative.
- C. Growth is a quantitative increase in output, while development includes qualitative improvements in living standards.
- D. Growth focuses on poverty reduction, while development focuses on industrialization.

**Answer: C.** Economic growth deals strictly with numbers (increase in output/GDP), while economic development goes further to include improvements in the quality of life, such as better health, education, and reduced poverty. Option B has the concepts reversed.

**2. A country can experience economic growth without economic development if:**
- A. its population is decreasing rapidly.
- B. the increase in national income is concentrated in the hands of a few people.
- C. there is a significant improvement in the healthcare sector.
- D. the agricultural sector contributes less to the GDP.

**Answer: B.** Growth without development happens when the national income increases, but the wealth is unevenly distributed, leaving the majority of citizens in poverty without access to basic amenities. 

**3. Which of the following is the most comprehensive indicator of economic development?**
- A. Real Gross Domestic Product
- B. Per Capita Income
- C. Human Development Index (HDI)
- D. Balance of Payments

**Answer: C.** The Human Development Index (HDI) is the most comprehensive measure because it combines per capita income, education (literacy/schooling), and health (life expectancy) to assess the overall quality of life. Real GDP (Option A) measures growth, not development.

**4. A major factor retarding the economic development of Nigeria is its:**
- A. diverse agricultural base.
- B. over-reliance on a single export commodity.
- C. low population growth rate.
- D. overly advanced technological sector.

**Answer: B.** Nigeria operates a mono-product economy, relying overwhelmingly on crude oil for its revenue. This over-reliance makes the economy highly unstable and retards long-term diverse development.

**5. The conscious effort by a government to direct its resources to achieve specific economic goals over a period is known as:**
- A. economic integration.
- B. development planning.
- C. capital accumulation.
- D. industrialization.

**Answer: B.** This is the exact definition of development planning.

**6. Which of the following best describes "capital accumulation" in an economy?**
- A. The printing of more currency notes by the Central Bank.
- B. An increase in the stock of physical assets like machinery, factories, and infrastructure.
- C. The total amount of foreign exchange reserves a country has.
- D. The taxation of wealthy individuals to redistribute income.

**Answer: B.** Capital in economics refers to man-made goods used to produce other goods. Capital accumulation means investing in and building up this stock of physical productive assets. Option A is a common misunderstanding of the word "capital" in everyday speech.

**7. A common reason why development plans fail in West African countries, including Nigeria, is:**
- A. the availability of too much statistical data.
- B. over-reliance on foreign manufactured goods.
- C. lack of accurate data and political instability.
- D. the use of short-term plans instead of long-term plans.

**Answer: C.** Effective planning requires accurate data on population, resources, and unemployment. Nigeria lacks reliable statistical data. Furthermore, political instability leads to policy somersaults, causing plans to be abandoned.

**8. Which of the following factors is a non-economic factor that affects economic growth and development?**
- A. Technological progress
- B. Capital accumulation
- C. Cultural attitudes and values
- D. Natural resource endowment

**Answer: C.** While technology, capital, and natural resources are strictly economic variables, cultural attitudes (how a society views work, saving, gender roles, or education) are social/non-economic factors that heavily impact the economy.

**9. NEEDS, SAP, and Vision 20:2020 are all examples of:**
- A. international trade agreements.
- B. foreign aid programs from the World Bank.
- C. development plans and economic strategies in Nigeria.
- D. commercial banks in Nigeria.

**Answer: C.** NEEDS (National Economic Empowerment and Development Strategy), SAP (Structural Adjustment Programme), and Vision 20:2020 are all historical economic development plans and reform strategies implemented by the Nigerian government.

**10. When skilled professionals such as doctors and engineers migrate from a developing country to a developed one, it negatively affects economic growth by depleting the country's:**
- A. physical capital.
- B. human capital.
- C. natural resources.
- D. technological capital.

**Answer: B.** Human capital refers to the skills, education, and experience possessed by individuals. The migration of highly skilled workers (often called "brain drain") depletes the home country's human capital, holding back its development.
