## Types and features of agriculture

Agriculture is the foundation of the Nigerian economy. It involves the cultivation of crops, the rearing of animals for human consumption, and the provision of raw materials for industries. It also includes forestry (managing forests for timber and other resources) and fishery (catching and rearing fish). 

### Identifying types and features

In Nigeria, agriculture can be identified and classified in two main ways: by the **scale of production** (subsistence or commercial) and by the **type of produce** (crops, livestock, forestry, or fishery).

**1. Classification by Scale of Production**

The two major types of farming systems in Nigeria based on scale and purpose are **subsistence agriculture** and **commercial agriculture**.

* **Subsistence (Peasant) Agriculture:** This is a system where a farmer grows crops and rears animals primarily to feed themselves and their immediate family. Any small surplus left over may be sold in the local market to buy basic needs, but maximizing profit is not the primary goal. It is the most widely practiced form of agriculture in Nigeria.
* **Commercial Agriculture:** This is a large-scale system where crops are grown and animals are reared primarily for sale and profit. It involves large expanses of land, huge capital investment, hired labour, and modern technology.

**Comparison between Subsistence and Commercial Agriculture**

| Feature | Subsistence Agriculture | Commercial Agriculture |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Purpose** | To feed the farmer and their family. | For sale to maximize profit. |
| **Scale of Production** | Small-scale. | Large-scale. |
| **Land Size** | Small, fragmented plots of land (often 1 to 2 hectares). | Large expanses of land (often hundreds of hectares or estates). |
| **Implements Used** | Crude or traditional tools (e.g., hoes, cutlasses, sickles). | Modern machinery (e.g., tractors, harvesters, ploughs, incubators). |
| **Labour Supply** | Mainly family labour. | Paid or hired skilled and unskilled labour. |
| **Capital Required** | Very little capital is needed. | Huge capital investment is required. |
| **Yield/Output** | Low yield per hectare. | High yield per hectare. |
| **Farming System** | Mixed cropping (growing multiple crops on one piece of land to reduce risk). | Monocropping (specializing in one crop, e.g., a rubber or cocoa plantation). |

**2. Classification by Type of Produce**

Agriculture is also divided into four main sub-sectors:
* **Crop Farming:** The cultivation of plants. This includes **food crops** (grown for human consumption, e.g., yam, cassava, rice, maize, beans) and **cash crops** (grown for sale or export, e.g., cocoa in the West, groundnut and cotton in the North, palm produce and rubber in the East and Mid-West).
* **Livestock Farming:** The rearing of animals for meat, milk, eggs, and hide. Examples include poultry (chickens, turkeys), cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs. Pastoral farming (nomadic herding) is common among the Fulani in northern Nigeria.
* **Forestry:** The management and conservation of forest resources to produce timber (wood for building and furniture), firewood, and medicinal plants. 
* **Fishery:** The catching of fish from natural water bodies (rivers, oceans) and the rearing of fish in artificial ponds (aquaculture, such as catfish farming).

## Role of agriculture in economic development

Agriculture is often described as the backbone of a developing economy. Before the discovery of crude oil in commercial quantities in the 1970s, agriculture was the absolute mainstay of the Nigerian economy. 

### Assessing the role of agriculture in economic development

Agriculture contributes to the economic development of Nigeria in several crucial ways:

1. **Provision of Food:** Agriculture is the only sector that produces food to feed the nation's rapidly growing population. A country that cannot feed its citizens will spend a massive portion of its wealth importing food, which drains national resources and harms economic development.
2. **Employment Generation:** Agriculture is the largest single employer of labour in Nigeria. It provides jobs for about 60-70% of the active labour force, especially in rural areas. This includes farmers, herdsmen, fishermen, marketers, and transporters.
3. **Source of Foreign Exchange:** By exporting cash crops, Nigeria earns foreign exchange (money from other countries) which is used to import essential industrial goods and technology. Historically, Nigeria was a world leader in exporting cocoa, groundnuts, and palm produce.
4. **Provision of Raw Materials for Industries:** Agriculture supplies the basic raw materials needed by agro-allied industries (industries that depend on agriculture). Examples include:
   * **Cotton** for the textile industry.
   * **Cocoa** for the beverage and chocolate industry.
   * **Rubber** for the tyre and plastics industry.
   * **Cassava and maize** for the flour, starch, and biofuel industries.
   * **Hides and skins** for the leather and footwear industry.
5. **Market for Industrial Goods:** The agricultural sector buys products manufactured by the industrial sector. Farmers purchase fertilizers, pesticides, tractors, cutlasses, and sacks. As agriculture develops, farmers' incomes rise, increasing their demand for industrial and consumer goods.
6. **Source of Government Revenue:** The government generates revenue from agriculture through taxes levied on farmers' incomes, export duties on agricultural products, and licenses for forestry and fishing.
7. **Infrastructural Development in Rural Areas:** Government efforts to boost agriculture often lead to the building of farm-to-market roads, rural electrification, and water supply projects, which develop the rural economy.

## Problems of agriculture in Nigeria

Despite its critical importance, the agricultural sector in Nigeria has faced severe neglect and ongoing challenges that hinder its growth and transformation into a modern sector.

### Examining the characteristics and problems

To understand the problems, we must first look at the defining **characteristics** of Nigerian agriculture, which are closely tied to its challenges:
* **Predominantly Subsistence:** The vast majority of Nigerian farmers produce mainly for family consumption.
* **Use of Crude Implements:** Most farming operations are done manually using simple tools.
* **Dependence on Rainfall:** Agriculture is largely rain-fed with very little irrigation, tying farming activities strictly to the wet and dry seasons.
* **Small Landholdings:** Land ownership is heavily fragmented.
* **Aging Farming Population:** The youth mostly migrate to cities, leaving farming to older men and women.

Building on these characteristics, the major **problems** of agriculture in Nigeria include:

1. **Inadequate Finance and Credit Facilities:** Farmers lack the capital needed to expand their farms or buy modern equipment. Commercial banks are often reluctant to lend to rural farmers because agriculture is considered high-risk, and peasant farmers lack acceptable collateral.
2. **Poor Infrastructure:** Rural farming communities lack basic amenities such as good road networks, electricity, and water. Bad farm-to-market roads mean that a lot of farm produce gets damaged during transportation, or farmers are forced to sell at very cheap prices locally.
3. **Inadequate Storage and Processing Facilities:** A large percentage of agricultural output (like yams, fruits, and vegetables) goes to waste after harvest because there are no modern silos, cold rooms, or processing plants to preserve them. This forces farmers to sell immediately at low prices.
4. **Land Tenure System:** The traditional system of land ownership in Nigeria often leads to the continuous fragmentation of land among heirs. This results in small, scattered plots that cannot support mechanized commercial farming. Furthermore, acquiring large tracts of land for commercial agriculture is legally and culturally difficult.
5. **Poor Technology and Extension Services:** The continued use of crude tools limits how much a farmer can produce. Additionally, there is a shortage of agricultural extension workers (experts who visit farmers to teach them new, improved farming methods, such as how to apply fertilizers properly).
6. **Pests and Diseases:** Crops and livestock are frequently attacked by pests (e.g., locusts, armyworms, tsetse flies) and diseases (e.g., avian influenza in poultry, cassava mosaic disease), which reduce output and cause massive losses.
7. **Rural-Urban Migration:** The movement of able-bodied youths from rural areas to cities in search of better jobs creates a severe shortage of agricultural labour in the villages, driving up the cost of hired labour.
8. **Unpredictable Climate:** Because Nigerian agriculture depends heavily on natural rainfall, droughts, delayed rains, or severe floods can easily destroy an entire farming season.

## Agricultural policies and their effects

Since independence, the Nigerian government has introduced various policies and programmes aimed at solving these agricultural problems, increasing food production, and encouraging commercialization. 

### Appraising agricultural policies

Here is an appraisal of the major agricultural policies and programmes established in Nigeria, their objectives, and their effects:

**1. Operation Feed the Nation (OFN) - 1976**
* **Initiator:** General Olusegun Obasanjo's military regime.
* **Objective:** To mobilize all Nigerians (including students, civil servants, and the armed forces) to participate in agriculture. The goal was to achieve self-sufficiency in food production and reduce dependence on imported food.
* **Effects:** It succeeded in creating mass awareness about the importance of farming. However, it failed to produce a long-term structural change because it relied on enthusiasm rather than solid agricultural planning, and it lacked adequate supporting infrastructure (like fertilizers and improved seeds).

**2. The Green Revolution - 1980**
* **Initiator:** President Shehu Shagari's civilian administration.
* **Objective:** Similar to the OFN, its goal was to ensure self-sufficiency in food production within five years and to boost cash crop production for export. It involved the large-scale distribution of improved seeds, fertilizers, and modern equipment to farmers.
* **Effects:** While it led to a temporary increase in the production of some crops, the programme was heavily undermined by mismanagement of funds, corruption, and the late arrival of agricultural inputs (e.g., fertilizers arriving long after the planting season).

**3. Agricultural Development Programmes (ADPs) - Started 1974**
* **Initiator:** Established with the assistance of the World Bank, eventually spreading to all states in Nigeria.
* **Objective:** To boost the productivity and income of small-scale farmers by providing them with extension services, improved seeds, fertilizers, and rural infrastructure (like feeder roads and wells).
* **Effects:** The ADPs have been one of the most successful and enduring agricultural policies in Nigeria. They significantly improved the delivery of extension services to rural farmers, leading to better crop yields. However, funding challenges have weakened their impact in recent years.

**4. River Basin Development Authorities (RBDAs) - 1976**
* **Objective:** To harness the water resources of Nigeria's major rivers for year-round agricultural production. The main focus was to build dams, provide irrigation systems, and control floods so farmers would not rely on rainfall alone.
* **Effects:** They led to the construction of several dams and helped boost dry-season farming (especially vegetables and rice in northern Nigeria). However, they were expensive to run and suffered from political interference and poor maintenance.

**5. Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund (ACGSF) - 1977**
* **Initiator:** Established by the Federal Government and managed by the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN).
* **Objective:** To encourage commercial banks to lend money to farmers. Since agriculture is risky, the ACGSF guarantees the loans. If a farmer defaults due to natural disasters or crop failure, the scheme repays a large percentage (often 75%) of the loan to the bank.
* **Effects:** It significantly increased the flow of bank credit to the agricultural sector, allowing many commercial farmers to expand. 

**6. National Agricultural Land Development Authority (NALDA) - 1992**
* **Objective:** To execute a national agricultural land development programme, providing strategic support for land clearing, land preparation, and soil conservation to encourage large-scale farming.
* **Effects:** It opened up large tracts of land for farming but suffered from inconsistent government funding and was dormant for many years before recent attempts to revive it.

## Instability in agricultural incomes

A major economic feature of agriculture is that farmers' incomes are highly unstable. A farmer can make a huge amount of money one year and face severe poverty the next, even if they worked just as hard both years. 

### Evaluating the causes and effects of income instability

#### Causes of Income Instability

1. **Inelastic Demand for Agricultural Products:** This is the most important economic cause. Food items are necessities. This means that consumer demand for food does not change much whether prices are high or low (price inelasticity of demand). 
   * If there is a bumper harvest (a massive increase in supply), the market becomes flooded with food. Because people will not suddenly eat ten times more food just because it is cheap, the price of the food drops drastically. Consequently, the farmers' total revenue actually *falls* despite producing more. 
   * Conversely, if there is a poor harvest, supply falls, prices skyrocket, and the farmers who have crops to sell make massive revenues.
2. **Fluctuations in Supply (Natural Factors):** Agricultural output cannot be tightly controlled like a factory. Supply is subject to unpredictable weather (droughts, floods), pests, and diseases. A good rainy season leads to oversupply; a bad one leads to scarcity.
3. **Perishability of Products:** Most agricultural goods spoil quickly. Because farmers lack storage facilities, they cannot withhold their crops when prices are low. They are forced to sell everything immediately during the harvest season, which crashes the price.
4. **Long Gestation Period (The Cobweb Theorem):** Agriculture takes time. If the price of tomatoes is very high this year, all farmers will decide to plant tomatoes next year. By the time the tomatoes are harvested months later, there is an oversupply, and the price crashes. Disappointed by the low price, farmers base their next decision on this low price and plant fewer tomatoes the following year, leading to a shortage and high prices again. This continuous cycle of overproduction and underproduction is called the "Cobweb effect."
5. **Changes in World Market Prices:** For export crops like cocoa and rubber, farmers' incomes are dictated by international prices, which fluctuate wildly based on global demand and supply outside the farmers' control.

#### Effects of Income Instability

1. **Fluctuating Standards of Living:** Farmers cannot guarantee their standard of living. They may be wealthy one year and struggle to feed their families the next.
2. **Discourages Investment:** Because profits are unpredictable, individuals and commercial banks are discouraged from investing capital into agriculture.
3. **Rural-Urban Migration:** Unstable and unreliable incomes push young people to abandon farming and migrate to cities in search of steady, salaried jobs.
4. **Unstable Government Revenue:** The government's revenue from agricultural export taxes and farmer income taxes becomes unpredictable, making national economic planning difficult.

#### Solutions to Instability in Agricultural Incomes

To protect farmers and stabilize the economy, several solutions can be applied:

1. **Buffer Stock Schemes:** The government establishes an agency to buy excess crops from farmers during periods of bumper harvests (which stops prices from crashing). The agency stores these crops in large silos. During periods of scarcity or poor harvests, the agency releases the stored crops into the market (which stops prices from skyrocketing).
2. **Price Support / Minimum Guaranteed Prices:** The government sets a "price floor" (a minimum legal price) for agricultural produce. If the market price falls below this level, the government steps in and buys the crops at the guaranteed price, ensuring farmers do not run into a loss.
3. **Establishment of Marketing Boards:** Historically in Nigeria, Marketing Boards were government bodies that had the sole right to buy cash crops (like cocoa and groundnut) from farmers. They bought the crops at a fixed, stable price, regardless of how world market prices fluctuated, acting as a shield for the farmers against international price shocks.
4. **Provision of Processing and Storage Facilities:** If farmers can process fresh tomatoes into tomato paste or store grains in modern silos, they can hold on to their produce and sell it later when prices are favorable, rather than being forced to sell at harvest time.
5. **Agricultural Insurance:** Schemes like the Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation (NAIC) provide insurance cover for farmers. If their crops are destroyed by floods or pests, the insurance company compensates them, preventing total income loss.
6. **Improved Irrigation and Pest Control:** Reducing reliance on rainfall through irrigation (via River Basin Development Authorities) and providing pesticides helps guarantee a steady supply, reducing price fluctuations caused by natural disasters.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Subsistence Agriculture** | Small-scale farming producing mainly to feed the farmer and their family, not for profit. |
| **Commercial Agriculture** | Large-scale farming using modern technology, producing primarily for sale and maximum profit. |
| **Agro-allied Industries** | Industries that depend on agriculture for their raw materials (e.g., textile, beverage industries). |
| **Cash Crop** | A crop grown specifically to be sold for profit or exported, rather than for consumption by the farmer (e.g., cocoa, rubber). |
| **Inelastic Demand** | A situation where a change in the price of a good leads to a very small or no change in the quantity demanded (typical of food items). |
| **Buffer Stock** | A system where a government agency buys and stores excess farm produce during good harvests and releases it during poor harvests to stabilize prices. |
| **Price Floor** | A minimum price set by the government for agricultural produce to guarantee farmers a certain level of income. |
| **Marketing Board** | A public agency set up to buy export crops from farmers at stabilized prices and sell them on the world market. |
| **Extension Services** | The system of educating farmers on new, improved agricultural techniques and providing them with technical advice. |
| **Cobweb Theorem** | An economic model explaining how cycles of overproduction and underproduction occur because farmers base their current planting on the previous season's prices. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Understand the role of Elasticity:** A very common JAMB question asks why farmers' incomes are unstable. The fundamental economic reason is the **price inelasticity of demand** for agricultural products. People don't buy significantly more food just because it is cheap.
* **Know your Policies and Dates:** JAMB loves testing the initiators and years of agricultural policies. 
  * OFN = 1976 (Olusegun Obasanjo)
  * Green Revolution = 1980 (Shehu Shagari)
* **Distinguish the purposes of agencies:** 
  * If a question mentions *irrigation and water resources*, the answer is **River Basin Development Authorities (RBDAs)**.
  * If it mentions *guaranteeing bank loans for farmers*, the answer is **ACGSF** (managed by the Central Bank of Nigeria).
  * If it mentions *stabilizing export farmers' incomes in the past*, the answer is **Marketing Boards**.
* **Common mistake:** Confusing subsistence with commercial agriculture features. Remember that subsistence farming relies on *family labour* and *crude implements*, while commercial uses *hired labour* and *heavy machinery*.
* **Cobweb Theorem:** Even if the name isn't used, understand the concept: the long gestation period of crops means farmers cannot adjust supply immediately, leading to boom and bust cycles based on past prices.

## Quick summary

1. Agriculture in Nigeria is broadly divided into subsistence (small-scale, family feeding, crude tools) and commercial (large-scale, profit-driven, mechanized) agriculture. 
2. The sub-sectors of agriculture are crop production, livestock farming, forestry, and fishery.
3. Nigerian agriculture is characterized by small landholdings, crude implements, reliance on rainfall, and a high rate of subsistence farming.
4. Major problems facing agriculture include poor rural infrastructure, lack of credit facilities, bad land tenure systems, and inadequate storage facilities.
5. Agriculture aids economic development by providing food, employing over 60% of the workforce, earning foreign exchange, and supplying raw materials to agro-allied industries.
6. Key historical policies include Operation Feed the Nation (1976) and the Green Revolution (1980), aimed at food self-sufficiency, though they had limited long-term structural success.
7. Instability in farmers' incomes is primarily caused by the price inelasticity of demand for food, combined with wild fluctuations in supply due to weather, pests, and long gestation periods.
8. Income instability can be solved using buffer stock schemes, minimum price legislation, improved storage/processing facilities, and agricultural insurance.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary aim of subsistence agriculture is to:**
- A. produce raw materials for agro-allied industries
- B. maximize profit from international trade
- C. produce food for the farmer and their family
- D. provide employment for the urban population

**Answer: C.** Subsistence farming is a small-scale system where the farmer grows food primarily to feed themselves and their household, with little or no surplus for the market. The most tempting wrong option is A, but producing raw materials for industries on a large scale is a feature of commercial agriculture.

**2. Which of the following is a major economic reason for the instability of farmers' incomes in Nigeria?**
- A. The price elasticity of demand for agricultural products is perfectly elastic
- B. The demand for agricultural products is price inelastic
- C. Agricultural products have a very short gestation period
- D. There is an oversupply of storage facilities

**Answer: B.** The demand for food is price inelastic. Because people buy roughly the same amount of food regardless of price, a bumper harvest causes a massive drop in price, which reduces the farmers' total income. Option A is the common misunderstanding; students confuse elastic and inelastic demand. Food is a necessity, making its demand inelastic.

**3. An industry that depends on agriculture for its raw materials is known as:**
- A. an extractive industry
- B. a manufacturing monopoly
- C. an agro-allied industry
- D. a primary industry

**Answer: C.** Agro-allied industries (like textile mills using cotton, or chocolate factories using cocoa) are industries that rely directly on agricultural products as their raw materials. Option D is incorrect because agriculture itself is the primary industry, while the agro-allied sector is secondary (manufacturing).

**4. The Operation Feed the Nation (OFN) programme was introduced in Nigeria in the year:**
- A. 1960
- B. 1976
- C. 1980
- D. 1992

**Answer: B.** OFN was launched in 1976 by the military administration of General Olusegun Obasanjo to mobilize the nation towards food self-sufficiency. Option C is a common trap, as 1980 is the year the Green Revolution was launched by Shehu Shagari.

**5. Which of the following government agencies was established primarily to provide water for irrigation and manage water resources for agriculture?**
- A. Agricultural Development Programmes (ADPs)
- B. River Basin Development Authorities (RBDAs)
- C. Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund (ACGSF)
- D. National Agricultural Land Development Authority (NALDA)

**Answer: B.** The River Basin Development Authorities (RBDAs) were set up specifically to harness water resources, build dams, and provide irrigation to support dry-season farming. Option A is tempting, but ADPs focus more broadly on extension services and rural infrastructure, not primarily on major irrigation and water management.

**6. A system where the government buys agricultural produce during periods of surplus and releases it during periods of scarcity to stabilize prices is called a:**
- A. Price ceiling system
- B. Buffer stock scheme
- C. Marketing board scheme
- D. Subsidy scheme

**Answer: B.** A buffer stock scheme uses government storage (silos) to buy up excess supply during a good harvest (stopping prices from falling) and sell it during a bad harvest (stopping prices from rising). Option C is incorrect because a Marketing Board buys export crops to sell on the world market, whereas a buffer stock scheme physically stores crops to balance local supply and demand.

**7. Which of the following is a characteristic feature of peasant agriculture in Nigeria?**
- A. Cultivation of large estates
- B. Heavy reliance on hired skilled labour
- C. Use of sophisticated farm machinery
- D. Heavy reliance on family labour and crude tools

**Answer: D.** Peasant (subsistence) agriculture in Nigeria is characterized by small land plots, the use of basic implements like hoes and cutlasses, and reliance on the farmer's family for labour. Option B is a characteristic of commercial agriculture.

**8. The main function of the Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund (ACGSF) is to:**
- A. directly grant loans to peasant farmers
- B. guarantee the prices of cash crops on the world market
- C. protect commercial banks from total loss when they lend to farmers
- D. provide insurance against poor weather for crop farmers

**Answer: C.** The ACGSF was created to encourage commercial banks to lend to the risky agricultural sector by guaranteeing the loan and promising to repay a large portion if the farmer defaults. Option A is the common misunderstanding; the ACGSF does not lend money directly to farmers, it only guarantees loans given by commercial banks.

**9. The traditional land tenure system in Nigeria poses a problem for agricultural development mainly because it:**
- A. encourages foreign ownership of land
- B. leads to the fragmentation of land into small, uneconomic plots
- C. transfers land only to the government
- D. increases the fertility of the soil excessively

**Answer: B.** Under traditional communal or inheritance tenure, land is continually divided among heirs, resulting in scattered, tiny plots that are too small for commercial, mechanized farming. Option C is incorrect; it is the Land Use Act of 1978 that vested land in the state governors, not the traditional tenure system.

**10. In the past, which institution was responsible for stabilizing the incomes of export cash-crop farmers in Nigeria?**
- A. The Central Bank of Nigeria
- B. The Marketing Boards
- C. The Commercial Banks
- D. The Ministry of Trade

**Answer: B.** Marketing Boards were given the monopoly to buy export crops (like cocoa and groundnuts) from farmers at fixed, stable prices, sheltering the farmers from wild fluctuations in world market prices. Option A is tempting because the CBN manages many modern funds (like the ACGSF), but it was the regional Marketing Boards that stabilized export incomes.
