## Concepts of location and localization of industry

**Objective (i): differentiate between location and localization of industry.**
**Content a: Concepts and effects of location and localization of industry in Nigeria (Concepts)**

In economics, an **industry** is a group of firms producing similar or related goods and services (for example, the textile industry, the banking industry, or the cement industry). When we talk about where these firms set up their operations, we use two very important and distinct terms: **location** and **localization**.

### Location of industry
The **location of industry** refers to the siting or establishment of a single firm or factory in a particular place. It is the choice made by a business owner regarding where to build their factory or set up their business based on certain economic and social factors. For example, Dangote Cement choosing to site a massive cement plant in Obajana, Kogi State, is a matter of *location*.

### Localization of industry
The **localization of industry** refers to the concentration of many firms (usually producing similar or related products) in a specific geographical area. Over time, an area becomes famous for a particular type of industry, and new firms in that industry naturally want to set up there to enjoy the shared benefits. For example, Nnewi in Anambra State is heavily localized with automobile parts manufacturing, and Aba in Abia State is famous for the localization of the leather and shoemaking industry.

### Comparison Table: Location vs. Localization

| Feature | Location of Industry | Localization of Industry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | Siting a single firm in a particular place. | The concentration of many related firms in one geographical area. |
| **Focus** | An individual firm's decision. | A regional or geographic phenomenon. |
| **Cause** | Driven by a firm trying to minimize its costs (e.g., nearness to raw materials). | Driven by firms wanting to share benefits (external economies of scale) like a skilled labor pool. |
| **Examples in Nigeria** | Siting a petroleum refinery in Eleme (Rivers State); siting a sugar factory in Bacita (Kwara State). | Concentration of IT and computer firms in Computer Village (Ikeja, Lagos); auto parts in Nnewi. |

## Factors influencing the location and localization of industry

**Objective (ii): identify the factors influencing the location and localization of industry.**

Why do business owners choose one place over another? The decision is usually driven by the desire to minimize production costs and maximize profits.

### Factors influencing the location of an industry

1. **Proximity to Raw Materials:** Firms that use bulky or heavy raw materials prefer to locate close to the source to save on transport costs. If the raw material loses weight during production (like limestone turned into cement, or sugarcane into sugar), the factory must be near the raw material. This is why cement factories are located in Obajana (Kogi State) and Ewekoro (Ogun State) where limestone is abundant.
2. **Nearness to the Market:** If the finished product is bulky, fragile, or highly perishable (like baked bread, glass, or furniture), the firm will locate near its customers. It is easier and cheaper to transport the raw materials than the finished goods in this case.
3. **Availability of Power Supply:** Heavy manufacturing industries need constant and cheap electricity. An aluminum smelting plant, for instance, consumes massive amounts of electricity and will locate where power is reliable and affordable.
4. **Adequate Transport and Communication Networks:** Industries need good roads, railways, or seaports to bring in inputs and send out finished goods. Lagos is an industrial hub largely because of its seaports (Apapa and Tin Can Island) and extensive road networks.
5. **Availability of Labor:** Industries that require a lot of workers (labor-intensive industries) will locate where labor is cheap and plentiful. If they need highly skilled workers, they will locate near major cities or universities where such talents live.
6. **Government Policy:** The government can influence location by creating **industrial estates** (like the Agbara Industrial Estate in Ogun State) or by offering tax holidays to firms that locate in rural areas to encourage rural development.

### Factors influencing the localization of an industry

When one or two firms succeed in a specific area, others often follow. The factors that encourage this clustering (localization) include:

1. **Availability of a Skilled Labor Pool:** If an area is known for a certain trade, workers with those specific skills will migrate there. New firms will locate there because they won't have to spend much time or money training new staff (e.g., leather workers in Aba).
2. **Development of Infrastructure:** When many firms gather in one place, the government or the firms themselves will build better roads, power stations, and water supplies to serve the area.
3. **Presence of Ancillary (Subsidiary) Industries:** These are smaller firms that supply parts or services to the main industry. If many car manufacturers are in one area, companies that make tires, windshields, and batteries will also open there, creating a strong industrial cluster.
4. **Established Markets:** Buyers from all over the country or continent will travel to a localized area because they know they will find a wide variety of goods at competitive prices. Examples include the Alaba International Market for electronics or the Kano textile markets.
5. **Financial and Commercial Services:** Banks, insurance companies, and transport firms quickly set up branches in localized areas to serve the booming businesses.

## Effects of location and localization of industry in Nigeria

**Content a: Concepts and effects of location and localization of industry in Nigeria (Effects)**

The concentration of industries in specific areas brings both positive and negative effects to the Nigerian economy.

### Advantages of Localization (External Economies of Scale)

When firms cluster together, they enjoy benefits that reduce their average costs of production. These benefits are called **external economies of scale**:
1. **Creation of Employment:** Localized areas become major employment hubs, providing jobs for thousands of people.
2. **Emergence of Subsidiary Firms:** Small businesses spring up to supply raw materials, packaging, and maintenance services to the big factories.
3. **Development of Infrastructure:** The high concentration of economic activity forces the government to provide better roads, electricity, and water supply.
4. **Pool of Skilled Labor:** Firms have easy access to highly trained workers, reducing recruitment and training costs.
5. **Joint Research and Development:** Firms close to one another can collaborate on research or share a local training institute to improve their products.

### Disadvantages of Localization (External Diseconomies of Scale)

When an area becomes *too* concentrated, the benefits can turn into problems (external diseconomies):
1. **Traffic Congestion:** Too many trucks, workers, and businesses cause severe traffic jams, delaying the transport of goods (a major issue in Lagos industrial areas).
2. **Environmental Pollution:** A concentration of factories leads to high levels of air, water, and noise pollution, which endangers the health of residents (e.g., soot in Port Harcourt).
3. **Overcrowding and Slums:** As people rush to industrial areas for jobs, housing becomes scarce and expensive, leading to the creation of slums.
4. **Rural-Urban Migration:** Young people abandon agriculture in rural areas to seek industrial jobs in the city, leading to a decline in food production and rural underdevelopment.
5. **Uneven Regional Development:** Wealth, jobs, and infrastructure become concentrated in a few cities (like Lagos, Kano, and Port Harcourt), leaving other parts of the country poor and underdeveloped.
6. **Security Risks:** In times of war or conflict, a localized industrial area becomes a prime target. If it is destroyed, the entire country's economy suffers.

## Industrialization and economic development in Nigeria

**Content c: Industrialization and economic development in Nigeria**
**Objective (v): examine the role of industry in economic development.**

**Industrialization** is the process of transforming an economy from primarily agricultural and primary production to one based on the manufacturing of goods. It involves the widespread use of machinery, technology, and factory production.

For a developing country like Nigeria, industrialization is often seen as the primary engine for economic development. Here is the role industry plays in economic development:

1. **Diversification of the Economy:** Nigeria heavily relies on the export of crude oil. Industrialization helps diversify the economy so that if global oil prices fall, the country can rely on the export of manufactured goods.
2. **Employment Generation:** Manufacturing industries create millions of direct jobs (factory workers, engineers, managers) and indirect jobs (transporters, raw material suppliers). This reduces unemployment and poverty.
3. **Increase in National Income (GDP):** Manufactured goods have a higher market value than raw, unprocessed agricultural goods. Adding value through industry significantly increases a country's Gross Domestic Product.
4. **Conservation of Foreign Exchange:** By manufacturing goods locally instead of importing them, a country saves its foreign exchange reserves. This strengthens the local currency (the Naira).
5. **Technological Advancement:** Industrialization forces a country to adopt new technologies, train its workforce in modern skills, and invest in research and development.
6. **Improvement in Standard of Living:** With better jobs, higher incomes, and a wider variety of locally produced goods available at cheaper prices, the general standard of living of the citizens improves.
7. **Stimulation of Other Sectors:** Industry requires raw materials from agriculture and mining. A booming industrial sector creates a massive market for farmers (e.g., a local fruit juice factory buys pineapples and oranges from local farmers).

## Strategies of Industrialization in Nigeria

**Content b: Strategies and Industrialization in Nigeria**
**Objective (iv): appraise some industrialization strategies.**

To promote industrial development, the Nigerian government has adopted several strategies over the years. We must not only list them but **appraise** them (look at their advantages and disadvantages).

### 1. Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI)
This strategy involves establishing domestic industries to produce goods that the country formerly imported. For example, instead of importing textile materials or assembled cars, Nigeria set up local textile mills (like Kaduna Textiles) and vehicle assembly plants (like Peugeot Automobile Nigeria in Kaduna) in the 1970s.

* **Appraisal (Advantages):** It helps conserve foreign exchange, creates local employment, protects infant industries from foreign competition, and encourages local production.
* **Appraisal (Disadvantages):** In Nigeria, this strategy often failed because the local factories still relied heavily on *imported* raw materials and machinery. It also led to the production of high-cost, low-quality goods due to a lack of foreign competition, and domestic markets were often too small to support large-scale production.

### 2. Export Promotion Industrialization (EPI)
This strategy focuses on producing manufactured goods specifically for export to foreign markets. Instead of just exporting raw cocoa beans, a country processes them into cocoa butter or chocolate for export. The Nigerian Export Promotion Council (NEPC) promotes this.

* **Appraisal (Advantages):** It earns foreign exchange, forces local industries to improve quality to meet international standards, and opens up a massive global market, allowing for economies of scale.
* **Appraisal (Disadvantages):** Nigerian industries face stiff competition from highly efficient producers in Asia and the West. Furthermore, poor infrastructure (like erratic power) makes Nigerian goods too expensive to compete globally. Western countries also place high tariffs on imported manufactured goods to protect their own industries.

### 3. Promotion of Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs)
Recognizing that large factories require massive capital, the government shifted focus to supporting SMEs. Agencies like SMEDAN (Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria) and the Bank of Industry (BOI) provide soft loans and training to small-scale manufacturers.

* **Appraisal:** SMEs use local raw materials, are labor-intensive (creating many jobs), and help develop indigenous entrepreneurial skills. However, they suffer from high mortality rates due to lack of capital, poor management, and harsh business environments.

### 4. Establishment of Industrial Estates
The government buys large tracts of land, provides them with good roads, electricity, water, and security, and then invites industries to set up their factories there.

* **Appraisal:** It solves the problem of poor infrastructure and land acquisition for factories. However, they are expensive for the government to maintain, and sometimes become concentrated in only a few urban areas, worsening rural-urban migration.

## Problems of industrialization in Nigeria

**Objective (iii): examine the problems of industrialization.**

Despite the strategies above, Nigeria's industrial sector (manufacturing) still contributes a small percentage to the GDP. The major problems hindering industrialization include:

1. **Inadequate Capital:** Setting up factories requires huge capital investment. Banks in Nigeria often charge very high interest rates, making it difficult for entrepreneurs to borrow money for long-term industrial projects.
2. **Poor Infrastructure:** This is arguably the biggest challenge. The erratic power supply forces industries to run on expensive diesel generators, drastically increasing the cost of production. Poor road and rail networks also increase transport costs.
3. **Lack of Skilled Manpower:** Modern industries require skilled engineers, technicians, and managers. Nigeria faces a shortage of technical skills, leading to a reliance on expensive foreign experts (expatriates).
4. **Political Instability and Policy Inconsistency:** Frequent changes in government or sudden changes in economic policies (policy somersaults) scare away foreign and local investors. An investor will not build a 20-year factory if they fear the rules will change next year.
5. **Small Market Size (Low Purchasing Power):** Although Nigeria has a large population, the high rate of poverty means many people have low purchasing power. Industries struggle to sell goods in large enough quantities to make a profit.
6. **Over-reliance on Imported Inputs:** Many Nigerian manufacturing firms are basically "assembly plants" that import 90% of their raw materials and machinery. When the Naira falls in value, their production costs skyrocket, forcing many to close down.
7. **Unfair Foreign Competition:** Smuggling and the dumping of cheap, substandard goods from foreign countries make it impossible for local industries to compete.

## Funding and management of business organization

**Content d: Funding and management of business organization**

For industries to thrive, the business organizations running them must be well-funded and properly managed.

### Funding of Business Organizations
Firms need capital to start, run, and expand their operations. The sources of funding are generally categorized into short-term, medium-term, and long-term capital.

1. **Internal Sources:**
   * **Personal Savings:** The owner's personal money (common in sole proprietorships).
   * **Retained Earnings (Ploughed-back profit):** A portion of the company's profit that is kept back in the business for expansion rather than being distributed as dividends to shareholders.

2. **External Sources:**
   * **Bank Overdrafts and Trade Credit:** Short-term funding where a bank allows a firm to withdraw more than it has in its account, or suppliers allow the firm to buy raw materials and pay later.
   * **Bank Loans:** Medium to long-term borrowing from commercial banks, industrial banks (like the Bank of Industry), or development banks.
   * **Issuance of Shares (Equity):** Public limited companies can raise massive amounts of permanent capital by selling shares to the general public on the Stock Exchange. Shareholders become part-owners of the business.
   * **Debentures (Bonds):** A firm can borrow long-term capital from the public by issuing debentures. A debenture is a certificate of indebtedness. Unlike shareholders, debenture holders are not owners; they are creditors who receive a fixed interest rate whether the company makes a profit or not.

### Management of Business Organizations
The management structure depends on the size and type of the business. (Note: The types of business organizations are covered fully in `A19-business-organizations.md`).

* **In Small Firms (Sole Proprietorships/Partnerships):** Ownership and management are usually the same. The owner makes all decisions, manages the daily operations, and takes all the risks.
* **In Large Firms (Limited Liability Companies):** There is a **separation of ownership and control**.
   * **Shareholders (Owners):** They own the company but are too numerous to run it. They vote at the Annual General Meeting (AGM) to elect a Board of Directors.
   * **Board of Directors:** They are responsible for making the broad, strategic policies of the company and protecting the shareholders' investments.
   * **Management Team:** Led by a Managing Director (MD) or Chief Executive Officer (CEO), they are hired by the Board to handle the day-to-day running of the business. They hire staff, manage production, and execute the board's policies.

## Factors determining the size of firms

**Content e: Factors determining the size of firms**

Why are some businesses as small as a neighborhood bakery, while others are as large as the Dangote Refinery? The size of a firm is determined by several factors:

1. **Availability of Capital:** A firm can only grow as large as its finances allow. Sole proprietorships remain small because one person's capital is limited. Public limited companies can grow very large because they can easily raise millions through the stock market.
2. **The Extent of the Market:** As Adam Smith noted, "the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market." If the demand for a product is small or localized, the firm will remain small. If there is a massive national or global demand, the firm will expand to meet it.
3. **Nature of the Business (Type of Product):** Some businesses are naturally small because they provide personalized services (e.g., hair salons, tailoring, legal advice). Large-scale mass production is not possible here. Conversely, heavy industries like steel making or petroleum refining *must* be large to be efficient and cover their massive fixed costs.
4. **Entrepreneurial and Managerial Ability:** A firm cannot grow beyond the organizational skills of its management. Poor managers will run a large firm into the ground, forcing it to shrink or close. Highly skilled managers can grow a small firm into a multinational corporation.
5. **Government Policy:** Anti-monopoly laws may prevent a firm from growing too large to stop it from dominating the market. Conversely, government subsidies and protection can help small local firms grow into large entities.
6. **Type of Business Organization:** A sole trader is restricted by law and capacity. A public limited company is legally structured to absorb limitless capital and thousands of owners, naturally allowing for a massive size.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Location vs. Localization:** The most common mistake is confusing these two. Remember: **Location** = One firm deciding where to sit. **Localization** = Many firms gathering together in one area.
* **Import Substitution vs. Export Promotion:** Import substitution looks *inward* (making things for ourselves so we stop buying from abroad). Export promotion looks *outward* (making things to sell to the rest of the world).
* **Debentures vs. Shares:** Both are ways large companies raise money. However, shareholders are **owners** and get *dividends* (which vary with profit). Debenture holders are **creditors** and get *fixed interest* (even if the company makes a loss).
* **Diseconomies of localization:** Questions often ask for the disadvantages of localization. Look out for options like "traffic congestion", "pollution", and "rural-urban drift".
* **Factors of location:** If a question asks why a cement factory is in Obajana, the answer is "nearness to raw materials" because limestone loses weight when turned into cement.

## Quick summary

1. The **location of industry** is the siting of a single firm, influenced by proximity to raw materials, markets, power, transport, and labor.
2. The **localization of industry** is the clustering of many related firms in one area, driven by a shared skilled labor pool, developed infrastructure, and specialized markets.
3. Localization brings external economies of scale (shared benefits) but can lead to congestion, pollution, and regional imbalance.
4. **Industrialization** drives economic development by creating jobs, diversifying the economy away from raw materials, raising GDP, and saving foreign exchange.
5. **Import Substitution** replaces imported goods with local ones, while **Export Promotion** focuses on producing manufactured goods for foreign markets.
6. Major problems of industrialization in Nigeria include poor infrastructure (electricity), inadequate capital, political instability, and over-reliance on imported raw materials.
7. Firms are funded through internal sources (retained profits) and external sources (shares, debentures, bank loans), and their size is determined by capital, market size, and the nature of the business.

## Practice questions

**1. The concentration of many firms producing related goods in a specific geographical area is known as:**
- A. location of industry
- B. industrial integration
- C. localization of industry
- D. industrial amalgamation

**Answer: C.** Localization of industry refers to the clustering of many firms in one area, like auto parts dealers in Nnewi. Location (Option A) refers to a single firm.

**2. A manufacturing firm that uses heavy, weight-losing raw materials is most likely to be located near the:**
- A. source of power
- B. market for the finished goods
- C. source of the raw materials
- D. financial center of the country

**Answer: C.** If raw materials are heavy and lose weight during production (like limestone to cement), transporting them is very expensive. The firm will locate near the raw materials to save on transport costs.

**3. Which of the following is a major disadvantage of localization of industry?**
- A. Creation of employment opportunities
- B. Emergence of subsidiary industries
- C. Joint research and development
- D. Uneven regional development

**Answer: D.** Uneven regional development is a disadvantage (diseconomy) because wealth and jobs become concentrated in a few areas, leaving other areas underdeveloped. Options A, B, and C are advantages.

**4. The industrialization strategy where a country establishes local industries to produce goods it formerly imported is called:**
- A. export promotion
- B. import substitution
- C. industrial integration
- D. indigenization

**Answer: B.** Import substitution industrialization (ISI) aims to replace imported goods with domestically produced ones to save foreign exchange and boost local manufacturing.

**5. Which of the following is a long-term source of finance for a public limited company but does NOT make the contributor an owner?**
- A. Retained earnings
- B. Issuance of ordinary shares
- C. Bank overdraft
- D. Issuance of debentures

**Answer: D.** A debenture is a certificate of a long-term loan. Debenture holders are creditors who receive fixed interest, not owners. Shareholders (Option B) are owners. Overdrafts (Option C) are short-term.

**6. The primary role of the Board of Directors in a large public limited company is to:**
- A. supervise the day-to-day factory operations
- B. formulate broad strategic policies and protect shareholders' interests
- C. audit the financial statements independently
- D. provide personal capital to fund the business

**Answer: B.** In large companies, ownership is separated from control. Shareholders elect the Board to make broad policies, while the Board hires a management team for day-to-day operations (Option A).

**7. In Nigeria, the major problem hindering rapid industrialization is:**
- A. a small population size
- B. excessive supply of skilled labor
- C. poor infrastructural facilities, especially power
- D. over-production of manufactured goods

**Answer: C.** Poor infrastructure, particularly erratic electricity supply, forces industries to rely on expensive generators, driving up production costs and crippling industrialization.

**8. Which of the following factors limits the size of a firm providing personalized services like hairdressing?**
- A. The extent of the market
- B. The nature of the business
- C. Government antitrust laws
- D. High technological requirements

**Answer: B.** The nature of the business (personalized service) prevents mass production. You cannot use a large factory assembly line to cut hair, so the firm naturally remains small.

**9. External economies of scale are benefits enjoyed by a firm as a result of:**
- A. the expansion of the firm itself
- B. the concentration of many firms in a particular area
- C. the introduction of new management techniques
- D. the increase in the price of its products

**Answer: B.** External economies (like a shared pool of skilled labor and better infrastructure) arise from the growth of the industry as a whole in a localized area, not just the single firm.

**10. One valid argument against the import substitution strategy in developing countries is that it often:**
- A. leads to the dumping of cheap foreign goods
- B. encourages excessive exportation of raw materials
- C. results in high-cost, protected industries that rely heavily on imported inputs
- D. decreases the employment rate in the urban centers

**Answer: C.** Import substitution industries in countries like Nigeria often failed because they were shielded from competition (leading to inefficiency and high costs) and still spent massive foreign exchange importing their machinery and raw materials.
