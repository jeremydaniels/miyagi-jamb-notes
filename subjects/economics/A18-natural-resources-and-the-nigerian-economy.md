## Development of major natural resources

**Objective i: trace the development of the major natural resources in Nigeria.**
**Content a: Development of major natural resources (petroleum, gold, diamond, timber etc).**

Natural resources are the "gifts of nature" found in a country, such as minerals, forests, water, and fertile land. In Nigeria, the development of these resources has shifted over time, moving from agriculture and solid minerals to crude oil.

### 1. Timber and Forest Resources
Before the discovery of crude oil, Nigeria relied heavily on agricultural and forest resources. **Timber** (wood used for building and carpentry) was one of the earliest natural resources exploited for export. Thick rainforests in the southern part of Nigeria (places like Edo, Delta, and Ondo states) provided mahogany, Iroko, and Obeche trees. Timber extraction peaked during the colonial era but has since declined due to severe deforestation and the shift to petroleum.

### 2. Solid Minerals (Coal, Tin, Columbite, Gold)
In the early to mid-20th century, solid minerals were the backbone of the Nigerian economy:
- **Coal:** Discovered in **Enugu** in 1909, commercial mining began in 1916. Coal was used to power the country's railway system and generate electricity. Its importance fell when Nigeria switched to diesel and gas.
- **Tin and Columbite:** Discovered and heavily mined on the **Jos Plateau**. Nigeria was once a leading global producer of columbite. 
- **Gold:** Gold mining began in Nigeria around 1913, reaching its peak in the 1930s before the Second World War. It is found in states like Osun (Ilesha), Zamfara, and Niger, but today it is mostly mined informally by artisanal miners.

*(Note on the syllabus wording: The syllabus mentions **diamonds**. While diamonds are a major natural resource in Africa—driving the economies of countries like Botswana, Sierra Leone, and South Africa—they are not found in commercial quantities in Nigeria. However, as an economics student, you should know that diamond extraction in Africa followed a similar path of early colonial discovery followed by massive export, and sometimes led to conflicts known as "blood diamonds".)*

### 3. Petroleum (Crude Oil)
The search for crude oil in Nigeria began in 1908 by the Nigerian Bitumen Corporation, but it was unsuccessful. Shell D'Arcy (later Shell-BP) started exploring in 1937. 
- **The Breakthrough:** In **1956**, commercial quantities of crude oil were discovered at **Oloibiri** in present-day Bayelsa State.
- **First Export:** Nigeria exported its first crude oil in **1958**.
- **The Oil Boom:** By the 1970s, the global price of oil skyrocketed. Nigeria experienced an "oil boom," and petroleum quickly replaced agriculture and solid minerals as the country's main source of revenue. Today, crude oil is extracted mostly from the Niger Delta region and offshore in the Atlantic Ocean.

## Contributions of the oil and the non-oil sectors to the Nigerian economy

**Objective ii: understand the contribution of the oil and the non-oil sectors to the Nigerian economy.**
**Content b: Contributions of the oil and the non-oil sectors to the Nigerian economy.**

The Nigerian economy is broadly divided into two sectors: the **oil sector** (crude oil and natural gas) and the **non-oil sector** (agriculture, solid minerals, manufacturing, telecommunications, and services). 

### The Oil Sector
1. **Foreign Exchange Earnings:** Crude oil is Nigeria's dominant export. It accounts for about 80% to 90% of the country's foreign exchange earnings (forex), which the country uses to import machinery, medical supplies, and consumer goods.
2. **Government Revenue:** Taxes, royalties, and export sales from crude oil provide the majority of the Federal Government's revenue, which is shared among the federal, state, and local governments.
3. **Infrastructural Development:** The massive revenues from the 1970s oil boom financed the building of highways, bridges, universities, and the new capital city, Abuja.
4. **Energy Supply:** Natural gas and petroleum products power Nigeria’s electrical grid, vehicles, and industries.

### The Non-Oil Sector
1. **Employment Generation:** While the oil sector generates the most money, it is highly mechanized and employs very few people. The non-oil sector, especially agriculture and the informal service sector, employs over 60% of the Nigerian workforce.
2. **Food Supply:** Agriculture provides the food needed to feed Nigeria's massive population.
3. **Raw Materials for Industries:** The non-oil sector provides inputs for local industries (e.g., cotton for textiles, cocoa for beverages, limestone for cement).
4. **Economic Diversification:** A strong non-oil sector protects the economy from the shock of falling global oil prices.

### Comparing the Two Sectors

| Feature | Oil Sector | Non-Oil Sector |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Main Output** | Crude oil, natural gas, petrochemicals | Agricultural produce, manufactured goods, services |
| **Employment** | Employs a very small percentage of the workforce (capital-intensive) | Employs the vast majority of Nigerians (labour-intensive) |
| **Government Revenue** | Contributes the majority of government revenue and foreign exchange | Contributes less to government revenue (though non-oil taxes are growing) |
| **Location** | Concentrated in the Niger Delta and offshore | Spread across the entire country |

**Common Mistake:** Students often assume the oil sector is the largest contributor to Nigeria's Gross Domestic Product (GDP). This is false. The non-oil sector (especially services and agriculture) makes up the largest share of the GDP. However, the oil sector is the largest source of *government revenue* and *foreign exchange*.

### The "Dutch Disease"
When Nigeria experienced the oil boom in the 1970s, it focused heavily on petroleum and neglected agriculture and solid minerals. This economic phenomenon—where the discovery and boom of one natural resource leads to the decline of other sectors (like agriculture and manufacturing)—is called the **Dutch Disease**.

## Linkage effects of natural resources

**Objective iii: establish the linkages between the natural resources and other sectors.**
**Content c: Linkage effects.**

In economics, a **linkage effect** (a concept introduced by Albert Hirschman) occurs when the growth of one industry encourages the growth of other industries. Natural resources can stimulate economic development through three main types of linkages:

1. **Forward Linkages:** This happens when a natural resource is used as a **raw material (input)** to create a new industry.
   - *Example:* The extraction of crude oil leads to the establishment of **refineries** and **petrochemical industries** (making plastics, fertilizers, and nylon). 
   - *Example:* Growing cotton (agriculture) leads to the creation of **textile mills** to make clothes.
2. **Backward Linkages:** This happens when an industry demands **tools, machinery, or services**, leading to the creation of new industries that supply those needs.
   - *Example:* The oil extraction industry needs drilling pipes, rigs, and engineering services. This encourages local businesses to start manufacturing pipes and offering maritime transport services to supply the oil rigs.
   - *Example:* The agriculture sector needs tractors and fertilizers, encouraging the growth of local tractor manufacturing and chemical plants.
3. **Consumption (or Demand) Linkages:** When workers in the natural resource sector earn wages, they spend their money on food, housing, and entertainment. This creates a market for local farmers, builders, and shopkeepers.

## Distinguishing upstream and downstream activities in the oil sector

**Objective v: distinguish between the upstream and downstream activities in the oil sector.**
**Content d: Upstream/downstream of the oil sector.**

The petroleum industry is broadly divided into two main stages: **Upstream** and **Downstream**. (Some modern textbooks also mention "Midstream" for transportation and storage, but JAMB strictly focuses on the upstream vs downstream distinction).

### Upstream Sector
The upstream sector is the "Exploration and Production" (E&P) phase. It involves searching for crude oil underground or underwater, and bringing it to the surface.
- **Activities:** Geological surveys, drilling test wells, operating oil rigs, and pumping crude oil out of the ground.
- **Key players:** Multinational oil companies (e.g., Shell, Chevron, ExxonMobil) and indigenous exploration companies.

### Downstream Sector
The downstream sector deals with processing the raw crude oil into usable products and delivering them to consumers.
- **Activities:** Refining crude oil into petrol (PMS), diesel, and kerosene; transporting products via pipelines and tankers; and selling them at petrol stations. It also includes the petrochemical industry.
- **Key players:** Oil refineries, fuel marketers (e.g., Oando, TotalEnergies, Conoil), and petrol station owners.

### Comparison Table: Upstream vs Downstream

| Feature | Upstream Sector | Downstream Sector |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | Finding and extracting raw crude oil and natural gas | Processing, marketing, and distributing the final products |
| **Primary Output** | Unrefined Crude Oil and Raw Natural Gas | Petrol, Diesel, Kerosene, Jet Fuel, Asphalt, Plastics |
| **Key Activities** | Exploration, drilling, pumping | Refining, transporting (tankers), retailing |
| **Location** | Oil fields, offshore platforms (rigs) | Refineries, pipelines, petrol filling stations |

## The role of NNPC and OPEC in the oil sector

**Objective vi: examine the roles of NNPC and OPEC in the oil sector.**
**Content e: The role of NNPC and OPEC in the oil sector.**

Nigeria's oil sector is heavily influenced by two major organizations: a national one (NNPC) and an international one (OPEC).

### The Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation (NNPC)
Established in 1977, the NNPC was created to represent the Nigerian government’s interests in the oil and gas industry. 
*(Note on current affairs: Under the Petroleum Industry Act of 2021, the corporation was transitioned into a commercial, profit-oriented company named **NNPC Limited**. However, its historical roles remain a key part of the JAMB syllabus).*

**Roles of the NNPC:**
1. **Exploration and Production:** NNPC explores for oil and manages the government's investment in joint ventures (JVs) with multinational oil companies like Shell and Chevron.
2. **Refining:** It manages Nigeria’s state-owned oil refineries located in Port Harcourt, Warri, and Kaduna.
3. **Distribution:** NNPC plays a major role in importing refined petroleum products and distributing them across the country to prevent fuel scarcity.
4. **Regulation (Historically):** In the past, NNPC regulated the petroleum industry, issuing licenses and setting safety standards. (Today, the PIA has created new independent agencies like the NUPRC and NMDPRA to handle regulation).

### The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC)
OPEC is an international cartel formed in **1960** in Baghdad, Iraq, by five founding members: Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela. **Nigeria joined OPEC in 1971**.

**Roles of OPEC:**
1. **Price Stabilization:** OPEC’s main goal is to coordinate the petroleum policies of its member countries to ensure stable global oil prices. It prevents prices from falling too low or rising too high.
2. **Production Quotas:** To control prices, OPEC uses a quota system. It tells each member country exactly how many barrels of oil they are allowed to produce per day. If there is too much oil in the world market (which makes prices drop), OPEC asks members to cut production.
3. **Protecting Member Interests:** OPEC ensures member nations get a steady income from their natural resources and are not exploited by powerful foreign consumer nations.
4. **Information Sharing:** OPEC provides technical and economic data to its members to help them manage their oil sectors efficiently.

## Challenges facing natural resources exploitation

**Content f: Challenges facing natural resources exploitation.**

Extracting natural resources is a difficult business. In Nigeria, the sector faces several severe challenges:
1. **Price Volatility:** The Nigerian economy depends heavily on crude oil exports. When global oil prices crash, government revenue drops sharply, leading to budget deficits and economic crises.
2. **Sabotage and Oil Theft (Bunkering):** In the Niger Delta, pipelines are frequently vandalized by criminals stealing crude oil, causing massive revenue loss for the country.
3. **Militancy and Insecurity:** Local communities, feeling cheated out of the wealth extracted from their lands, have sometimes resorted to armed militancy. This disrupts oil production and scares away foreign investors.
4. **Over-dependence (The Resource Curse):** Because oil money was so easy to make in the 1970s, Nigeria abandoned agriculture and manufacturing (the Dutch Disease). This over-dependence means the economy lacks a solid foundation.
5. **Poor Maintenance of Refineries:** Because Nigeria's domestic refineries (in Port Harcourt, Warri, and Kaduna) frequently break down or operate below capacity, the country exports crude oil but ironically has to import refined fuel at a higher cost.

## The environmental effects of exploitation activities in Nigeria

**Objective iv: analyse the environmental effects of exploitation activities in Nigeria.**

Exploiting natural resources, particularly crude oil and timber, has caused severe damage to the Nigerian environment, especially in the Niger Delta.

1. **Oil Spills:** Pipeline leaks and equipment failures cause millions of gallons of crude oil to spill into the environment. Oil spills destroy farmlands, making the soil infertile for agriculture.
2. **Water Pollution and Destruction of Aquatic Life:** Oil spills flow into rivers and creeks, killing fish, crabs, and other aquatic life. This destroys the livelihood of local fishermen and poisons drinking water sources.
3. **Gas Flaring:** When drilling for oil, natural gas often comes up with it. Instead of capturing this gas, companies often burn it off into the air—a process called gas flaring. This releases toxic chemicals, causes acid rain (which corrodes zinc roofs), and contributes to global warming.
4. **Deforestation and Land Degradation:** Extracting timber and solid minerals requires cutting down vast areas of forests and digging large open pits. This leads to soil erosion, loss of wildlife habitat, and landslides.

## Controlling the effects of natural resources exploitation

**Objective vii: suggest ways of controlling the effects of natural resources exploitation.**

To protect the environment and the economy, several measures must be taken:
1. **Strict Environmental Legislation:** The government must enforce strict laws against pollution. Agencies like the **National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA)** and the **National Oil Spill Detection and Response Agency (NOSDRA)** must be empowered to fine and penalize polluting companies.
2. **Banning Gas Flaring:** The government must strictly enforce the ban on gas flaring and force companies to capture the gas for domestic electricity generation or export (e.g., as Liquefied Natural Gas - LNG).
3. **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR):** Oil companies must be compelled to develop their host communities by building schools, hospitals, and providing clean water, reducing the feeling of marginalization that causes militancy.
4. **Bioremediation:** This is the use of microorganisms to clean up polluted environments. Oil companies must fund the clean-up of oil spills in places like Ogoniland using scientific methods.
5. **Economic Diversification:** To cure the Dutch Disease, Nigeria must heavily invest oil revenues into agriculture, technology, and manufacturing, ensuring the economy can survive when the oil dries up.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Upstream Sector** | The part of the oil industry focused on exploration and extraction of crude oil. |
| **Downstream Sector** | The part of the oil industry focused on refining, marketing, and distributing petroleum products. |
| **Dutch Disease** | An economic condition where a boom in one sector (like oil) causes a decline in other sectors (like agriculture). |
| **Forward Linkage** | When the output of one industry is used as the raw material for a new industry (e.g., crude oil to plastics). |
| **Backward Linkage** | When an industry's demand for tools and services stimulates the growth of industries that provide those inputs. |
| **Gas Flaring** | The burning off of natural gas associated with crude oil extraction, causing severe air pollution. |
| **Production Quota** | A limit set by OPEC on how much crude oil a member country is allowed to produce, used to control global prices. |
| **Resource Curse** | The paradox where countries with abundant natural resources tend to have less economic growth and worse development outcomes than countries with fewer natural resources. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Oloibiri (1956):** Always remember the location and date of the first commercial oil discovery in Nigeria. It is a highly tested fact.
- **NNPC vs OPEC:** Know the difference. NNPC is Nigeria's national corporation; OPEC is the international cartel fixing global quotas. 
- **Upstream vs Downstream:** If a question mentions "refining" or "petrol stations", it is downstream. If it mentions "drilling" or "exploration", it is upstream.
- **Linkages:** A forward linkage moves the product *forward* in the production chain (towards the consumer). A backward linkage moves *backward* (towards the supplier of tools/services).
- **GDP vs Revenue:** Remember that while oil provides most of Nigeria's *revenue* and *foreign exchange*, agriculture/services employ more people and contribute more to the overall *GDP*.

## Quick summary

1. Natural resources in Nigeria transitioned from agricultural products and solid minerals (coal in Enugu, tin in Jos) to crude oil following the 1956 discovery in Oloibiri.
2. The oil sector provides over 80% of Nigeria's foreign exchange and government revenue, while the non-oil sector provides the vast majority of employment.
3. The "Dutch Disease" occurs when an oil boom leads to the neglect of agriculture and other productive sectors.
4. Linkage effects can be forward (resource used as input for new factories) or backward (resource extraction demands local tools and services).
5. The upstream oil sector deals with exploration and drilling, while the downstream sector deals with refining and marketing.
6. NNPC manages Nigeria's oil interests and refineries, while OPEC (which Nigeria joined in 1971) controls global oil prices using production quotas.
7. Exploitation of oil causes severe environmental damage, particularly oil spills and gas flaring, which destroy aquatic life and cause acid rain.
8. These effects can be controlled through strict environmental laws (NESREA, NOSDRA), ending gas flaring, and ensuring companies clean up spills and develop host communities.

## Practice questions

**1. Commercial quantities of crude oil were first discovered in Nigeria at**
- A. Warri in 1960
- B. Oloibiri in 1956
- C. Enugu in 1909
- D. Port Harcourt in 1958

**Answer: B.** Crude oil was first discovered in commercial quantities in Oloibiri (in present-day Bayelsa State) in 1956. Option C is the discovery of coal.

**2. Which of the following is an example of a forward linkage in the petroleum sector?**
- A. The establishment of companies that manufacture drilling pipes
- B. The employment of local security guards at oil rigs
- C. The building of petrochemical plants to manufacture plastics
- D. The provision of catering services for oil workers

**Answer: C.** A forward linkage occurs when the output of an industry (crude oil) is used as a raw material for a new industry (petrochemicals/plastics). Option A is a backward linkage, and D is a consumption linkage.

**3. The economic phenomenon where a boom in the oil sector leads to the neglect and decline of the agricultural sector is known as**
- A. the linkage effect
- B. the Dutch Disease
- C. the multiplier effect
- D. backward integration

**Answer: B.** The Dutch Disease describes the negative impact on an economy of anything that gives rise to a sharp inflow of foreign currency, leading to the decline of other sectors, especially manufacturing and agriculture.

**4. Which of the following activities belongs to the downstream sector of the petroleum industry?**
- A. Searching for new oil fields
- B. Pumping crude oil from underground
- C. Refining crude oil into kerosene
- D. Conducting seismic geological surveys

**Answer: C.** The downstream sector involves processing (refining) the crude oil and marketing the final products like kerosene and petrol. Options A, B, and D are upstream activities.

**5. The main objective of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) is to**
- A. explore for oil in member countries
- B. provide loans to developing nations
- C. stabilize global oil prices through production quotas
- D. build refineries in Africa

**Answer: C.** OPEC's primary role is to coordinate the petroleum policies of its members and use production quotas to stabilize oil prices on the international market.

**6. Which of the following statements best compares the oil and non-oil sectors in Nigeria?**
- A. The oil sector employs the majority of the workforce, while the non-oil sector provides the most foreign exchange.
- B. The oil sector provides the most foreign exchange, while the non-oil sector employs the majority of the workforce.
- C. The non-oil sector provides the majority of government revenue.
- D. The oil sector is spread evenly across all regions of the country.

**Answer: B.** The oil sector is highly capital-intensive and provides ~80-90% of forex, but employs very few people. The non-oil sector (agriculture, services) is labour-intensive and employs over 60% of the workforce.

**7. Gas flaring in the Niger Delta region primarily results in**
- A. an increase in the fertility of agricultural land
- B. the release of toxic gases and the formation of acid rain
- C. a massive increase in government revenue
- D. the rapid growth of aquatic life

**Answer: B.** Gas flaring burns off natural gas into the atmosphere, releasing toxic chemicals that pollute the air, cause global warming, and lead to acid rain which corrodes roofs and damages crops.

**8. Solid minerals were once the mainstay of the Nigerian economy. Tin and columbite were historically mined in large quantities in**
- A. Enugu
- B. Ilesha
- C. Jos
- D. Oloibiri

**Answer: C.** Tin and columbite were heavily mined on the Jos Plateau. Enugu (Option A) is famous for coal, Ilesha (Option B) for gold, and Oloibiri (Option D) for crude oil.

**9. In Nigeria, the government agency historically responsible for exploring, refining, and managing the state's interest in the petroleum sector is the**
- A. NOSDRA
- B. NESREA
- C. NNPC
- D. OPEC

**Answer: C.** The Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation (NNPC) was established in 1977 to manage the government's interests in the oil industry. NOSDRA and NESREA are environmental agencies.

**10. Which of the following is a suggested strategy to control the negative environmental effects of natural resource exploitation?**
- A. Encouraging more gas flaring to clear the air
- B. Banning the operation of local refineries entirely
- C. Enforcing strict environmental impact assessments and using bioremediation
- D. Withdrawing from the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries

**Answer: C.** Enforcing strict environmental laws and using scientific methods like bioremediation (using microorganisms to clean up spills) are effective ways to control environmental damage. Option A would worsen the problem.
