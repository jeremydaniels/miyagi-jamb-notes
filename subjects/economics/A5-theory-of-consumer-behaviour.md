## Basic Concepts

Every time you buy a bottle of Coke or a plate of rice, you do so because you expect it to give you some level of satisfaction. Economists use specific concepts to measure and analyse this behaviour.

### Various Utility Concepts

**Objective i: explain the various utility concepts.**

**Utility** is the amount of satisfaction or pleasure a consumer derives from consuming a particular good or service. It is the "want-satisfying power" of a commodity. Utility is subjective; the utility a student gets from a textbook is very different from the utility a farmer gets from the same book.

Economists approach utility in two different ways:
1. **Cardinal Utility:** This approach assumes that utility can be measured in exact numerical values called **utils**. For example, you might say a bottle of Coke gives you 50 utils of satisfaction, while a packet of biscuits gives you 20 utils. This theory is associated with the economist Alfred Marshall. 
2. **Ordinal Utility:** This approach argues that satisfaction cannot be measured in exact numbers. Instead, consumers can only **rank** their preferences. For example, you can say "I prefer Coke to biscuits", meaning Coke gives you higher utility, but you cannot say exactly how much more. This theory uses Indifference Curves and is associated with J.R. Hicks.

Under the cardinal approach, there are three main ways to look at utility:

*   **Total Utility (TU):** The total amount of satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming a specific quantity of a commodity. For example, the total satisfaction from eating 3 balls of akara.
*   **Average Utility (AU):** The total satisfaction divided by the number of units consumed. It is the satisfaction gained per unit. 
    $$AU = \frac{TU}{Q}$$
*   **Marginal Utility (MU):** The *additional* satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming *one extra unit* of a commodity. It is the change in total utility resulting from a one-unit change in quantity.
    $$MU = \frac{\Delta TU}{\Delta Q}$$
    *(Where $\Delta$ means "change in" and Q is quantity).*

**Schedule of Utility:**
Let us look at a student eating slices of yam:

| Quantity (Slices) (Q) | Total Utility (TU) (utils) | Marginal Utility (MU) (utils) | Average Utility (AU) (utils) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10.0 |
| 2 | 18 | 8 | 9.0 |
| 3 | 24 | 6 | 8.0 |
| 4 | 28 | 4 | 7.0 |
| 5 | 30 | 2 | 6.0 |
| 6 | 30 | 0 | 5.0 |
| 7 | 28 | -2 | 4.0 |

**The relationship between Total Utility (TU) and Marginal Utility (MU):**
*   As long as MU is **positive** (from 1 to 5 slices), TU is **increasing**.
*   When MU is **zero** (at the 6th slice), TU reaches its **maximum** point (30 utils). The consumer is fully satisfied (the point of satiety).
*   When MU becomes **negative** (at the 7th slice), TU begins to **decrease**. Eating more now brings discomfort (disutility).

### The Indifference Curve and Budget Line

Because measuring satisfaction in "utils" is unrealistic, modern economists prefer the ordinal approach using **indifference curves** and **budget lines**.

**1. The Indifference Curve**
An indifference curve is a graph showing all the different combinations of two commodities that yield the *exact same level of satisfaction* to the consumer. Because all combinations on the curve give the same satisfaction, the consumer is "indifferent" (does not mind) which combination they choose.

**Properties of an Indifference Curve:**
*   **It slopes downwards from left to right:** To keep satisfaction constant, if a consumer wants more of Good X, they must give up some of Good Y. 
*   **It is convex to the origin:** This is due to the **Diminishing Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS)**. As you get more and more of Good X, you are willing to give up less and less of Good Y to get even more of Good X.
*   **Indifference curves never intersect (cross) each other:** If they crossed, it would logically mean that one combination of goods gives two different levels of total satisfaction, which is impossible.
*   **A higher indifference curve represents a higher level of satisfaction:** Combinations on an indifference curve further to the right contain more goods, giving more satisfaction. A set of multiple indifference curves on one graph is called an **Indifference Map**.

The curves are labelled IC1, IC2, and IC3, with IC1 closest to the origin and IC3 furthest away. A small arrow points outwards from IC1 to IC3 labelled "Increasing utility". Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

**2. The Budget Line**
While an indifference curve shows what a consumer *wants* to buy, a **budget line** (or price line) shows what they can *afford* to buy. 
A budget line is a graph showing all the combinations of two goods that a consumer can purchase when they spend all their available income, given the prices of the goods.

The equation for the budget line is:
$$Income = (P_x \times Q_x) + (P_y \times Q_y)$$
Where $P_x$ and $P_y$ are the prices of Good X and Good Y, and $Q_x$ and $Q_y$ are the quantities bought.

If a student has ₦1000, and a pen (Good X) costs ₦100 while a pencil (Good Y) costs ₦50, the student could buy 10 pens and 0 pencils, or 20 pencils and 0 pens, or a mix like 5 pens and 10 pencils. Drawing a line between the maximum pens (10 on the horizontal axis) and maximum pencils (20 on the vertical axis) creates a straight, downward-sloping budget line.

## Diminishing Marginal Utility and the Law of Demand

**Objective ii: apply the law of demand using the marginal utility analysis.**

**The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility** states that as a consumer consumes more and more units of a specific commodity, the additional satisfaction (marginal utility) derived from each successive unit decreases. 
Think about drinking cold sachet water when you are extremely thirsty. The first sachet gives you massive satisfaction. The second gives you some satisfaction, but not as much as the first. By the time you reach the fourth sachet, you might not want it at all (zero or negative marginal utility).

**Applying this to the Law of Demand:**
The Law of Demand states that consumers will buy more of a good at a lower price and less at a higher price (the demand curve slopes downwards). Marginal utility perfectly explains *why* this happens.

*   A rational consumer will only buy a good if the price they have to pay ($P$) is equal to or less than the satisfaction they get from it ($MU$). Therefore, they buy units up to the point where **$P = MU$**.
*   Because of the law of diminishing marginal utility, the consumer gets less and less satisfaction from every extra unit they buy.
*   Since the extra satisfaction is falling, the consumer will only be willing to buy additional units if the **price also falls**.
*   This inverse relationship—requiring a lower price to encourage the purchase of extra, less-satisfying units—is exactly what creates the downward-sloping demand curve.

## Consumer Equilibrium

**Objective iii: use indifference curve and marginal analyses to determine consumer equilibrium.**

**Consumer equilibrium** is the point where a consumer achieves the maximum possible satisfaction from spending their limited income, and therefore has no reason to change their spending pattern. 

### Marginal Utility Analysis (The Cardinal Approach)

For a single commodity, equilibrium is simply when $MU_x = P_x$. However, consumers buy many goods. 

When a consumer must choose between two or more goods, they reach equilibrium using the **Equi-Marginal Principle** (also known as Gossen's Second Law). This law states that to maximise total utility, a consumer must distribute their income across different goods so that the *marginal utility per naira spent* is equal for all goods.

**The condition for equilibrium:**
$$\frac{MU_x}{P_x} = \frac{MU_y}{P_y}$$
*(Subject to the constraint that total expenditure must equal the consumer's income).*

**Example Calculation:**
Suppose a student has an income of **₦10**. The price of Gala (Good X) is **₦2** and the price of a local drink (Good Y) is **₦1**. The marginal utilities for the student are given below. Let us find the equilibrium combination.

*Step 1: Calculate $MU / P$ for both goods.*

| Qty | $MU_x$ (utils) | $\frac{MU_x}{P_x}$ (divide by ₦2) | $MU_y$ (utils) | $\frac{MU_y}{P_y}$ (divide by ₦1) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 24 | 12 | 11 | 11 |
| 2 | 20 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| 3 | 16 | **8** | 9 | 9 |
| 4 | 12 | 6 | 8 | **8** |
| 5 | 8 | 4 | 7 | 7 |

*Step 2: Find where $\frac{MU_x}{P_x} = \frac{MU_y}{P_y}$.*
Looking at the table, the ratio is equal at **8**. This happens when the student buys **3 units of Gala** (Good X) and **4 units of Drink** (Good Y).

*Step 3: Check if it fits the budget limit.*
Expenditure on Gala = 3 units $\times$ ₦2 = ₦6.
Expenditure on Drink = 4 units $\times$ ₦1 = ₦4.
Total expenditure = ₦6 + ₦4 = **₦10**.
This exactly matches the student's income of ₦10! Therefore, the consumer equilibrium is at 3 units of X and 4 units of Y.

### Indifference Curve Analysis (The Ordinal Approach)

Under the ordinal approach, consumer equilibrium is found by combining the consumer's Indifference Map (what they want) with their Budget Line (what they can afford).

*   The consumer wants to reach the highest possible indifference curve because it gives the most satisfaction.
*   However, they cannot go beyond their budget line.
*   **Equilibrium occurs at the point where the Budget Line is exactly tangent to (just touching) the highest possible Indifference Curve.**

At this point of tangency, the slope of the indifference curve (which is the Marginal Rate of Substitution, MRS) is exactly equal to the slope of the budget line (which is the ratio of their prices, $P_x / P_y$).

## Shifts in the Budget Line and Income and Substitution Effects

**Objective iv: relate the income and substitution effects.**

### Shifts and Pivots in the Budget Line
The budget line can change its position for two reasons:
1. **A change in income:** If a consumer's income increases (while prices stay the same), they can buy more of both goods. The budget line **shifts completely to the right** (parallel shift). If income falls, it shifts to the left.
2. **A change in price:** If the price of one good changes while income and the price of the other good stay the same, the budget line **pivots** (swings like a door). For example, if the price of Good X falls, the consumer can now buy more of Good X with the same money. The bottom end of the budget line swings out to the right along the horizontal axis, changing the slope of the line.

### Income and Substitution Effects
When the price of a commodity falls, consumers usually buy more of it. This total change in quantity demanded is called the **Price Effect**. The price effect is actually made up of two separate reactions occurring at the same time: the **Substitution Effect** and the **Income Effect**. 

**Total Price Effect = Substitution Effect + Income Effect**

1.  **The Substitution Effect:** When the price of Milo falls, Milo becomes *relatively cheaper* compared to Bournvita. Even if the consumer's purchasing power remained unchanged, they would rationally switch (substitute) away from Bournvita towards the cheaper Milo. The substitution effect *always* causes a consumer to buy more of the good whose price has fallen.
2.  **The Income Effect:** When the price of Milo falls, the consumer's **real income** (their purchasing power) increases. Even though their actual salary hasn't changed, their money can now buy more goods than before. 
    *   For a **normal good**, this increase in purchasing power makes them buy *more* of it.
    *   For an **inferior good**, an increase in purchasing power might make them buy *less* of it.

**Common Mistake:** Students often confuse "money income" with "real income". The income effect is driven by a change in *real income* (purchasing power caused by a price drop), not a change in the physical cash the consumer earns.

**Giffen Goods Note:** A Giffen good is a highly inferior good where the negative income effect is so strong that it outweighs the substitution effect. If the price of a Giffen good falls, the consumer ends up buying *less* of it! 

## Consumer Surplus and its Applications

**Objective v: apply consumer surplus to real life situations.**

**Consumer Surplus** is the difference between the maximum amount a consumer is willing to pay for a commodity and the actual market price they do pay. It represents the "extra benefit" or "saving" the consumer feels they got from a transaction.

$$Consumer\ Surplus = \text{Maximum Willingness to Pay} - \text{Actual Price Paid}$$

**Example Calculation:**
Imagine you are very hungry in the market and you want to buy roasted plantain (boli). 
*   You are so hungry that you would be willing to pay **₦1000** for the first one.
*   Because you are slightly less hungry after eating one, you would only pay **₦800** for a second one.
*   You would pay **₦600** for a third.
*   You would pay **₦500** for a fourth.

However, the seller has a fixed market price of **₦500** per plantain. You decide to buy 4 of them. Let's calculate your consumer surplus:
*   1st plantain: Willing to pay ₦1000, paid ₦500 $\rightarrow$ Surplus = **₦500**
*   2nd plantain: Willing to pay ₦800, paid ₦500 $\rightarrow$ Surplus = **₦300**
*   3rd plantain: Willing to pay ₦600, paid ₦500 $\rightarrow$ Surplus = **₦100**
*   4th plantain: Willing to pay ₦500, paid ₦500 $\rightarrow$ Surplus = **₦0**
*   **Total Consumer Surplus = ₦500 + ₦300 + ₦100 + ₦0 = ₦900.**

Graphically, consumer surplus is the triangular area **below the demand curve and above the price line**.

### Real-Life Applications of Consumer Surplus

1. **Pricing Strategies (Price Discrimination):** Monopolists (single sellers) often try to "capture" the consumer surplus. If an airline knows business travellers are willing to pay ₦200,000 for a ticket, while students are only willing to pay ₦80,000, the airline will charge different prices instead of one flat price. This turns the consumer's surplus into the seller's profit.
2. **Cost-Benefit Analysis for Public Projects:** Governments use consumer surplus to decide if building a new public good (like the Second Niger Bridge) is worth the cost. If the total consumer surplus (the massive value and time-saving citizens gain, beyond any tolls they pay) is higher than the construction cost, the project is economically justified.
3. **Evaluating Taxes and Subsidies:** If the government puts a heavy tax on a good, the price rises and the consumer surplus shrinks. Economists measure how much consumer surplus is lost to decide if a tax is too harmful to citizens.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **TU and MU Relationship:** This is a very common exam question. Memorise this rule: When TU is rising, MU is positive. When TU is maximum, MU is zero. When TU falls, MU is negative.
*   **Law of Demand:** Remember that the law of downward-sloping demand is directly derived from the Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility.
*   **Equilibrium condition:** For two goods, equilibrium is NOT when $MU_x = MU_y$. It is when their ratios to their prices are equal: $\frac{MU_x}{P_x} = \frac{MU_y}{P_y}$.
*   **Budget Line Shifts vs Pivots:** A change in income shifts the entire budget line parallel. A change in the price of just one good makes the budget line pivot (swing) on the axis of the good whose price changed.
*   **Consumer Surplus graphical area:** Always remember consumer surplus is the area *below* the demand curve but *above* the equilibrium price line. 
*   **Income vs Substitution Effect:** The substitution effect is always negative (price and quantity move in opposite directions). The income effect depends on whether the good is normal or inferior.

## Quick summary

1. Utility is the satisfaction derived from consuming a commodity. It can be viewed cardinally (measured in numbers) or ordinally (ranked in order of preference).
2. Total Utility (TU) increases until it reaches a maximum (point of satiety) where Marginal Utility (MU) is zero. Beyond that, TU falls and MU becomes negative.
3. The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility explains why the demand curve slopes downwards—because extra units give less satisfaction, consumers will only buy them at a lower price.
4. An Indifference Curve shows combinations of goods that yield equal satisfaction, while a Budget Line shows combinations a consumer can afford.
5. In the marginal approach, consumer equilibrium occurs where the marginal utility per naira spent is equal for all goods ($\frac{MU_x}{P_x} = \frac{MU_y}{P_y}$).
6. In the ordinal approach, consumer equilibrium occurs where the budget line is completely tangent to the highest possible indifference curve.
7. A fall in price causes an increase in quantity demanded due to a combination of the Substitution Effect and the Income Effect.
8. Consumer surplus is the difference between what a consumer is willing to pay and what they actually pay. It is used in pricing, taxes, and public project evaluations.

## Practice questions

**1. The point of satiety for a consumer is reached when:**
- A. Marginal utility is negative.
- B. Total utility is increasing.
- C. Marginal utility is zero.
- D. Total utility is zero.

**Answer: C.** When marginal utility is zero, it means the consumer gets no extra satisfaction from the last unit consumed, meaning their Total Utility has reached its maximum peak (the point of satiety). Option A describes disutility, not satiety.

**2. Which of the following best describes an indifference curve?**
- A. A curve showing the maximum output of two goods a firm can produce.
- B. A curve showing combinations of two goods that a consumer can afford.
- C. A curve showing combinations of two goods that give the consumer the same level of satisfaction.
- D. A curve showing the relationship between price and quantity supplied.

**Answer: C.** An indifference curve shows combinations of goods giving equal utility. Option A is a Production Possibility Curve, and Option B is a Budget Line. 

**3. If the price of Good X is ₦5 and the price of Good Y is ₦10, and a consumer is in equilibrium, which of the following must be true?**
- A. The marginal utility of X is twice the marginal utility of Y.
- B. The marginal utility of Y is twice the marginal utility of X.
- C. The total utility of X equals the total utility of Y.
- D. The marginal utility of X equals the marginal utility of Y.

**Answer: B.** At equilibrium, $\frac{MU_x}{P_x} = \frac{MU_y}{P_y}$. If $P_x = 5$ and $P_y = 10$, then $\frac{MU_x}{5} = \frac{MU_y}{10}$. By cross-multiplying, $10 \times MU_x = 5 \times MU_y$, which simplifies to $2 \times MU_x = MU_y$. Therefore, the marginal utility of Y must be twice that of X. Option D is the common mistake of ignoring prices.

**4. The Law of Demand is directly derived from the:**
- A. Law of variable proportions.
- B. Law of diminishing marginal utility.
- C. Equi-marginal principle.
- D. Indifference curve theory.

**Answer: B.** The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility states that successive units yield less satisfaction. Thus, consumers will only buy more if the price falls, creating a downward-sloping demand curve. 

**5. At the point of consumer equilibrium using indifference curve analysis, the budget line is:**
- A. Parallel to the indifference curve.
- B. Intersecting the indifference curve at two points.
- C. Tangent to the highest possible indifference curve.
- D. Above the indifference curve.

**Answer: C.** Equilibrium is achieved exactly where the budget line just touches (is tangent to) the highest reachable indifference curve. Option B represents points that are affordable but do not maximise utility.

**6. If a consumer's money income increases while the prices of goods remain the same, the budget line will:**
- A. Pivot outward on the horizontal axis.
- B. Shift completely to the right.
- C. Shift completely to the left.
- D. Pivot inward on the vertical axis.

**Answer: B.** An increase in income increases purchasing power for both goods equally, causing a parallel shift of the budget line to the right. Option A is the mistake made if only the price of one good falls.

**7. A consumer was willing to pay ₦3000 for a shirt but found it on sale for ₦1800. The difference of ₦1200 represents the:**
- A. Income effect.
- B. Producer surplus.
- C. Substitution effect.
- D. Consumer surplus.

**Answer: D.** Consumer surplus is the difference between the maximum willingness to pay (₦3000) and the actual price paid (₦1800). Option A relates to real income changes, not this specific gap.

**8. The income effect of a price fall describes how consumers respond to:**
- A. An increase in their physical salary.
- B. The good becoming relatively cheaper than other goods.
- C. An increase in their real purchasing power.
- D. A shift in their preferences.

**Answer: C.** The income effect occurs because a lower price means the consumer's existing money can buy more (real purchasing power increases). Option A is incorrect because their actual money income hasn't changed. Option B describes the substitution effect.

**9. Which of the following assumptions belongs to the ordinal utility theory rather than the cardinal utility theory?**
- A. Satisfaction can be measured in units called utils.
- B. Consumers can rank their preferences for different bundles of goods.
- C. The marginal utility of money remains constant.
- D. Total utility is the sum of marginal utilities.

**Answer: B.** Ordinal theory assumes utility cannot be measured numerically but can be ordered or ranked (1st, 2nd, 3rd choice). Option A is the core assumption of the cardinal utility theory.

**10. Total Utility is calculated as:**
- A. The sum of the marginal utilities of each unit consumed.
- B. The change in marginal utility divided by the change in quantity.
- C. Average utility multiplied by the price of the good.
- D. Marginal utility multiplied by quantity.

**Answer: A.** Total utility is the cumulative sum of the extra satisfaction (marginal utilities) gained from consuming each successive unit. Option D is a mathematical error, as marginal utility changes with each unit.
