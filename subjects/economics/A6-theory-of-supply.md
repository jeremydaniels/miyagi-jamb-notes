## Meaning and Determinants of Supply

**Content a(i): Meaning and determinants of supply**

In economics, **supply** is defined as the quantity of a commodity that a producer is **willing and able** to offer for sale at a given price, in a specific market, over a specific period of time. 

**Common mistake:** Do not confuse supply with **stock**. Stock is the total amount of a commodity that exists or has been produced (e.g., a Kano farmer harvests 1,000 bags of groundnuts). Supply is the portion of that stock the farmer is actually willing to bring to the market and sell at the current price (e.g., the farmer only sells 200 bags because the current market price is low, keeping the rest in a warehouse). 

### Objective i: Identify the factors determining supply

The quantity of a good that producers are willing to supply does not just happen by chance; it is influenced by several factors, known as the **determinants of supply**.

1.  **Price of the commodity itself:** This is the most important determinant. According to the law of supply, a higher price encourages producers to supply more of a good in order to maximize profit. 
2.  **Cost of production:** This includes the prices of inputs or factors of production (wages for labour, cost of raw materials, rent, and machinery). If the cost of fertilizer and tractors increases, a cassava farmer's cost of production rises, and they will supply less cassava at any given price.
3.  **Level of technology:** Improvements in technology make production faster, more efficient, and cheaper. For example, using mechanized harvesters instead of manual labour increases the supply of rice.
4.  **Weather and Climate:** This is highly relevant in Nigeria, where a large part of the economy depends on agriculture. Favourable weather (good rainfall) increases the supply of agricultural products like cocoa and yams. Bad weather (drought or flooding) drastically reduces supply.
5.  **Government policy (Taxes and Subsidies):** 
    *   **Taxes:** High taxes on production increase costs and reduce supply.
    *   **Subsidies:** A subsidy is financial help given by the government to producers. It lowers production costs and increases supply (e.g., fertilizer subsidies for farmers).
6.  **Prices of other related commodities:** If the price of maize suddenly shoots up, a farmer currently growing sorghum might switch to growing maize. The supply of sorghum will fall because of the increase in the price of maize.
7.  **Number of producers:** If more firms enter an industry (e.g., more pure water bottling companies open in Lagos), the total market supply of that product increases.

## Supply Schedules and Supply Curves

**Content a(ii): Supply schedules and supply curves**

To understand how supply behaves, economists use schedules (tables) and curves (graphs) based on a fundamental rule: **The Law of Supply**.

The **Law of Supply** states that, *all other factors being equal (ceteris paribus)*, the higher the price of a commodity, the higher the quantity supplied, and the lower the price, the lower the quantity supplied. Price and quantity supplied have a **direct (positive) relationship**.

### Objective ii: Interpret supply curves from supply schedules

A **supply schedule** is a table showing the different quantities of a commodity a producer is willing to supply at various prices over a given period.

**Table 1: Individual Supply Schedule for Bags of Rice in Onitsha Market**

| Price per bag (₦) | Quantity Supplied (Bags) |
| :--- | :--- |
| 10,000 | 20 |
| 15,000 | 40 |
| 20,000 | 60 |
| 25,000 | 80 |
| 30,000 | 100 |

*Interpretation:* As the price of a bag of rice increases from ₦10,000 to ₦30,000, the seller is motivated by higher profits to increase the quantity supplied from 20 bags to 100 bags.

A **supply curve** is the graphical representation of the supply schedule. Because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, the normal supply curve slopes **upward from left to right**.

## Change in Quantity Supplied vs. Change in Supply

**Content a(iii): The distinction between change in quantity supplied and change in supply**

This is one of the most frequently tested topics in JAMB. You must strictly separate what happens when the *price of the commodity itself* changes, versus what happens when *any other factor* changes.

### Objective iii: Differentiate between change in quantity supplied and change in supply

| Feature | Change in Quantity Supplied | Change in Supply |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | A change in the amount offered for sale due to a change in the price of the commodity itself. | A change in the amount offered for sale due to changes in *other* determinants (e.g., cost, weather), while the price of the commodity remains constant. |
| **Graphical Effect** | A **movement along** the same supply curve (upward or downward). | A **shift** of the entire supply curve to a new position (left or right). |
| **Causes** | Only a change in the **price of the commodity**. | Changes in technology, cost of production, weather, government policy, etc. |
| **Terminology** | An upward movement is an **expansion (or extension)** of supply. A downward movement is a **contraction** of supply. | A rightward shift is an **increase** in supply. A leftward shift is a **decrease** in supply. |

An arrow points from A to B along the curve, showing price increasing from P1 to P2 and quantity increasing from Q1 to Q2. Title: "Movement along the curve". Graph 2 (right) shows a "Change in Supply": Three separate upward-sloping supply curves labeled S1 (original), S2 (right shift), and S3 (left shift). A horizontal dashed line shows price remains constant at P1, while arrows point right from Q1 to Q2 (increase in supply) and left from Q1 to Q3 (decrease in supply). Title: "Shift of the curve". ## Types of Supply

**Content b: Types of Supply: Joint/complementary, competitive and composite**

Supply does not always occur in isolation. Sometimes, the production of one good directly affects the production of another. 

### Objective iv: Compare the various types of supply and their interrelationships

| Type of Supply | Meaning | Real-life Nigerian Examples | Relationship / Interrelationship |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Joint (Complementary) Supply** | This occurs when two or more commodities are produced together from the same single process or source. | 1. **Beef and Hides (leather):** A cow slaughtered in Kano yields both meat and leather.<br>2. **Palm oil and Palm kernel:** From the oil palm tree. | They have a **positive** relationship. An increase in the production (and supply) of beef automatically leads to an increase in the supply of leather. |
| **Competitive Supply** | This occurs when two or more alternative goods compete for the same limited resources (e.g., land or labour) to be produced. | 1. **Cassava vs. Maize:** A farmer with one plot of land must choose. Planting more cassava means less land for maize.<br>2. **Biofuel vs. Food:** Using corn for ethanol reduces the corn available for food. | They have a **negative (inverse)** relationship. An increase in the production of one good means a decrease in the supply of the competing good. |
| **Composite Supply** | This is the total supply of a particular commodity or service that comes from several different sources. | **Electricity supply in Nigeria:** Total megawatts supplied to the grid comes from hydro (Kainji dam), gas plants (Egbin), and solar. | The different sources combine to form the total market supply. If one source fails (e.g., gas pipelines are vandalized), the other sources must increase output, or total composite supply drops. |

## Elasticity of Supply

**Content c: Elasticity of Supply: determinants, measurements, nature and applications**

**Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)** measures the degree of responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a commodity to a change in its price. In simple terms, if the price of a good goes up, how quickly and by how much can producers increase their supply?

### Objective vi: Compute elasticity coefficients

The formula for Price Elasticity of Supply using the percentage method is:

$$PES = \frac{\% \text{ change in Quantity Supplied}}{\% \text{ change in Price}}$$

To break down the percentages:
*   $\% \text{ change in Quantity Supplied} = \frac{\text{New Quantity} - \text{Old Quantity}}{\text{Old Quantity}} \times 100$
*   $\% \text{ change in Price} = \frac{\text{New Price} - \text{Old Price}}{\text{Old Price}} \times 100$

**Step-by-Step Calculation Example:**
A pure water seller supplied 200 bags of water when the price was ₦100 per bag. When the price increased to ₦120 per bag, the seller increased the quantity supplied to 250 bags. Calculate the price elasticity of supply.

1.  **Identify the variables:** 
    *   Old Price ($P_1$) = ₦100, New Price ($P_2$) = ₦120
    *   Old Quantity ($Q_1$) = 200, New Quantity ($Q_2$) = 250
2.  **Calculate % change in Price:**
    *   Change in price = $120 - 100 = 20$
    *   $\% \text{ change in Price} = (\frac{20}{100}) \times 100 = 20\%$
3.  **Calculate % change in Quantity Supplied:**
    *   Change in quantity = $250 - 200 = 50$
    *   $\% \text{ change in Quantity Supplied} = (\frac{50}{200}) \times 100 = 25\%$
4.  **Calculate PES:**
    *   $PES = \frac{25\%}{20\%} = 1.25$

*The elasticity coefficient is 1.25.* (Since it is greater than 1, supply is elastic).

**Common mistake:** Students often flip the formula and divide the percentage change in price by the percentage change in quantity. Always remember: **Quantity is on top (Numerator), Price is on the bottom (Denominator).**

### Objective v: Relate the determinants to the nature of elasticity

The coefficient we just calculated determines the **nature of elasticity**:

1.  **Perfectly Inelastic Supply (PES = 0):** A change in price causes no change in quantity supplied. The supply curve is a vertical straight line. (e.g., a rare painting, or total land area in Lagos).
2.  **Inelastic Supply (PES < 1):** A large percentage change in price leads to a small percentage change in quantity supplied. The curve is steep. 
3.  **Unitary Elastic Supply (PES = 1):** The percentage change in price is exactly equal to the percentage change in quantity supplied. The curve passes through the origin.
4.  **Elastic Supply (PES > 1):** A small percentage change in price leads to a larger percentage change in quantity supplied. The curve is gently sloping (flatter).
5.  **Perfectly Elastic Supply (PES = $\infty$):** At a specific price, producers supply an infinite amount, but if price falls even slightly, supply drops to zero. The curve is horizontal.

**Determinants of Elasticity of Supply (Why are some goods elastic and others inelastic?)**

*   **Time:** This is the most crucial determinant. 
    *   *Short run:* Supply is **inelastic** because producers cannot quickly acquire new land, machinery, or factories to expand production.
    *   *Long run:* Supply becomes **elastic** because producers have enough time to adjust all factors of production.
*   **Nature of the product (production time):** Agricultural products (like tomatoes or yams) take time to grow, making their supply **inelastic** in the short run. Manufactured goods (like bottled water or cement) can be produced rapidly by running machines overnight, making their supply **elastic**.
*   **Availability of spare capacity:** If a factory is only running at 50% capacity, it can easily hire more workers and increase output if the price rises. Supply is highly **elastic**. If it is already at 100% full capacity, supply is **inelastic**.
*   **Mobility of factors of production:** If resources (labour and capital) can be easily shifted from producing one good to another, the supply of that good will be elastic.

### Objective vii: Interpret the coefficients in relation to real life situations

Understanding whether supply is elastic or inelastic helps in real-life decision-making for both governments and businesses.

*   **Application in Agriculture:** Because the PES for agricultural goods is often less than 1 (inelastic) in the short term, a sudden spike in the price of onions in Nigerian markets will not immediately lead to more onions appearing the next day. Farmers must plant and wait months for the next harvest. Government agricultural policies must therefore be planned years in advance.
*   **Application in Taxation (Tax Incidence):** If the government places an indirect tax on a product whose supply is very elastic (manufacturers can easily stop making it), the manufacturers will pass most of the tax burden onto consumers. If supply is inelastic, producers cannot easily escape the market and will bear more of the tax burden themselves.
*   **Application in Housing:** The supply of housing in major cities like Abuja or Port Harcourt is highly inelastic (PES < 1) in the short run because building houses takes a long time. Consequently, a sudden increase in demand for housing causes rents (prices) to skyrocket rather than causing a fast increase in housing supply.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Supply** | The quantity of a commodity producers are willing and able to offer for sale at a given price over a period of time. |
| **Law of Supply** | Ceteris paribus, the higher the price, the higher the quantity supplied, and vice versa. |
| **Change in Quantity Supplied** | A movement along the supply curve caused solely by a change in the price of the commodity. |
| **Change in Supply** | A shift of the entire supply curve left or right, caused by non-price factors (e.g., cost, technology). |
| **Joint Supply** | When two or more goods are produced simultaneously from a single source (e.g., beef and leather). |
| **Competitive Supply** | When two goods compete for the same factors of production; producing more of one means less of the other. |
| **Composite Supply** | The total supply of a commodity originating from different sources. |
| **Elasticity of Supply** | The degree of responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the product's price. |
| **Inelastic Supply** | When a change in price results in a proportionately smaller change in quantity supplied (PES < 1). |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Movement vs. Shift:** This is a classic JAMB trap. Remember: **Price** causes a **Movement** (Expansion/Contraction). **Other factors** (weather, tech, tax) cause a **Shift** (Increase/Decrease).
*   **Calculation trap:** When calculating elasticity, never mix up the formula. It is always $\% \Delta Q$ divided by $\% \Delta P$. A common wrong option in exams will give you the inverted answer. 
*   **Joint vs. Competitive:** Beef and leather = Joint supply. Cassava and maize on the same farmland = Competitive supply.
*   **Nature of agricultural goods:** Agricultural produce generally has an inelastic supply in the short run. Do not guess "elastic" just because prices fluctuate a lot. They fluctuate *because* supply cannot adjust quickly!

## Quick summary

1. Supply refers to the willingness and ability of a producer to sell specific quantities of a good at various prices, not the total stock available.
2. The Law of Supply states a direct (positive) relationship between price and quantity supplied, resulting in an upward-sloping supply curve.
3. Determinants of supply include the good's own price, costs of production, technology, weather, and government policies.
4. A change in the commodity's own price causes a movement along the supply curve (change in quantity supplied). Changes in other determinants cause the curve to shift entirely (change in supply).
5. Joint supply involves goods produced together (beef and leather); competitive supply involves goods competing for the same resources (cassava and maize); composite supply is total supply from various sources.
6. Price Elasticity of Supply (PES) measures responsiveness. PES = (% change in Qs) / (% change in Price).
7. Supply is elastic in the long run and for manufactured goods, but inelastic in the short run and for agricultural products.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following best defines supply in economics?**
- A. The total volume of goods produced in a country in a year.
- B. The total stock of a commodity kept in a warehouse by producers.
- C. The quantity of a commodity a producer is willing and able to offer for sale at a given price over a given period.
- D. The quantity of goods consumers are willing to buy at a specific price.

**Answer: C.** Supply is specifically the amount producers are both willing and able to bring to the market for sale at a given price over a specific period, differentiating it from total stock (B) or demand (D).

**2. Which of the following factors will cause a rightward shift of the supply curve for agricultural products?**
- A. An increase in the price of the agricultural products.
- B. A severe drought in the farming region.
- C. An increase in wages paid to farm labourers.
- D. The introduction of high-yielding, disease-resistant seeds.

**Answer: D.** A rightward shift represents an increase in supply caused by a non-price factor. Improved technology (better seeds) increases output. Option A causes a movement along the curve. Options B and C would cause a leftward shift (decrease in supply).

**3. The supply of meat and leather from cattle is an example of:**
- A. Competitive supply
- B. Composite supply
- C. Joint supply
- D. Elastic supply

**Answer: C.** Joint (or complementary) supply occurs when two or more goods are produced together from the same process or source, such as meat and leather from a cow.

**4. A farmer possesses a 10-hectare piece of land and must decide whether to plant yam or cassava. This situation best illustrates:**
- A. Joint supply
- B. Competitive supply
- C. Composite supply
- D. Inelastic supply

**Answer: B.** Competitive supply occurs when alternative goods compete for the same limited resources (land). Planting more yam means less land is available for cassava, creating an inverse relationship.

**5. The price of a bag of cement increases from ₦4,000 to ₦5,000, causing a manufacturer to increase the quantity supplied from 1,000 bags to 1,500 bags. What is the price elasticity of supply?**
- A. 0.5
- B. 2.0
- C. 1.5
- D. 0.2

**Answer: B.** Step 1: % change in Price = ((5,000 - 4,000) / 4,000) * 100 = (1,000 / 4,000) * 100 = 25%. 
Step 2: % change in Quantity = ((1,500 - 1,000) / 1,000) * 100 = (500 / 1,000) * 100 = 50%. 
Step 3: PES = 50% / 25% = 2.0. 
Option A (0.5) is the common mistake of dividing %P by %Q (25/50).

**6. If the price elasticity of supply for a commodity is 0.4, this indicates that:**
- A. A large percentage change in price leads to a small percentage change in quantity supplied.
- B. Supply is perfectly elastic.
- C. The commodity is likely a manufactured good in the long run.
- D. A small percentage change in price leads to a large percentage change in quantity supplied.

**Answer: A.** An elasticity coefficient of 0.4 is less than 1, meaning supply is inelastic. This means quantity supplied is relatively unresponsive to price changes (a large price change causes only a small quantity change). Option D describes elastic supply.

**7. An upward movement along a single supply curve is referred to as:**
- A. An increase in supply
- B. A decrease in supply
- C. An expansion in supply
- D. A contraction in supply

**Answer: C.** A movement upward along the supply curve caused by a price increase is called an expansion (or extension) of supply. "Increase" and "decrease" (Options A and B) refer to shifts of the entire curve.

**8. Which of the following is the most important determinant of the elasticity of supply?**
- A. The price of substitute goods
- B. The level of consumer income
- C. Time
- D. The size of the population

**Answer: C.** Time is the most critical determinant of supply elasticity. In the short run, it is difficult to adjust factors of production, making supply inelastic. In the long run, producers have time to adjust, making supply elastic. Options B and D are determinants of demand.

**9. The total supply of electricity in a country derived from hydroelectric dams, natural gas plants, and solar grids is an example of:**
- A. Competitive supply
- B. Composite supply
- C. Joint supply
- D. Substitute supply

**Answer: B.** Composite supply refers to the total market supply of a particular commodity or service (like electricity) that is generated from several different sources.

**10. Government subsidies paid to local rice farmers in Nigeria will result in:**
- A. A movement down the supply curve for local rice.
- B. A leftward shift of the supply curve for local rice.
- C. A rightward shift of the supply curve for local rice.
- D. A completely vertical supply curve for local rice.

**Answer: C.** Subsidies lower the cost of production for farmers, allowing them to supply more rice at every given price. This non-price factor causes an increase in supply, which is represented by a rightward shift of the entire supply curve.
