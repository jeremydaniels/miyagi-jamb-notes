## Meaning and types of production

**Production** is defined as the creation of utility (usefulness). It is any human activity that involves making goods or providing services that satisfy human wants. 

**Textbook variation / Common mistake:** In Economics, production is **not complete until the goods reach the final consumer**. This means that transporting and selling goods in a market (for example, moving yams from Benue State to sell in Mile 12 Market in Lagos) is just as much a part of "production" as farming the yams. Wholesalers and retailers create "place utility" and "time utility."

Production is broadly classified into three main types:

1. **Primary (Extractive) Production:** This involves extracting or harvesting raw materials directly from nature. 
   - *Nigerian examples:* Farming cassava and maize, crude oil drilling by NNPC in the Niger Delta, coal mining in Enugu, fishing in the Atlantic Ocean, and forestry. Primary production is the foundation of all other types of production because it provides the initial raw materials.
2. **Secondary Production:** This involves processing raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods. It adds "form utility" by changing the shape or chemical makeup of the raw materials. It is divided into:
   - **Manufacturing:** Turning raw cotton into textiles in Kaduna, processing cocoa beans into chocolate, or grinding limestone into cement at the Dangote Cement plant in Obajana.
   - **Construction:** Assembling manufactured materials to build houses, roads, bridges, and factories.
3. **Tertiary Production:** This involves the provision of services. It does not produce tangible goods but is essential for society and for the distribution of goods. It is divided into:
   - **Commercial Services (Indirect services):** Activities that help distribute goods from producers to consumers. This includes transportation, wholesale and retail trading, insurance, warehousing, and banking (e.g., Zenith Bank or First Bank).
   - **Professional / Personal Services (Direct services):** Services rendered directly to individuals. Examples include the work of teachers, doctors, lawyers, musicians, and barbers. The consumer benefits directly from the person's skills.

## Concepts of production and their interrelationships

To understand how a firm produces efficiently, we must understand three key concepts relating to output: Total Product, Average Product, and Marginal Product. 

- **Total Product (TP):** The total quantity of output produced by a firm using a given combination of inputs over a specific period.
- **Average Product (AP):** The output per unit of the variable input (usually labour). It tells us how much an average worker produces.
  $$AP = \frac{TP}{L}$$
  *(where $L$ is the number of units of labour)*
- **Marginal Product (MP):** The additional (extra) output produced as a result of employing one extra unit of the variable input. It measures the contribution of the last worker hired.
  $$MP = \frac{\Delta TP}{\Delta L}$$
  *(where $\Delta$ is the Greek letter Delta, meaning "change in")*

### Relating TP, AP and MP with the law of variable proportion

The short-run relationship between these three concepts is governed by a fundamental economic principle known as the **Law of Variable Proportions** (also called the Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns). 

**The Law states that:** As more and more units of a variable factor (such as labour) are added to a fixed factor (such as land or capital), the total product will initially increase at an increasing rate, but after a certain point, the extra output (marginal product) generated by each additional unit of the variable factor will begin to fall.

**Assumptions of the law:**
1. It operates in the **short run** (meaning at least one factor of production, like land or factory size, is fixed and cannot be changed).
2. All units of the variable factor are identical (homogeneous) in quality and efficiency.
3. The state of technology remains constant. If technology improves, the entire production curve would shift upward.

#### Production Schedule and the Three Stages of Production

Let us look at a farm with a fixed plot of land (1 hectare) where more labourers are hired one by one to plant and harvest cassava.

| Labour ($L$) | Total Product ($TP$) in kg | Marginal Product ($MP$) in kg | Average Product ($AP$) in kg | Stages of Production |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | --- |
| 0 | 0 | - | - | |
| 1 | 4 | 4 | 4.0 | **Stage 1: Increasing Returns** |
| 2 | 10 | 6 | 5.0 | *MP rises, peaks at $L=3$, then falls.* |
| 3 | 18 | 8 | 6.0 | *Stage ends where AP is at its maximum.* |
| 4 | 24 | 6 | 6.0 | *(At $L=4$, MP = AP)* |
| 5 | 28 | 4 | 5.6 | **Stage 2: Diminishing Returns** |
| 6 | 30 | 2 | 5.0 | *MP is falling but still positive.* |
| 7 | 30 | 0 | 4.3 | *TP reaches its absolute maximum where MP = 0.* |
| 8 | 28 | -2 | 3.5 | **Stage 3: Negative Returns** (*MP is negative, TP falls*) |

**Interrelationships:**
1. **Relationship between TP and MP:** 
   - As long as MP is positive, TP is increasing. 
   - When MP is rising, TP increases at an increasing rate.
   - When MP starts falling (but is still positive), TP increases at a decreasing rate.
   - When MP reaches zero (at $L = 7$), TP is at its absolute maximum (30 kg). 
   - When MP becomes negative (at $L = 8$), TP begins to fall. 
2. **Relationship between AP and MP:** 
   - The Marginal Product curve always "pulls" the Average Product curve in its direction.
   - When MP is greater than AP (e.g., at $L = 3$, $MP=8$, $AP=6$), AP must be rising.
   - When MP is less than AP (e.g., at $L = 5$, $MP=4$, $AP=5.6$), AP must be falling.
   - MP intersects AP exactly at AP's maximum point (at $L = 4$, $MP=6$, $AP=6$).

**The rational producer:** A sensible business owner will never produce in **Stage 1** (because adding workers rapidly increases average efficiency, so it makes sense to keep expanding). The producer will absolutely avoid **Stage 3** (because hiring more workers actually reduces total output—the farm is too crowded). Therefore, a rational producer always chooses to operate somewhere in **Stage 2** (the stage of diminishing returns).

## Division of labour and specialization

**Specialization** occurs when an individual, firm, region, or country concentrates on producing a limited range of goods or services in which it has an advantage. For example, Kano specializes in leather and groundnuts, while the Niger Delta specializes in crude oil.

**Division of labour** is a specific type of specialization. It is the breaking down of a production process into a series of smaller, distinct tasks, with each task performed by a different worker or group of workers. Instead of one person making an entire pair of shoes, one cuts the leather, another stitches it, and a third glues the soles.

### Advantages of division of labour
1. **Increase in productivity:** Output per worker increases significantly compared to when one person does everything.
2. **Time-saving:** Workers do not waste time switching tools or walking from one workstation to another.
3. **Development of greater skill:** "Practice makes perfect." Doing the same task repeatedly makes a worker extremely fast and expert at it.
4. **Encourages the use of machinery:** Breaking complex tasks down into simple, repetitive motions makes it much easier to invent and use machines to do the work.
5. **Less training time:** It takes only a few days to train someone to sew a button, whereas it takes years to train a master tailor to make a whole suit.

### Disadvantages of division of labour
1. **Monotony and boredom:** Doing the same tiny task all day becomes incredibly boring, which can reduce a worker's focus and lead to mistakes.
2. **Decline in craftsmanship:** Workers no longer feel the pride of creating a complete finished product from start to finish. The work becomes mechanical.
3. **Interdependence problems:** If the workers at one stage go on strike or a single machine breaks down, the entire factory comes to a halt because subsequent stages cannot proceed.
4. **Risk of unemployment (Immobility of labour):** A worker trained to perform only one highly specific task may struggle to find a new job if they are retrenched or if a machine replaces their specific task.

## Scale of production

In the long run, all factors of production are variable. This means a firm can increase its scale (overall size) by building larger factories and buying more land and machines. As a firm expands its scale, its **Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC)** of production usually falls initially. This cost advantage of large-scale production is called **Economies of Scale**. 

There are two main types:

1. **Internal economies of scale:** These are advantages that arise from the expansion of an *individual firm* itself, independent of what is happening to other firms in the industry. It is driven by the firm's own management decisions and growth.
2. **External economies of scale:** These are advantages that arise from the growth of the *entire industry* in a particular geographic area, which benefits all individual firms located there, regardless of whether a specific firm has grown or not.

### Compare internal and external economies of scale in production and their effects

| Feature | Internal Economies of Scale | External Economies of Scale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Cost savings that happen because a *single firm* grows larger in size. | Cost savings that happen because the *whole industry* grows larger in one area. |
| **Source of benefit** | Depends entirely on the actions, investments, and size of the firm itself. | Depends on the actions of the industry as a whole and government policies (e.g., clustering). |
| **Who benefits?** | Only the specific firm that has grown large enough to unlock the benefits. | All firms in the industry within that region, whether they are large or small. |
| **Effect on cost curves** | Causes a downward movement *along* the firm’s Long-Run Average Cost curve. | Causes the entire Long-Run Average Cost curve of the firm to *shift downwards*. |
| **Types / Examples** | - **Technical:** A large bakery can afford a huge automated industrial oven that bakes 10,000 loaves an hour, which a small bakery cannot afford.<br>- **Financial:** Large companies (like MTN or Dangote) can easily secure bank loans at much lower interest rates because they have huge assets as collateral.<br>- **Commercial (Marketing):** Buying raw materials in bulk at discount prices; affording national television advertising.<br>- **Managerial:** Hiring highly paid specialist managers (accountants, HR experts, engineers) instead of the owner doing everything.<br>- **Risk-bearing:** A large firm can diversify its products and markets so if one product fails, others survive.<br>- **Welfare:** Providing staff buses and clinics, which keeps workers healthy and productive. | - **Concentration (Localization):** The government may build better roads, power grids, and water supply because many firms are clustered in one industrial estate (e.g., Agbara Industrial Estate or Nnewi).<br>- **Information:** Firms can easily share research facilities, or industry magazines spring up to keep everyone informed of new technology.<br>- **Skilled Labour Pool:** A cluster of tech companies (e.g., in Yaba, Lagos) attracts a large pool of software developers to live in the area, making hiring cheaper and easier.<br>- **Disintegration (Specialized services):** As an industry grows, specialist support firms emerge (e.g., specialized transport companies for crude oil). |

**Diseconomies of Scale (The Implications of getting *too* large):**
If a firm or industry continues to expand endlessly, eventually, average costs will start to rise again. 
- **Internal diseconomies** happen when a firm becomes too massive to manage efficiently. Communication breaks down, bureaucracy slows down decision-making, and workers feel alienated.
- **External diseconomies** occur when an industry becomes too concentrated in one area. This leads to severe traffic congestion, pollution, and fierce competition for land and labour, which drives up rents and wages for all firms in the area.

## Production functions and returns to scale

### Identify the types of production functions

A **production function** is a mathematical or technical relationship showing the maximum output that can be produced from a given combination of inputs (factors of production) under a given state of technology.

It is generally written as:
$$Q = f(K, L, Ld, E)$$
Where $Q$ is Output; and $K$ (Capital), $L$ (Labour), $Ld$ (Land), $E$ (Entrepreneurship) are inputs.

In basic economic analysis, we simplify this to just two main inputs: Labour ($L$) and Capital ($K$): 
$$Q = f(K, L)$$

There are three key classifications of production functions you must identify for exams:

1. **Short-run production function:** In this period, at least one factor (usually capital or land) is fixed in quantity. It shows how output changes when only the variable factor (labour) is increased. It is the basis for the **Law of Variable Proportions**. Example equation: $Q = f(L, \bar{K})$ where the bar over $K$ means capital is fixed.
2. **Long-run production function:** In this period, all factors of production are variable. A firm can buy new land, build new factories, and hire new workers simultaneously. It shows how output changes when a firm increases all inputs at once. It is the basis for **Returns to Scale**. Example equation: $Q = f(L, K)$.
3. **Cobb-Douglas production function:** A famous and widely used mathematical form of a production function, commonly written as $Q = A K^\alpha L^\beta$. The symbol $A$ represents total factor productivity (technology). The exponents $\alpha$ (alpha) and $\beta$ (beta) show how output responds to changes in Capital and Labour. If $\alpha + \beta = 1$, the firm has constant returns to scale.

### Compare the different types of returns to the scale and their implications

**Returns to scale** describe what happens to total output in the long run when a firm increases **all its inputs by the exact same percentage**. 

If a firm doubles all its inputs (for example, a 100% increase in labour and a 100% increase in capital), what happens to output?

| Type of Returns to Scale | What happens to output? | Implication for Average Cost |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Increasing Returns to Scale (IRTS)** | Output increases by a *greater percentage* than the percentage increase in inputs. (e.g., inputs are doubled (100%), but output triples (200%)). | The firm is enjoying **internal economies of scale**. The Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC) curve is falling. |
| **Constant Returns to Scale (CRTS)** | Output increases by the *exact same percentage* as inputs. (e.g., inputs are doubled, and output exactly doubles). | The firm has exhausted economies of scale. The Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC) curve is constant (flat at the bottom). |
| **Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRTS)** | Output increases by a *smaller percentage* than the percentage increase in inputs. (e.g., inputs are doubled, but output only increases by 50%). | The firm is suffering from **internal diseconomies of scale**. The Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC) curve is rising. |

## Producers' equilibrium isoquant-isocost and marginal analyses

Just as consumers try to maximize their satisfaction using indifference curves and budget lines (covered in `A5-theory-of-consumer-behaviour.md`), producers try to either maximize output for a given cost budget, or minimize cost for a given target output. They achieve this using Isoquants and Isocost lines.

**1. The Isoquant (Equal Product Curve):**
An isoquant is a curve that shows all the possible combinations of two inputs (Labour and Capital) that produce the *exact same level of total output*.
- **Properties of Isoquants:** They slope downwards from left to right; they are convex to the origin; higher isoquants represent higher output levels; and two isoquants can never intersect.
- **Slope of the Isoquant:** The slope is called the **Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution ($MRTS_{LK}$)**. It tells us how much capital a firm can give up in exchange for one more unit of labour while keeping output exactly the same. Mathematically, it equals the ratio of their marginal products: 
  $$MRTS_{LK} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K}$$

**2. The Isocost Line (Equal Cost Line):**
An isocost line shows all the possible combinations of two inputs a firm can purchase with a specific total budget (cost outlay), given the prices of the inputs. 
- **Equation:** $C = wL + rK$
  *(where $C$ = Total Cost budget, $w$ = wage or price of labour, $r$ = rent or price of capital).*
- **Slope of the Isocost Line:** The slope is determined by the ratio of the factor prices: $\frac{w}{r}$ (which can also be written as $\frac{P_L}{P_K}$).

### Determine the firm's equilibrium position using the isoquant-isocost and marginal analyses

**The Geometric Analysis:**
A firm reaches its equilibrium (the most cost-effective way to produce) at the point where the highest possible Isoquant is exactly tangent to the Isocost line. At this tangency point, the slope of the Isoquant exactly equals the slope of the Isocost line.

[IMAGE: A graph showing Producer Equilibrium. The vertical axis is labelled "Capital (K)" and the horizontal axis is labelled "Labour (L)". A straight downward-sloping line, the Isocost line, is drawn between the two axes. A convex curve, the Isoquant (labelled IQ), touches the Isocost line at a single tangency point (labelled E). At point E, dashed lines drop to the axes to show the equilibrium quantities of Labour (L*) and Capital (K*). Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**The Marginal Analysis:**
Because the slopes are equal at the equilibrium tangency point, we can write:
$$MRTS_{LK} = \frac{w}{r}$$

Since we know that $MRTS_{LK} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K}$, we can substitute it into the equation:
$$\frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \frac{w}{r}$$

By cross-multiplying and rearranging this equation, we get the fundamental marginal condition for producer's equilibrium:
$$\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}$$

**What this formula means in simple English:** The firm is at equilibrium when the extra output (Marginal Product) generated by the *last naira spent* on labour is exactly equal to the extra output generated by the *last naira spent* on capital. If spending one more naira on labour yields more output than spending it on capital, the rational firm will hire more labour and buy less capital until the two ratios balance out.

## Factors affecting productivity

**Productivity** is a measure of efficiency. It is the ratio of total output to total inputs used in a given time period. For example, labour productivity is the total output produced per worker per hour.

### Identify the factors affecting productivity

Several key factors determine how productive workers and firms are in an economy:

1. **Quality of Labour (Human Capital):** A well-educated, well-trained, and healthy workforce is much more productive. This is why schools, vocational training centers, and hospitals are crucial to economic growth. A sick worker cannot perform efficiently.
2. **Level of Technology:** Using modern technology vastly increases productivity. A Nigerian farmer using a modern tractor and chemical fertilizers will produce far more bags of rice per hectare than a farmer using a traditional hand hoe.
3. **Availability of Capital (Machinery and Tools):** Even a highly skilled worker cannot be productive without the right tools. Giving factory workers modern computers, automated assembly lines, and reliable equipment raises their daily output.
4. **Working Conditions and Remuneration (Wages):** Workers who are paid well, given bonuses, and work in safe, well-lit, and comfortable environments are highly motivated and produce more. Poor wages and dangerous conditions lead to low morale, strikes, and low productivity.
5. **Efficiency of Management:** Good managers know how to organize resources, reduce waste, and place the right worker in the right job based on their strengths, boosting overall factory productivity.
6. **Government Policies and Infrastructure:** Reliable electricity, good road networks, and favourable government tax policies help firms run smoothly. Without stable electricity, manufacturing firms in Nigeria have to spend heavily on diesel generators, which slows down production and reduces efficiency.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mixing up Stages of Production:** Remember that Stage 1 ends where AP is at its maximum (where AP = MP). Stage 2 (the rational stage) ends where MP = 0 (and TP is at its absolute highest). Stage 3 begins when MP becomes negative.
- **Short-run vs. Long-run:** The Law of Variable Proportions is strictly a **short-run** concept (because one factor is fixed). Returns to Scale is strictly a **long-run** concept (because all factors are variable).
- **Internal vs. External Economies:** If an exam question asks about a firm buying a larger machine or hiring a better manager, it is an *internal* technical or managerial economy. If it asks about the government building a road to an industrial estate, it is an *external* economy.
- **Distribution is production:** Never forget that a trader selling goods in the market or a truck driver moving goods is engaged in production (tertiary commercial services) because they are adding "place utility" and "time utility".
- **Producer's Equilibrium Formula:** Memorize $\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}$. Exam questions frequently ask you to identify this exact formula among a list of wrong mathematical variations.

## Quick summary

1. Production is the creation of utility to satisfy human wants, and it is divided into primary (extraction), secondary (manufacturing/construction), and tertiary (services/distribution) stages.
2. The short-run Law of Variable Proportions states that adding more variable inputs (labour) to a fixed input (land) eventually causes the marginal product to decline (diminishing returns).
3. A rational producer always operates in Stage 2 of production, where Marginal Product is declining but still positive.
4. Division of labour increases productivity, saves time, and encourages the use of machinery, but it causes monotony and makes workers highly interdependent.
5. Economies of scale lower a firm's long-run average cost. Internal economies arise from the firm's own expansion, while external economies arise from the growth of the whole industry in a specific area.
6. Returns to scale happen in the long run: Increasing (output grows faster than inputs), Constant (output grows at the same rate), and Decreasing (output grows slower than inputs).
7. Producer equilibrium is reached at the point where the Isoquant curve is exactly tangent to the Isocost line, mathematically expressed as $\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}$.
8. Worker productivity is heavily influenced by their level of education, health, wages, and the technology and machinery they have access to.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following activities is NOT classified as tertiary production?**
- A. A doctor treating a patient in a clinic
- B. A teacher teaching in a secondary school
- C. A textile mill weaving cotton into fabric
- D. A commercial bank offering loans to businesses

**Answer: C.** Weaving cotton into fabric is secondary production (manufacturing). Options A, B, and D are all services, which fall under tertiary production.

**2. Based on the data: at 3 units of labour, total product is 18kg. At 4 units of labour, total product is 24kg. What is the marginal product of the 4th unit of labour?**
- A. 6kg
- B. 8kg
- C. 18kg
- D. 24kg

**Answer: A.** Marginal product is the change in total product divided by the change in labour. $MP = 24 - 18 = 6$kg. A common mistake is to calculate the average product ($24 / 4 = 6$kg), which coincidentally is the same number here, or to mix up the MP of the 3rd unit with the 4th unit.

**3. The rational producer will always choose to operate in which stage of production?**
- A. Stage 1, because marginal product is increasing
- B. Stage 2, because marginal product is declining but positive
- C. Stage 3, because total product is maximized
- D. Any stage, as long as fixed costs are covered

**Answer: B.** A rational producer produces in Stage 2. In Stage 1, adding workers increases average efficiency rapidly, so it makes sense to keep hiring and move into Stage 2. In Stage 3, extra workers reduce total output (MP is negative), which is irrational. 

**4. When a firm experiences a fall in its average costs due to its ability to buy raw materials in large quantities at a discount, it is enjoying:**
- A. External economies of scale
- B. Internal financial economies of scale
- C. Internal commercial economies of scale
- D. Increasing returns to scale

**Answer: C.** Buying in bulk and getting discounts is a commercial (or marketing) internal economy of scale. Option B is a misunderstanding, as financial economies refer specifically to securing bank loans at lower interest rates.

**5. In the long run, if a firm increases all its inputs by 50% and its total output increases by 80%, the firm is experiencing:**
- A. Increasing returns to scale
- B. Constant returns to scale
- C. Decreasing returns to scale
- D. Diminishing marginal returns

**Answer: A.** Because the percentage increase in output (80%) is greater than the percentage increase in inputs (50%), the firm has increasing returns to scale. Option D is incorrect because diminishing marginal returns is a short-run concept, while returns to scale is long-run.

**6. Which of the following is the correct mathematical condition for a firm's equilibrium using marginal analysis?**
- A. $\frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \frac{r}{w}$
- B. $\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}$
- C. $MP_L + w = MP_K + r$
- D. $\frac{w}{MP_L} = \frac{MP_K}{r}$

**Answer: B.** The condition is $\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}$, meaning the marginal product of the last naira spent on labour must exactly equal the marginal product of the last naira spent on capital. Option A flips the wage-rental ratio incorrectly.

**7. An isoquant is convex to the origin because of:**
- A. The diminishing marginal rate of technical substitution
- B. The increasing returns to scale
- C. The law of variable proportions
- D. Constant opportunity costs

**Answer: A.** As a firm substitutes more labour for capital, it takes increasingly larger amounts of labour to replace one unit of capital while keeping output constant. This diminishing marginal rate of technical substitution makes the curve bend inwards (convex).

**8. Which of the following is a major disadvantage of the division of labour?**
- A. It increases the time taken to produce goods
- B. It makes workers highly skilled in multiple areas
- C. It leads to monotony and boredom for the workers
- D. It discourages the invention of machines

**Answer: C.** Performing the same tiny, repetitive task every day leads to extreme boredom and monotony. Option A and D are incorrect because division of labour actually *saves* time and *encourages* the invention of machinery. 

**9. If the price of labour ($w$) is ₦5,000, the price of capital ($r$) is ₦10,000, and a firm's total budget ($C$) is ₦100,000, which of the following is the correct equation for the isocost line?**
- A. $10,000L + 5,000K = 100,000$
- B. $5,000L + 10,000K = 100,000$
- C. $100,000 = \frac{5,000}{L} + \frac{10,000}{K}$
- D. $5,000 + 10,000 = 100,000K$

**Answer: B.** The isocost equation is $C = wL + rK$. Substituting the given values: $100,000 = 5,000L + 10,000K$. Option A is a common mistake where the prices of labour and capital are swapped.

**10. Which of the following factors is most likely to increase the productivity of agricultural labour in Nigeria?**
- A. Employing more workers on a fixed piece of land
- B. Providing traditional hoes and cutlasses
- C. Providing access to modern tractors and high-yield seeds
- D. Increasing the working hours from 8 to 14 hours a day without extra pay

**Answer: C.** Improving technology (tractors) and capital (high-yield seeds) directly increases a worker's output per hour. Option A leads to diminishing returns. Option D would reduce morale and productivity due to exhaustion and poor working conditions.
