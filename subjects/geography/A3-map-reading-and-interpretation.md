## Relief Representation on Topographical Maps

**Objective ii: interpret physical and human features from topographical maps.**

**Relief** refers to the physical shape, height and unevenness of the earth's surface. A **topographical map** is a large-scale, detailed map that shows both natural (physical) and man-made (human) features. 

### Methods of showing relief

1. **Contours:** These are continuous lines drawn on a map joining places of equal height above sea level. They are the most common and accurate way to show relief because they reveal both the exact height and the shape of the land (landforms). The height difference between two successive contour lines is the **Contour Interval (C.I.)**.
2. **Spot heights:** A dot on the map with a number beside it (e.g., • 450), showing the exact height of that specific spot in metres.
3. **Trigonometrical stations:** Fixed surveying points usually found on hilltops. They are marked by a small triangle with the height beside it (e.g., $\Delta$ 500).
4. **Bench marks:** Heights carved into permanent man-made structures like bridges, walls or buildings by surveyors. They are marked with the letters "BM" and the height (e.g., BM 120).
5. **Form lines:** Broken lines used to show approximate heights in areas that have not been properly surveyed.
6. **Hachures:** Short lines drawn pointing down slopes. They are drawn thicker and closer together on steep slopes, and thinner on gentle slopes. They show the steepness but do not give exact heights.

### Common landforms shown by contours

You must be able to recognise standard landforms by looking at the pattern of contour lines.

- **Conical hill:** The contours are concentric (circles inside circles) and are spaced fairly evenly. The highest point is in the centre. (Example: small volcanic hills).
- **Plateau (Tableland):** An extensive area of high ground with a flat top and steep sides. The map shows closely packed contours on the edges (the steep sides) and an empty space in the middle with no contours (the flat top). (Example: Jos Plateau).
- **Ridge:** A long, narrow stretch of high ground. The contours are drawn out in long, oval shapes.
- **Escarpment (or Cuesta):** A ridge that is asymmetrical (uneven). One side has a very steep slope called the **scarp slope** (contours are very close together), while the other side has a gentle slope called the **dip slope** (contours are widely spaced). (Example: Udi Hills in Enugu State). *Textbook variation: some textbooks distinguish between an escarpment and a cuesta based on the exact angle of the slope, but for JAMB, they share the same contour pattern.*
- **Col, Saddle and Pass:** A **col** is a low dip or gap between two peaks on a ridge. A **saddle** is similar to a col but wider. A **pass** is a very deep gap cutting through a mountain range, often used for roads or railways.
- **Gorge:** A very deep, narrow river valley with almost vertical sides. The contours are extremely close together, sometimes touching.

### The Valley vs Spur rule

This is the most common trap in exam questions. Both look like V-shapes or U-shapes on a map, but they are opposite features.

| Feature | What it is | Contour Shape | Direction the "V" points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Valley** | Low land between hills, often containing a river. | V-shaped or U-shaped | Towards **higher** contour values (uphill). |
| **Spur** | A piece of high land jutting out from a hill into lower ground. | V-shaped or U-shaped | Towards **lower** contour values (downhill). |

## Drawing Cross Profiles

**Objective i: illustrate the relief of an area through profile drawing.**

A **cross profile** (or cross-section) is a side-view drawing of the land between two points on a map. It shows exactly what the hills and valleys would look like if you sliced the earth in half along a straight line.

### Steps to draw a cross profile

1. Draw a straight line in pencil connecting the two points (e.g., A and B) on the map.
2. Take a straight-edged strip of paper and place it along the line A–B.
3. Mark points A and B on the edge of the paper.
4. Make a small tick mark on the paper everywhere a contour line crosses the edge, and write down the height of that contour.
5. On a piece of graph paper, draw a horizontal line exactly the same length as A–B.
6. Look at the lowest and highest contour values you marked. Choose a suitable vertical scale (for example, 1 cm represents 50 m) and draw a vertical axis for height.
7. Place the strip of paper along the horizontal axis. For every tick mark, move straight up to the matching height on the vertical axis and make a dot.
8. Join all the dots together with a smooth, freehand curve (do not use a ruler) to show the shape of the land.

[IMAGE: A cross-profile drawing process. Top: A topographical map with a line A-B crossing a hill. A straight-edged strip of paper is laid along A-B, with tick marks where contours cross it. Bottom: A graph with a horizontal axis A-B and a vertical axis showing height in metres. The tick marks from the paper are projected upwards to their corresponding heights, and a smooth curve joins them to show a hill profile. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### Vertical Exaggeration (V.E.)

Because the earth's features are very wide compared to their height, if we drew a cross profile using the exact same scale for both height and distance, the hills would look like tiny, flat bumps. To make the landforms visible, we deliberately stretch the height by using a larger scale for the vertical axis. The number of times the height has been stretched is called the **Vertical Exaggeration**.

To calculate it, use the denominators (the bottom numbers) of the scales.

$$ \text{V.E.} = \frac{\text{Denominator of Horizontal Scale}}{\text{Denominator of Vertical Scale}} $$

**Worked Example:**
On a topographical map, the horizontal scale is 1:50,000. You are drawing a cross profile and choose a vertical scale of 1 cm to 100 m. Calculate the Vertical Exaggeration.

**Step 1:** Identify the horizontal scale denominator.
The horizontal scale is 1:50,000.
The denominator is **50,000**.

**Step 2:** Convert the vertical scale into a ratio to find its denominator.
The scale is 1 cm to 100 m.
Convert metres to centimetres (multiply by 100): 100 m = 10,000 cm.
The vertical ratio is 1:10,000.
The denominator is **10,000**.

**Step 3:** Apply the formula.
$$ \text{V.E.} = \frac{50,000}{10,000} = 5 $$
**Answer:** The Vertical Exaggeration is **5 times**.

## Recognition of Intervisibility

**Intervisibility** simply means whether one point on the map can be seen from another point by someone standing on the ground. 

To determine intervisibility on a map, draw a straight line (the **line of sight**) between the two points. The points are **not intervisible** if the line of sight is blocked by:
1. **An intervening hill or ridge:** If there is a landform between the two points that is higher than both of them, it blocks the view.
2. **A convex slope:** A convex slope bulges outward. On a map, its contours are closely spaced at the bottom and widely spaced at the top. If one person stands at the top of a convex slope and another at the bottom, they cannot see each other because the bulge of the hill gets in the way.

The points **are intervisible** if they are on a **concave slope**. A concave slope dips inward like a bowl (contours are widely spaced at the bottom and closely spaced at the top), leaving a clear line of sight between the top and bottom.

## Interpreting Physical Features

**Objective ii: interpret physical and human features from topographical maps.**

When interpreting physical features on a map, look at relief (covered above), drainage, and vegetation.

### Drainage (Water features)
Rivers and streams are usually shown in blue. You must be able to identify **drainage patterns** (the layout of the river network):
- **Dendritic pattern:** The river and its tributaries look like the branches of a tree. This is the most common pattern, occurring on rocks of uniform resistance (e.g., the River Niger system).
- **Trellis pattern:** Tributaries join the main river at roughly right angles (90°), creating a rectangular shape. This happens where hard and soft rocks alternate in parallel bands.
- **Radial pattern:** Rivers flow outwards in all directions from a central highland (like a conical hill or dome), resembling the spokes of a bicycle wheel.
- **Centripetal pattern:** Rivers flow inwards from all directions towards a central depression, such as a lake or basin (e.g., rivers flowing into Lake Chad).

### Vegetation
Natural vegetation is usually shown by green shading or specific symbols (like tiny trees, grass tufts, or marsh symbols). If an area has no vegetation symbols, it is usually cultivated farmland or built-up land.

## Interpreting Human Features

**Objective ii: interpret physical and human features from topographical maps.**

### Settlements
Settlements (towns, villages, camps) are usually shown as small black or red squares. You must recognise **settlement patterns**:
- **Nucleated (Clustered):** Buildings are packed closely together. This pattern forms at route centres (crossroads), near markets, or around a central water source. Most large towns and cities are nucleated.
- **Linear:** Buildings are arranged in a long line along a transport route (a road, railway, or river). 
- **Dispersed (Scattered):** Buildings are separated by large distances. This is common in farming areas with large individual farms, or in rugged, mountainous areas where flat building space is rare.

### Transport and Communication
Look for roads (often red or brown lines, varying in thickness for highways vs footpaths), railways (black lines with cross-marks), and airports. Where a road or railway crosses a river, a **bridge** is built. If the river is wide and there is no bridge, there may be a ferry crossing.

### Land Use
You can guess the economic activities of the area from map evidence:
- **Agriculture:** Presence of scattered farms, plantation symbols, or large unwooded lowland areas.
- **Lumbering:** Settlements near large forests with tracks leading into the woods.
- **Mining:** Symbols for quarries, mines, or spoil heaps.
- **Fishing:** Settlements located along coastlines, large rivers, or lakes.

## Relationships Between Physical and Human Features

Exam questions often ask you to explain *why* a human feature is located where it is, based on the physical landscape.

### Relief and Settlement
- **Avoidance of steep slopes:** Settlements are rarely built where contours are very close together (steep slopes) because it is difficult to build houses, construct roads, and farm.
- **Preference for lowlands/gentle slopes:** Settlements concentrate on flat or gently sloping land where contours are wide apart.
- **Dry point settlements:** In a marshy, swampy, or flood-prone area, people build on slightly raised, dry ground to stay safe from floods. 

### Drainage and Settlement
- People need water, so settlements often develop near rivers, lakes or springs (**wet point settlements**). However, they usually avoid the immediate flood plain of large rivers.

### Relief and Transport
- **Valleys and gaps:** Roads and railways are expensive to build on steep slopes. They prefer to follow the flat floors of river valleys. If they must cross a mountain range, they will go through a **pass** or **col**.
- **Following contours:** In hilly areas, a road or railway will often twist and turn, running parallel to the contour lines to avoid steep gradients, rather than crossing the contours at right angles.

### Drainage and Transport
- Rivers are obstacles to land transport. Where a road meets a river, a **bridge** is required. 
- Road networks are denser in well-drained lowlands than in marshy areas, where the soft ground makes road construction difficult.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Topographical map** | A detailed map showing natural features (relief, drainage) and human features (settlements, roads). |
| **Contour line** | A line joining places of equal height above sea level. |
| **Contour interval** | The difference in vertical height between two successive contour lines. |
| **Cross profile** | A side-view drawing of the relief along a specific line on a map. |
| **Vertical exaggeration** | The number of times the vertical scale has been enlarged compared to the horizontal scale to make landforms visible. |
| **Intervisibility** | The ability to see one point on the map from another without the view being blocked by relief. |
| **Spur** | A piece of high land projecting into lower ground; V-shaped contours pointing downhill. |
| **Valley** | A depression between hills; V-shaped contours pointing uphill. |
| **Nucleated settlement** | A settlement pattern where buildings are clustered tightly together. |
| **Dry point settlement** | A settlement built on raised, dry land in a generally wet or marshy area. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Spur vs Valley:** This is heavily tested. Look at the contour values. If the tip of the V points towards a smaller number, it's a spur (jutting down). If it points towards a bigger number, it's a valley (cutting up into the hill).
- **Convex vs Concave slopes:** Remember that a **convex** slope blocks intervisibility, while a **concave** slope allows it. Convex = contours close at the bottom.
- **Calculating Vertical Exaggeration:** Always convert your vertical scale to a ratio (cm to cm) before using the formula. A common mistake is dividing 50,000 by 100 instead of converting 100 m to 10,000 cm first.
- **Settlement patterns:** If the houses are strung along a road, it is a **linear** settlement. Do not confuse it with a dispersed settlement, which is randomly scattered over a large area.
- **Spot height vs Trigonometrical station:** A spot height is just a dot. A trigonometrical station is a triangle. Both give exact heights, but they look different on the map.

## Quick summary

1. Relief on topographical maps is shown using contours, spot heights, trigonometrical stations, bench marks, form lines, and hachures. 
2. A cross profile is a side-view section of the land, drawn using a strip of paper to transfer contour heights onto a graph.
3. Vertical Exaggeration (V.E.) is calculated by dividing the denominator of the horizontal scale by the denominator of the vertical scale.
4. Two points are not intervisible if separated by a higher ridge or if they lie on a convex slope (bulging outward). 
5. Drainage patterns include dendritic (tree-like), trellis (rectangular), radial (flowing outward from a peak), and centripetal (flowing inward to a basin).
6. Settlements can be nucleated (clustered), linear (along a route), or dispersed (scattered).
7. Human activities adapt to the physical landscape: settlements avoid steep slopes and swamps, while roads follow valleys and passes to avoid steep gradients.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following is the most accurate method used to show relief on modern topographical maps?**

- A. Hachures
- B. Form lines
- C. Contours
- D. Spot heights

**Answer: C.** Contours are continuous lines joining places of equal height, and they show both the exact height and the overall shape of the landforms accurately. Option A (hachures) only shows slope direction, and Option D (spot heights) only gives the height for single isolated points, not the overall shape.

**2. On a topographical map, the horizontal scale is 1:50,000 and the vertical scale is 1 cm to 100 m. What is the Vertical Exaggeration (V.E.)?**

- A. 2
- B. 5
- C. 10
- D. 50

**Answer: B.** The formula for V.E. is the denominator of the horizontal scale divided by the denominator of the vertical scale. The horizontal scale denominator is 50,000. For the vertical scale, 1 cm to 100 m is 1 cm to 10,000 cm (since 100 × 100 = 10,000), so its denominator is 10,000. V.E. = 50,000 ÷ 10,000 = 5. Option A comes from dividing 100 by 50. Option D comes from dividing 50,000 by 1,000 (forgetting to convert metres to centimetres).

**3. Two points at the top and bottom of a hill will NOT be intervisible if the slope between them is**

- A. concave
- B. uniform
- C. convex
- D. terraced

**Answer: C.** A convex slope bulges outward (contours are closer at the bottom and wider apart at the top), which physically blocks the line of sight between the top and bottom. Option A is incorrect because a concave slope dips inward like a bowl, allowing a clear view.

**4. A landform represented by V-shaped contours pointing towards lower heights is a**

- A. valley
- B. ridge
- C. spur
- D. gorge

**Answer: C.** A spur is a piece of high ground jutting out, so its V-shaped contours point downhill towards lower contour values. Option A (valley) is the most common mistake; however, a valley's V-shaped contours point uphill towards higher heights.

**5. A drainage pattern where rivers flow outwards in all directions from a central highland is called**

- A. dendritic
- B. trellis
- C. centripetal
- D. radial

**Answer: D.** Radial drainage flows outwards from a central dome or conical hill, like the spokes of a bicycle wheel. Option A (dendritic) looks like tree branches, while Option C (centripetal) is the exact opposite of radial, flowing inwards into a basin.

**6. On a topographical map, a major road that closely follows a river along the floor of a valley is most likely doing so to**

- A. cross the river frequently
- B. avoid the steep gradients of the surrounding hills
- C. act as a dam for the river
- D. prevent the settlement from expanding

**Answer: B.** Roads follow valleys to stay on relatively flat ground, avoiding the steep, difficult slopes of the hills (where contours are close together). Option A is incorrect because crossing a river requires expensive bridges, which road builders try to minimise.

**7. A triangle with a number beside it on a topographical map represents a**

- A. spot height
- B. bench mark
- C. trigonometrical station
- D. form line

**Answer: C.** A trigonometrical station is a fixed surveying point on high ground, shown as a small triangle. Option A (spot height) is just a dot with a number, while Option B (bench mark) is marked with "BM" and is typically found on man-made structures.

**8. Which of the following features consists of a steep slope on one side and a gentle slope on the other?**

- A. Plateau
- B. Escarpment
- C. Col
- D. Conical hill

**Answer: B.** An escarpment (or cuesta) has an asymmetrical profile: a steep scarp slope (contours very close together) and a gentle dip slope (contours far apart). Option A is wrong because a plateau has steep slopes on all sides with a flat top.

**9. A settlement located on slightly raised ground in the middle of a marshy or flood-prone area is known as a**

- A. wet point settlement
- B. dry point settlement
- C. nodal settlement
- D. linear settlement

**Answer: B.** A dry point settlement is built on a patch of elevated, dry land to avoid flooding and dampness in a wet environment (like a swamp). Option A (wet point) is the opposite: a settlement near a water source in an otherwise dry area.

**10. On a map, a large area with no contour lines in the middle, surrounded by closely packed contour lines on all sides, indicates a**

- A. deep valley
- B. conical hill
- C. plain
- D. plateau

**Answer: D.** A plateau (tableland) has a wide, flat top (indicated by the absence of contours in the centre) and steep sides (indicated by closely packed contours on the edges). Option C (plain) would have few contours everywhere, without the steep, closely packed edges.
