# JAMB Geography Notes: Interpretation of Statistical Data, Maps and Diagrams

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section I (Practical Geography), Topic D of the JAMB Geography syllabus: Interpretation of statistical data; maps and diagrams. 

The syllabus does not list specific printed content items for this topic, but it defines the scope as the computation of quantitative geographic information and the analysis and interpretation of statistical data, diagrams, and statistical maps.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
1. Compute quantitative information from statistical data, diagrams and maps.
2. Interpret statistical data, diagrams and maps.

Geographers deal with massive amounts of information about populations, climates, crop yields, and trade. To make sense of it all, this data must be summarised and presented visually. In the UTME, you will often be given a table of data, a graph, or a map, and asked to calculate values or interpret what the diagram shows.

## Computing Quantitative Information from Statistical Data

**Objective i: Compute quantitative information from statistical data, diagrams and maps.**

Statistical data is information collected in numerical form. Before drawing a map or a graph, geographers often need to summarise the raw data using statistics. 

### 1. Averages (Measures of Central Tendency)

An average gives a single central number that represents a whole set of data. 

**The Mean:** This is the most common average. You find it by adding all the values together and dividing by the total number of items.
**The Median:** This is the middle value when all the numbers are arranged in order of size. If there is an even number of values, the median is the mean of the two middle numbers.
**The Mode:** This is the value that appears most frequently in the data set.

**Worked Example:**
A weather station in Lokoja recorded the following monthly mean temperatures (°C) over a year:
Jan: 26, Feb: 28, Mar: 30, Apr: 30, May: 28, Jun: 26, Jul: 24, Aug: 24, Sep: 25, Oct: 27, Nov: 28, Dec: 28.

Calculate the mean, median, and mode for this temperature data.

**Finding the Mean:**
1. **Step 1:** State the formula: $\text{Mean} = \frac{\text{Sum of all values}}{\text{Number of values}}$
2. **Step 2:** Add all the temperatures together: $26 + 28 + 30 + 30 + 28 + 26 + 24 + 24 + 25 + 27 + 28 + 28 = 324^\circ\text{C}$
3. **Step 3:** Divide by the number of months (12): $324 \div 12 = 27^\circ\text{C}$
4. **Answer:** The mean annual temperature is $27^\circ\text{C}$.

**Finding the Median:**
1. **Step 1:** Arrange the 12 temperatures in ascending order (from smallest to largest): 24, 24, 25, 26, 26, 27, 28, 28, 28, 28, 30, 30.
2. **Step 2:** Identify the middle values. Since there are 12 items (an even number), the middle falls between the 6th and 7th values.
3. **Step 3:** The 6th value is 27 and the 7th value is 28. 
4. **Step 4:** Find the mean of these two middle numbers: $\frac{27 + 28}{2} = 27.5^\circ\text{C}$.
5. **Answer:** The median temperature is $27.5^\circ\text{C}$.

**Finding the Mode:**
1. **Step 1:** Look at the data to see which temperature occurs most often.
2. **Step 2:** 24 appears twice; 26 appears twice; 30 appears twice; 28 appears four times. 
3. **Answer:** The mode is $28^\circ\text{C}$.

### 2. Measures of Dispersion (Spread)

Geographers also need to know how spread out the data is. The simplest measure of spread is the **range**.

**The Range:** This is the difference between the highest value and the lowest value in a data set. In climate, the Annual Range of Temperature is a very common calculation.

**Finding the Range (using the Lokoja data above):**
1. **Step 1:** State the formula: $\text{Range} = \text{Maximum value} - \text{Minimum value}$.
2. **Step 2:** Identify the highest temperature in the year: $30^\circ\text{C}$ (March and April).
3. **Step 3:** Identify the lowest temperature in the year: $24^\circ\text{C}$ (July and August).
4. **Step 4:** Subtract the lowest from the highest: $30 - 24 = 6^\circ\text{C}$.
5. **Answer:** The annual temperature range is $6^\circ\text{C}$.

### 3. Densities and Rates

Geographers often convert raw population totals into densities or rates so that different regions can be compared fairly.

**Population Density:** The average number of people living in a given area (usually per square kilometre).
*   **Formula:** $\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Total Population}}{\text{Total Land Area}}$

**Worked Example:**
A hypothetical Nigerian state has a population of 5,000,000 people and a land area of 20,000 km². Calculate its population density.
1. **Step 1:** State the formula: $\frac{\text{Total Population}}{\text{Total Land Area}}$.
2. **Step 2:** Substitute the values: $\frac{5,000,000}{20,000}$.
3. **Step 3:** Cancel out the zeros: $\frac{500}{2}$.
4. **Step 4:** Divide: 250.
5. **Answer:** The population density is 250 persons per km².

> **Common mistake in computing quantitative data:** 
> When calculating a median with an even number of values, students often just pick one of the middle numbers. You *must* add the two middle numbers and divide by two. When calculating density, students sometimes divide the area by the population, which gives a meaningless tiny decimal. Always remember: people divided by space!

## Interpreting Statistical Diagrams

**Objective ii: Interpret statistical data, diagrams and maps.**

Statistical diagrams (or charts) are graphical ways to present data. You must know how to read them, compare them, and calculate values from them.

### 1. Line Graphs
Line graphs connect data points with a continuous line. They are best used to show **trends over time** (how a variable changes from month to month or year to year).
*   **Simple line graph:** Shows one variable (e.g., temperature over 12 months).
*   **Multiple line graph:** Shows two or more variables on the same axes to compare them (e.g., temperature of Lagos and Kano on the same graph).

When interpreting a line graph, the independent variable (like time or months) is placed on the horizontal **X-axis**, and the dependent variable (like temperature) is placed on the vertical **Y-axis**.

### 2. Bar Graphs
Bar graphs use rectangular bars to show data. The length or height of the bar is proportional to the value it represents. They are excellent for comparing discrete quantities (like rainfall per month, or crop production of different states).

*   **Simple bar graph:** Shows a single variable using standalone bars.
*   **Multiple (Grouped) bar graph:** Shows two or more variables grouped together for each category (e.g., bars for cocoa and rubber production placed side-by-side for each state).
*   **Divided (Compound) bar graph:** A single bar represents the total value, but it is divided into shaded segments to show the sub-components that make up the total.
*   **Divergent bar graph:** Shows values spreading out in two opposite directions from a central zero line (e.g., a population pyramid showing males on the left and females on the right, or trade surpluses and deficits).

**Comparison: Line Graph vs Bar Graph**

| Feature | Line Graph | Bar Graph |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Best used for** | Showing continuous changes and trends over time. | Comparing totals or absolute values between different categories. |
| **Common climatic use** | Representing temperature (because temperature is continuous). | Representing rainfall (because rain is collected as a monthly total). |
| **Visual representation** | A single continuous line connecting plotted dots. | Distinct, separate rectangular columns. |

### 3. Pie Charts (Divided Circles)
A pie chart is a circle divided into sectors (slices), where the angle of each sector is proportional to the value it represents. It is used to show percentages or how a total amount is divided up. The whole circle represents 360° or 100%.

**How to compute and draw a pie chart (Objective i):**

**Worked Example:**
In a given year, a region produced 1,000 tonnes of cocoa. Ondo State produced 400 tonnes, Osun produced 300 tonnes, Ekiti produced 200 tonnes, and Oyo produced 100 tonnes. Represent this on a pie chart.

1. **Step 1:** Write down the formula for finding the angle: $\text{Angle} = \left( \frac{\text{Value of component}}{\text{Total value}} \right) \times 360^\circ$
2. **Step 2:** Find the total value. (Here, the total is given as 1,000 tonnes. If it is not given, you must add all the components together first).
3. **Step 3:** Calculate the angle for Ondo: $\left(\frac{400}{1000}\right) \times 360^\circ = 0.4 \times 360^\circ = 144^\circ$.
4. **Step 4:** Calculate the angle for Osun: $\left(\frac{300}{1000}\right) \times 360^\circ = 0.3 \times 360^\circ = 108^\circ$.
5. **Step 5:** Calculate the angle for Ekiti: $\left(\frac{200}{1000}\right) \times 360^\circ = 0.2 \times 360^\circ = 72^\circ$.
6. **Step 6:** Calculate the angle for Oyo: $\left(\frac{100}{1000}\right) \times 360^\circ = 0.1 \times 360^\circ = 36^\circ$.
7. **Step 7:** Always check that your calculated angles add up to 360°: $144^\circ + 108^\circ + 72^\circ + 36^\circ = 360^\circ$. You would then use a protractor to draw these angles inside a circle.

To convert a value into a percentage instead of an angle:
*   $\text{Percentage} = \left( \frac{\text{Value}}{\text{Total}} \right) \times 100\%$. For Ondo: $\left(\frac{400}{1000}\right) \times 100\% = 40\%$.

### 4. Wind Roses
A wind rose is a diagram used to show the direction and frequency of winds over a specific period (usually a month or a year) at a specific weather station. 

*   **Structure:** It consists of a central octagon. Radiating from this octagon are eight bars representing the main compass directions (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW).
*   **Interpretation:** The number written inside the central octagon represents the number of **calm days** (days with no wind). The length of each radiating bar shows the number of days the wind blew *from* that direction.
*   **Compound wind roses:** These have bars with different shadings or varying thicknesses to show both the direction, the frequency, and the *speed* of the wind.

[IMAGE: A simple wind rose diagram for a month. A central octagon with the number '3' inside it representing 3 calm days. Eight bars radiate out for N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW. The longest bar is pointing from the South-West, representing 12 days. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### 5. Scatter Graphs (Scattergrams)
Scatter graphs are used to show the relationship (or correlation) between two sets of variables. The points are plotted as dots on the X and Y axes, but they are not joined by lines.

*   **Positive correlation:** As one variable increases, the other also increases. The dots form a pattern sloping upwards to the right (e.g., as annual rainfall increases, crop yield increases).
*   **Negative correlation:** As one variable increases, the other decreases. The dots slope downwards to the right (e.g., as distance from a city centre increases, the cost of land decreases).
*   **No correlation:** The dots are scattered completely randomly, meaning there is no relationship between the two variables.

A **line of best fit** is often drawn through the middle of the scattered dots to highlight the general trend.

[IMAGE: Scatter graph showing a positive correlation. X-axis labelled 'Annual Rainfall (mm)', Y-axis labelled 'Crop Yield (tonnes)'. Many small dots scattered but forming a clear upward-sloping trend from bottom-left to top-right. A line of best fit is drawn through the center of the dots. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

> **Common mistake in diagram interpretation:** 
> Forgetting to add all the data categories together to find the "Total" before calculating pie chart angles. If you divide a component value by another component value instead of the total, your angles will be completely wrong.

## Interpreting Statistical Maps

Statistical maps (also called quantitative thematic maps) represent numerical data spatially across a geographic area. They show *where* something happens and *how much* of it is there.

### 1. Dot Maps
A dot map uses identically sized dots to represent a specific quantity of a variable (like people, cattle, or crops). 

*   **How it works:** The geographer chooses a scale (e.g., 1 dot represents 50,000 cattle). The dots are then placed on the map exactly where the cattle are located. Areas with many dots clustered together have a high concentration; areas with few dots have a low concentration.
*   **Advantages:** It shows absolute totals accurately and gives a good visual impression of actual distribution patterns (dots can be placed precisely on towns or fertile plains, avoiding deserts or lakes).
*   **Disadvantages:** In very dense areas, the dots merge into a solid block, making them impossible to count.

**How to compute for a Dot Map (Objective i):**

**Worked Example:**
You are drawing a dot map of cattle distribution in Northern Nigeria. Kano state has 1,200,000 cattle. Your chosen scale is: 1 dot represents 50,000 cattle. How many dots will you draw in Kano state?
1. **Step 1:** State the formula: $\text{Number of dots} = \frac{\text{Total value}}{\text{Unit value of one dot}}$.
2. **Step 2:** Substitute the values: $\text{Number of dots} = \frac{1,200,000}{50,000}$.
3. **Step 3:** Simplify by crossing out four zeros on top and bottom: $\frac{120}{5}$.
4. **Step 4:** Divide: $120 \div 5 = 24$.
5. **Answer:** You will draw 24 dots in Kano state.

### 2. Choropleth Maps (Shading Maps)
A choropleth map uses different shades of colour, or different patterns of shading (like lines and crosses), to show average values or densities within specific boundaries (like states or local government areas).

*   **How it works:** The map is divided into administrative regions. Data (usually a derived rate like population density or yield per hectare) is calculated for each region. The regions are then shaded, with **darker shades representing higher values** and lighter shades representing lower values.
*   **Advantages:** Excellent for showing derived values like densities, percentages, and rates across different administrative zones.
*   **Disadvantages:** It falsely suggests that the distribution is completely uniform across a single state. It also creates a false impression that the value changes abruptly exactly at the state border.

**Comparison: Dot Map vs Choropleth Map**

| Feature | Dot Map | Choropleth Map |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Best used for** | Absolute numbers (totals), e.g., total number of people, cows, or metric tonnes of wheat. | Derived values (averages, densities, rates), e.g., persons per km², or percentage of literate people. |
| **Visual technique** | Identical dots placed to show precise locations. | Progressive shading within fixed administrative boundaries. |
| **Showing distribution** | Shows actual distribution patterns accurately (dots can avoid empty areas like lakes). | Assumes uniform distribution across the whole shaded state, hiding empty areas. |

### 3. Isoline Maps (Isopleth Maps)
An isoline is a line drawn on a map joining places that have the **same numerical value**. Isoline maps are excellent for showing a gradual change over space. To interpret them, you must interpolate (estimate) values between the lines. If lines are close together, the value changes rapidly (a steep gradient). If they are far apart, the change is gradual.

Important isolines you must know for JAMB:
*   **Isotherms:** Join places with equal temperature.
*   **Isohyets:** Join places with equal rainfall.
*   **Isobars:** Join places with equal atmospheric pressure.
*   **Contours (Isobaths):** Join places with equal height above sea level (isobaths for depth below sea level).
*   **Isohels:** Join places with equal sunshine duration.
*   **Isoneph:** Join places with equal cloud cover.
*   **Isohalines:** Join places with equal salinity in the ocean.

[IMAGE: An isoline map showing isotherms over a landmass. Wavy lines cross the map labelled 24°C, 26°C, 28°C, and 30°C. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### 4. Flow Line Maps
Flow lines are used to show the **movement** of people, goods, or traffic between different places.
*   **Interpretation:** The direction of the arrow shows the direction of movement. The **thickness (or width) of the line** is drawn proportional to the volume of movement (e.g., a thick line shows heavy traffic, a thin line shows light traffic). Flow lines are commonly used to show migration, ocean currents, and trade routes.

### 5. Proportional Symbol Maps
Instead of placing multiple dots, this map places a single large symbol (usually a circle, square, or sphere) over a location.
*   **Interpretation:** The area or volume of the symbol is mathematically drawn proportional to the total value it represents. For example, a map of Nigeria might have a very large circle over Lagos, a medium circle over Kano, and a smaller circle over Lokoja, representing their respective total populations.

> **Common mistake in statistical maps:** 
> Never use a choropleth map to show absolute totals (like total population). If a massive state has 5 million people and a tiny state has 5 million people, they would have the same dark shading for total population, yet the tiny state is severely overcrowded while the massive state is practically empty. This is why choropleth maps must *only* be used for densities and averages.

| **Median** | The middle value in a set of data when arranged in order of size. |
| **Mode** | The most frequently occurring value in a data set. |
| **Range** | The difference between the highest and lowest values in a data set. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Pie chart angles require the total:** If you are given raw values and asked to find the angle, always sum up the values first to get the denominator. Formula: $(Value \div Total) \times 360^\circ$.
*   **Match the tool to the data:** Line graphs are for continuous data (temperature, time trends). Bar graphs are for discrete totals (monthly rainfall, crop output). 
*   **Choropleth vs. Dot:** Remember that Choropleth = Densities/Averages (shading administrative zones). Dot maps = Totals/Absolute numbers (placing exact distributions).
*   **Wind Roses central number:** The number inside the central octagon is the number of **calm days** (days with no wind), not the total number of days in the month.
*   **Know your "Isos":** JAMB frequently asks you to identify isolines. Memorise that isobars = pressure, isohyets = rainfall, and isohels = sunshine.
*   **Correlation on Scatter Graphs:** Upward sloping trend = positive correlation. Downward sloping trend = negative correlation. Random dots = zero correlation.

## Quick summary

1. Statistical data is summarised using measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode), dispersion (range), and density (population divided by land area).
2. Line graphs show continuous trends over time (like temperature), while bar graphs compare discrete quantities (like monthly rainfall totals).
3. Pie charts display data as proportions of a 360° circle, calculated using the formula: $(\text{Value} \div \text{Total}) \times 360^\circ$.
4. Wind roses indicate wind direction and frequency with a central octagon for calm days, whereas scatter graphs show the correlation between two variables.
5. Dot maps use identically valued dots to show total numbers and exact geographic distributions of variables like crops or population.
6. Choropleth maps use progressive shading for derived rates (like density) within boundaries, isolines join places of equal value, and flow lines show movement direction and volume.

## Practice questions

**1. A station recorded the following monthly rainfall figures (mm) over five months: 22, 24, 26, 28, 30. Calculate the mean rainfall.**

- A. 24 mm
- B. 25 mm
- C. 26 mm
- D. 28 mm

**Answer: C.** 
1. **Step 1:** Sum the values: $22 + 24 + 26 + 28 + 30 = 130$.
2. **Step 2:** Divide by the number of months (5): $130 \div 5 = 26$ mm.

**2. Which of the following statistical mapping techniques is most suitable for representing population density across different states?**

- A. Dot map
- B. Flow line map
- C. Isoline map
- D. Choropleth map

**Answer: D.** Choropleth maps use shading to represent derived values like densities or averages within administrative boundaries. Dot maps are used for absolute totals, not densities.

**3. In a pie chart, if a sector representing 45 students is drawn out of a total population of 180 students, what will be the angle of the sector?**

- A. 45°
- B. 90°
- C. 180°
- D. 360°

**Answer: B.**
1. **Step 1:** Use the formula: $\text{Angle} = (\frac{\text{Value}}{\text{Total}}) \times 360^\circ$.
2. **Step 2:** Substitute the values: $(\frac{45}{180}) \times 360^\circ$.
3. **Step 3:** Simplify: $\frac{45}{180} = \frac{1}{4}$.
4. **Step 4:** Calculate: $\frac{1}{4} \times 360^\circ = 90^\circ$.
A common mistake is simply taking the raw value (45) and thinking it is the degree, leading to option A.

**4. A state has a total land area of 20,000 km² and a population of 5,000,000. What is its population density?**

- A. 25 persons per km²
- B. 40 persons per km²
- C. 250 persons per km²
- D. 400 persons per km²

**Answer: C.**
1. **Step 1:** Formula for density = Total Population ÷ Total Area.
2. **Step 2:** $5,000,000 \div 20,000$.
3. **Step 3:** Cross out four zeros from both sides: $500 \div 2 = 250$ persons per km².
Dividing incorrectly by mixing up the divisor and dividend, or miscounting zeros, leads to the wrong options.

**5. Which lines are drawn on a map to join places having the same amount of rainfall?**

- A. Isobars
- B. Isotherms
- C. Isohyets
- D. Isohels

**Answer: C.** Isohyets are lines joining places with equal rainfall. Isobars are for atmospheric pressure, isotherms for temperature, and isohels for sunshine.

**6. On a scatter graph, if the dots form a pattern sloping downwards from top-left to bottom-right, it indicates:**

- A. Positive correlation
- B. Negative correlation
- C. Zero correlation
- D. Perfect distribution

**Answer: B.** A downward-sloping pattern means that as one variable increases, the other variable decreases, which is a negative correlation. An upward slope indicates positive correlation.

**7. In a wind rose diagram, what does the number placed inside the central octagon represent?**

- A. The number of days with strong winds
- B. The total number of days in the month
- C. The number of days with no wind (calm days)
- D. The maximum wind speed recorded

**Answer: C.** The number in the centre of a wind rose represents the calm days (days without wind). The radiating bars handle the days when the wind did blow.

**8. You are drawing a dot map to represent 250,000 cattle. If your scale is 1 dot represents 10,000 cattle, how many dots will you draw?**

- A. 2.5 dots
- B. 25 dots
- C. 250 dots
- D. 2,500 dots

**Answer: B.**
1. **Step 1:** Formula: Number of dots = Total value ÷ Unit value.
2. **Step 2:** $250,000 \div 10,000$.
3. **Step 3:** Cancel out four zeros: $25 \div 1 = 25$ dots.
Miscounting the zeros (e.g., dividing by 1,000 instead of 10,000) would lead to 250 dots (Option C).

**9. A single vertical bar that represents a total amount but is shaded in distinct segments to show the sub-components is called a:**

- A. Multiple bar graph
- B. Simple bar graph
- C. Divergent bar graph
- D. Compound bar graph

**Answer: D.** A compound (or divided) bar graph is a single bar stacked with segments representing sub-components that make up the total. Multiple bar graphs use separate bars placed side-by-side.

**10. When interpreting statistical diagrams for a tropical climate, which combination is traditionally used to show temperature and rainfall on the same graph?**

- A. Line graph for both rainfall and temperature
- B. Bar graph for temperature and line graph for rainfall
- C. Line graph for temperature and bar graph for rainfall
- D. Pie chart for rainfall and bar graph for temperature

**Answer: C.** Conventionally, temperature is plotted as a continuous line graph (since temperature fluctuates continuously), while rainfall is shown as a bar graph (since it is collected as a discrete monthly total).
