## Introduction to Elementary Surveying

**Surveying** is the scientific process of measuring distances, angles, and elevations on the earth's surface to produce an accurate map, plan, or boundary of an area. In secondary school geography, we focus on **elementary surveying**, which deals with relatively small, flat areas of land where the curvature of the earth is ignored (this is known as *plane surveying*).

The two primary techniques you must know for your JAMB exam are **chain surveying** (which measures only distances) and **prismatic compass surveying** (which measures both angles and distances).

## Chain Surveying

**Objective i: analyse the principle and procedure of each technique**

Chain surveying is the simplest and oldest form of surveying. It is used to map small, relatively flat areas of land (like a school compound or a small cassava farm) by measuring linear distances. No angles are measured with a compass or theodolite in this method.

### The Principle of Chain Surveying
The fundamental principle of chain surveying is **triangulation**. 
Because a triangle is the only simple polygon that cannot be deformed or changed in shape without changing the length of its sides, the area to be surveyed is divided into a framework of interconnected triangles. If the sides of these triangles are measured accurately on the ground, they can be plotted precisely on paper to create a map.

### Equipment Used in Chain Surveying

1. **The Chain:** A measuring instrument made of connected steel links. A standard metric chain is usually 20 m or 30 m long. Historically, *Gunter's chain* (22 yards long, with 100 links) was popular. Each link in a metric chain is typically 20 cm long. Brass tallies (tags) are attached at every metre to help read the distance.
2. **Measuring Tape:** Made of steel, fibreglass, or linen, used for measuring shorter distances or perpendicular offsets. Commonly 30 m or 50 m long.
3. **Ranging Poles:** Long wooden or metal poles (2 m to 3 m long), painted with alternating red and white bands for high visibility. They are used to mark survey stations and help measure straight lines over long distances.
4. **Arrows (Pins):** Steel pins about 40 cm long. They are pushed into the ground to mark the end of each chain length during measuring. A surveyor usually carries a set of 10 arrows.
5. **Cross-staff or Optical Square:** Instruments used to establish a perfect right angle (90°) from the main chain line to a specific object (an offset).
6. **Pegs:** Wooden stakes driven into the ground to permanently mark survey stations.
7. **Field Book:** A special notebook for recording measurements. Measurements are uniquely recorded **from the bottom of the page upward** to the top, representing the surveyor's forward movement.

### Procedure for Chain Surveying

Conducting a chain survey is a step-by-step process:

**Step 1: Reconnaissance (Preliminary Survey)**
The surveyor walks around the entire area to understand its layout, boundaries, and obstacles (like rivers, hills, or thick bushes). A rough sketch of the area is drawn.

**Step 2: Marking Stations and Ranging**
Major points are chosen to form the framework of triangles. Wooden pegs and ranging poles are placed at these stations. If the distance between two stations is longer than the chain, extra ranging poles are placed in a perfectly straight line between them. This process of establishing a straight line is called **ranging**.

**Step 3: Chaining (Measuring the Line)**
Two people are required to measure the distance:
- The **Leader** takes the front handle of the chain and 10 arrows, walking forward toward the ranging pole.
- The **Follower** holds the back handle at the starting station. 
- When the chain is pulled tight and straight, the Leader pushes one arrow into the ground at the end of the chain. 
- The Leader moves forward, dragging the chain, and the Follower walks to the newly placed arrow. 
- This continues until the end of the line. The distance is calculated by multiplying the number of arrows collected by the Follower by the length of the chain, plus any remaining links.

**Step 4: Offsetting**
To map details like trees, paths, or building corners, measurements are taken at 90° from the main chain line to the object. This is called taking an **offset**. A cross-staff is used to ensure the angle is exactly 90°, and a tape measures the distance.

**Step 5: Booking**
The Follower records all chain distances and offsets in the field book. A single line or double line is drawn up the middle of the page to represent the chain line. Offsets to the left of the chain on the ground are written on the left side of the page; offsets to the right are written on the right side. **Booking always starts from the bottom of the page and works upwards.**

### Problems in Chain Surveying

Surveyors often encounter obstacles in the field that block chaining or vision. 
- **Vision obscured, chaining free:** For example, a small hill blocking the view between two stations. Surveyors use a stepping method or intermediate ranging to overcome this.
- **Chaining obscured, vision free:** For example, a river or pond. The surveyor can see the pole on the other side but cannot walk a chain straight across the water. Geometrical methods (like constructing similar triangles on the riverbank) are used to calculate the distance across.
- **Both chaining and vision obscured:** For example, a large building in the way. Surveyors must construct rectangular or triangular detours around the building.
- **Sagging:** If the chain is pulled across a ditch or uneven ground, it may sag in the middle, causing the recorded distance to be longer than the true horizontal distance.

## Prismatic Compass Surveying

**Objective i: analyse the principle and procedure of each technique**

Prismatic compass surveying uses a magnetic compass to measure the direction (angle) of a line with respect to the Magnetic North, and a tape or chain to measure the distance. It is much faster for surveying large, wooded, or complex areas like town layouts.

### The Principle of Prismatic Compass Surveying
The fundamental principle of prismatic compass surveying is **traversing**. 
Traversing involves establishing a series of connected straight lines. At each station, the surveyor measures the length of the line (using a tape) and the angular direction of the line, known as the **bearing** (using a prismatic compass).

### Equipment Used in Compass Surveying

1. **Prismatic Compass:** A delicate instrument containing a magnetic needle attached to a circular compass card marked in degrees (0° to 360°). It has a glass prism that allows the surveyor to look through the sighting slit at a ranging pole and simultaneously read the bearing on the card below.
2. **Tripod Stand:** Used to mount the compass to keep it steady and level.
3. **Ranging Poles, Chain/Tape, and Arrows:** Used for marking stations and measuring the distances between them, just as in chain surveying.
4. **Field Book:** Used to record distances, Forward Bearings, and Back Bearings.

### Procedure for Compass Surveying

**Step 1: Reconnaissance and Station Fixing**
Just as in chain surveying, the surveyor walks the site, chooses stations, and marks them with pegs and ranging poles.

**Step 2: Centering and Levelling**
The compass is mounted on the tripod and placed exactly over the first station (Station A). This is called **centering**. The compass is then adjusted so the circular card floats freely and horizontally; this is called **levelling**.

**Step 3: Sighting and Reading Bearings**
- The surveyor at Station A looks through the compass prism at a ranging pole placed at Station B. 
- The magnetic bearing from A to B is read. This is called the **Forward Bearing (FB)**.
- To check for accuracy, the surveyor moves the compass to Station B, centers it, and looks back at Station A to read the bearing from B to A. This is called the **Back Bearing (BB)**.

**Step 4: Measuring Distance**
The distance between Station A and Station B is measured using a tape or chain.

**Step 5: Booking**
The station names, distances, forward bearings, and back bearings are recorded in a tabular format in the field book.

### Understanding Forward and Back Bearings

A bearing is an angle measured clockwise from Magnetic North. 
- **Forward Bearing (FB):** The bearing in the direction the survey is progressing (e.g., from A to B).
- **Back Bearing (BB):** The bearing in the reverse direction (e.g., from B back to A).

Because a straight line represents 180°, the difference between the Forward Bearing and the Back Bearing of any line must be exactly **180°**.

**Formula:**
- If Forward Bearing is **less** than 180°: $BB = FB + 180^\circ$
- If Forward Bearing is **greater** than 180°: $BB = FB - 180^\circ$

**Worked Example 1:**
If the Forward Bearing from Station A to B is 065°, what is the Back Bearing?
- Step 1: Check if FB is less or more than 180°. 65° is less than 180°.
- Step 2: Add 180°.
- Step 3: $65^\circ + 180^\circ = 245^\circ$.
- **Answer:** The Back Bearing is 245°.

**Worked Example 2:**
If the Forward Bearing from Station C to D is 285°, what is the Back Bearing?
- Step 1: Check if FB is less or more than 180°. 285° is greater than 180°.
- Step 2: Subtract 180°.
- Step 3: $285^\circ - 180^\circ = 105^\circ$.
- **Answer:** The Back Bearing is 105°.

### Problems in Prismatic Compass Surveying

1. **Local Attraction:** This is the most common error. The magnetic needle in the compass is easily distracted by nearby iron or steel objects (such as zinc roofs, vehicles, keys in the surveyor's pocket, steel electric poles, or iron ore deposits in the ground). If local attraction occurs, the difference between the FB and BB will not be exactly 180°.
2. **Magnetic Declination:** The compass points to *Magnetic North*, which is slightly different from the earth's true geographic North Pole (*True North*). The angle between them changes slowly over the years and varies depending on where you are in Nigeria or the world. Surveyors must adjust their calculations to account for this.

## Open and Closed Traverse

A traverse is a series of connected survey lines. There are two types:

### 1. Open Traverse (Unclosed Traverse)
An open traverse starts at one known point and finishes at a different, unknown point. It does not return to its starting station.
- **Uses:** It is used for surveying long, narrow strips of land, such as a new road (e.g., the Lagos-Ibadan expressway), a railway line, a pipeline, or the winding path of a river.
- **Problem:** Because it doesn't close back on itself, it is very difficult to check if a mistake was made during the survey.

### 2. Closed Traverse
A closed traverse starts at a known point and returns to that exact same starting point, forming a closed polygon shape (e.g., starting at A, going to B, C, D, and finally back to A).
- **Uses:** It is used for surveying bounded areas like a school compound, a lake, an estate, or a forest reserve.
- **Error of Closure:** When plotting a closed traverse on paper, the last line drawn should perfectly meet the starting point. If there were slight inaccuracies in reading the compass or measuring the tape, the lines will not meet. The small gap is called the **error of closure**. Surveyors correct this error on paper using mathematical rules (like *Bowditch's Rule*) or graphical adjustment methods.

## Comparing the Advantages and Disadvantages

**Objective ii: compare the advantages and disadvantages of the two techniques**

When choosing how to survey an area, geographers must weigh the pros and cons of chain surveying versus prismatic compass surveying.

### Comparison Table: Chain Surveying vs Prismatic Compass Surveying

| Feature | Chain Surveying | Prismatic Compass Surveying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Basic Principle** | Triangulation (forming triangles) | Traversing (measuring angles and lengths) |
| **Measurements taken** | Linear distances only (no angles) | Both angular bearings and linear distances |
| **Suitability** | Small, open, and level ground | Large, complex, or heavily wooded areas |
| **Speed** | Slow, as every line must be chained | Faster, as obstacles can be bypassed using angles |
| **Advantages** | 1. Very simple to understand and do.<br>2. Equipment is rugged, cheap, and easy to repair or replace.<br>3. No complex mathematics is needed.<br>4. Unaffected by magnetic objects. | 1. Excellent for dense forests where laying long straight chain lines is impossible.<br>2. Requires fewer stations.<br>3. A closed traverse allows for easy error checking (error of closure). |
| **Disadvantages** | 1. Not suitable for large or crowded areas.<br>2. Difficult in hilly or heavily wooded terrain.<br>3. Equipment (especially a 30m steel chain) can be very heavy.<br>4. Prone to errors if the chain sags or links get bent. | 1. Equipment (compass) is delicate, costly, and easily broken.<br>2. Highly prone to **local attraction** (magnetic interference).<br>3. Requires more skill to read bearings accurately.<br>4. Requires complex math to adjust closure errors. |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Surveying** | The measurement of distances, elevations, and angles on the earth to produce a map. |
| **Plane surveying** | Surveying of small areas where the earth's curvature is ignored. |
| **Triangulation** | The principle of chain surveying; dividing an area into triangles. |
| **Traversing** | The principle of compass surveying; measuring a series of connected lines and angles. |
| **Ranging pole** | A red and white striped pole used to mark stations and sight straight lines. |
| **Offset** | A lateral measurement taken at a right angle (90°) from the chain line to an object. |
| **Cross-staff** | An instrument used to set out right-angled offsets. |
| **Forward Bearing (FB)** | The compass angle of a line in the direction the survey is progressing. |
| **Back Bearing (BB)** | The compass angle of a survey line in the reverse direction. |
| **Local Attraction** | The deflection of a compass needle away from Magnetic North due to nearby metal objects. |
| **Error of closure** | The failure of a closed traverse to meet its starting point precisely when plotted on paper. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mixing up Principles:** Remember that Chain Surveying uses **Triangulation**, while Compass Surveying uses **Traversing**. Exam questions love to test this difference.
- **Booking Direction:** In chain surveying, the field book is ALWAYS written from the **bottom to the top**. This is a very common exam question.
- **Calculating Back Bearing:** Don't guess. If the Forward Bearing is under 180°, add 180°. If it is over 180°, subtract 180°.
- **Magnetic Declination vs Local Attraction:** Local attraction is caused by immediate metal objects (keys, cars, roofs) and only affects specific stations. Magnetic declination is a global/regional difference between True North and Magnetic North and affects the whole survey area.
- **Choosing the right traverse:** If a question asks which method is best for a river or a road, the answer is an **open traverse**. For an estate or school, it is a **closed traverse**.

## Quick summary

1. Elementary surveying maps small, flat areas of land. The two main types required for JAMB are chain surveying and prismatic compass surveying.
2. Chain surveying relies on the principle of **triangulation** and measures only distances using a chain or tape, arrows, ranging poles, and a cross-staff.
3. In chain surveying, measurements are recorded in a field book starting from the bottom of the page upwards. Offsets are right-angle measurements taken to locate details.
4. Prismatic compass surveying relies on **traversing** and measures both the direction (bearing) of lines and their distances.
5. The relationship between Forward Bearing (FB) and Back Bearing (BB) is exactly 180° ($BB = FB \pm 180^\circ$).
6. An **open traverse** is used for linear features like roads, while a **closed traverse** forms a polygon and is used for boundaries. A closed traverse allows errors to be checked.
7. Chain surveying is cheap and simple but slow and bad for forests. Compass surveying is faster in rough or wooded areas but suffers from **local attraction** (interference from metal objects).

## Practice questions

**1. The fundamental principle of chain surveying is based on**
- A. traversing
- B. levelling
- C. triangulation
- D. offsetting

**Answer: C.** The principle of chain surveying is triangulation, which involves dividing the surveyed area into a framework of rigid triangles.

**2. Which of the following is the most suitable method for surveying the winding path of the River Benue?**
- A. Closed traverse
- B. Open traverse
- C. Triangulation
- D. Levelling

**Answer: B.** An open traverse does not return to its starting point and is ideal for surveying long, narrow, and continuous features like rivers, roads, and railways.

**3. If the Forward Bearing of a survey line is 065°, what is its Back Bearing?**
- A. 115°
- B. 155°
- C. 245°
- D. 295°

**Answer: C.** Because the Forward Bearing (65°) is less than 180°, you add 180° to find the Back Bearing. $65^\circ + 180^\circ = 245^\circ$. (Option D comes from wrongly subtracting 65 from 360, while Option A comes from subtracting 65 from 180).

**4. In chain surveying, an offset is used to**
- A. measure the main baseline
- B. establish a straight line between two poles
- C. locate objects on either side of the chain line at right angles
- D. correct the sagging of the chain

**Answer: C.** Offsets are lateral measurements taken perpendicular (90°) to the main chain line to map details like trees and buildings.

**5. During a prismatic compass survey, the needle is deflected by a surveyor's iron wristwatch. This error is known as**
- A. error of closure
- B. local attraction
- C. magnetic declination
- D. compass sagging

**Answer: B.** Local attraction occurs when nearby iron or steel objects pull the magnetic needle away from Magnetic North, causing incorrect bearing readings.

**6. How is data booked in a chain survey field book?**
- A. From the left margin to the right margin
- B. From the top of the page downwards
- C. From the bottom of the page upwards
- D. Alphabetically according to station names

**Answer: C.** The field book is booked from the bottom of the page upwards to represent the surveyor's forward movement along the chain line.

**7. If the Forward Bearing of line XY is 285°, the Back Bearing is**
- A. 075°
- B. 105°
- C. 195°
- D. 465°

**Answer: B.** Because the Forward Bearing (285°) is greater than 180°, you subtract 180°. $285^\circ - 180^\circ = 105^\circ$. (Option A comes from wrongly subtracting 285 from 360).

**8. Which of the following is an advantage of prismatic compass surveying over chain surveying?**
- A. It is unaffected by magnetic objects
- B. It does not require any mathematical calculations
- C. It is easier to use in thick forests or wooded areas
- D. The equipment is very cheap and unbreakable

**Answer: C.** Compass surveying is better in wooded areas because it uses traversing (angles and lines), allowing surveyors to navigate around obstacles without needing to clear long straight paths for chaining.

**9. The failure of the final plotted line of a closed traverse to meet the starting point on paper is called**
- A. local attraction
- B. error of closure
- C. offset gap
- D. magnetic declination

**Answer: B.** Error of closure is the small gap left in a closed polygon on paper due to slight inaccuracies in field measurements.

**10. In chain surveying, the arrows are carried by the**
- A. follower at the back of the chain
- B. leader at the front of the chain
- C. recorder sitting at the station
- D. local guide

**Answer: B.** The leader carries the 10 arrows and pushes one into the ground at the end of every chain length. The follower picks them up to keep count of the distance.
