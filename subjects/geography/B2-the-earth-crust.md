## The structure of the Earth (internal and external)
**Objective i: compare the internal and external components of the earth.**

The Earth is made up of different layers. We divide these into the **internal structure** (what is inside the Earth) and the **external structure** (the outer layers where life and weather happen).

### Internal components of the Earth
The inside of the Earth consists of three main concentric layers (like the layers of an onion):

1. **The Core (Barysphere):** This is the innermost layer. It is very hot and dense, composed mainly of iron and nickel (NiFe). It is divided into two parts:
   - **Inner Core:** Solid, despite the extreme heat (up to 5,500 °C), because of the immense pressure from the layers above it.
   - **Outer Core:** Liquid molten rock and metal.
2. **The Mantle (Mesosphere):** The middle layer, about 2,900 km thick. It lies between the core and the crust. It consists of very hot, dense, semi-solid rocks (rich in iron and magnesium). The upper part of the mantle is called the **asthenosphere**, which is soft and plastic-like, allowing tectonic plates to slide over it.
3. **The Crust:** The outermost solid shell of the Earth. It is the thinnest layer (about 5 to 50 km thick). It is divided into two parts:
   - **Continental Crust (Sial):** The upper layer forming the continents. It is made mostly of granite rocks which are rich in **Si**lica and **Al**uminium (SIAL). It is lighter and thicker.
   - **Oceanic Crust (Sima):** The lower layer forming the ocean floors. It is made mostly of heavier basaltic rocks rich in **Si**lica and **Ma**gnesium (SIMA).

### External components of the Earth
The external part of the Earth is composed of the "spheres" that surround the solid body of the planet:
1. **Atmosphere:** The layer of gases (air) surrounding the Earth, containing nitrogen (78%), oxygen (21%), carbon (IV) oxide, and others. It protects the Earth from harmful solar radiation and contains weather systems.
2. **Hydrosphere:** All the water bodies on the Earth, including oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, groundwater, and ice caps. It covers about 71% of the Earth's surface.
3. **Biosphere:** The zone of the Earth where life exists. It includes all living organisms (plants, animals, microbes) and the environment they interact with.
4. **Lithosphere (surface view):** The very top solid surface of the crust where soils, landforms, and habitats exist.

### Comparing internal and external components

| Feature | Internal components (Core, Mantle, Crust) | External components (Atmosphere, Hydrosphere, Biosphere) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Location** | Beneath the Earth's surface | On or above the Earth's surface |
| **State of matter** | Mostly solid rock, plastic (semi-solid) rock, and liquid metal | Gases (air), liquids (water), and organic (living) matter |
| **Density** | Very high density; increases sharply towards the core | Very low density; decreases with altitude (for the atmosphere) |
| **Dominant materials** | Iron, nickel, silicates, magnesium, aluminium | Nitrogen, oxygen, water (H₂O), carbon compounds |
| **Energy source** | Driven by internal geothermal heat (radioactive decay) | Driven primarily by external solar energy (the Sun) |

## Relationships among the four spheres
**Objective ii: understand the existing relationship among atmosphere, biosphere and hydrosphere in terms of energy balance and water cycle.**

The four spheres—Lithosphere, Atmosphere, Hydrosphere, and Biosphere—are completely interconnected. Matter and energy flow constantly between them. The syllabus focuses on the energy balance and the water cycle as key examples of how the atmosphere, biosphere, and hydrosphere interact.

### Energy Balance
The **energy balance** is the relationship between the incoming energy from the Sun and the outgoing heat lost back into space. The spheres interact to maintain the Earth's temperature:
1. **Atmosphere:** It acts as a filter and a blanket. Incoming short-wave solar radiation passes through it. The atmosphere reflects some energy back to space and absorbs some. Gases like carbon (IV) oxide and water vapour trap outgoing long-wave heat from the Earth (the **greenhouse effect**), keeping the planet warm enough for life.
2. **Hydrosphere:** Water absorbs massive amounts of solar heat without its temperature rising too quickly (it has a high specific heat capacity). Ocean currents move this heat from the hot equator towards the cold poles, balancing global temperatures.
3. **Biosphere:** Plants absorb solar energy to produce food through **photosynthesis** (converting light energy into chemical energy). The biosphere also affects the Earth's **albedo** (how much sunlight the surface reflects). For example, dark, dense rainforests absorb a lot of sunlight, while bare, dry areas reflect more.

### The Water Cycle (Hydrological Cycle)
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between the hydrosphere, atmosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere.

**Step-by-step process:**
1. **Evaporation (Hydrosphere to Atmosphere):** Heat from the Sun causes liquid water in oceans, lakes, and rivers (hydrosphere) to turn into water vapour (gas) and rise into the atmosphere.
2. **Transpiration (Biosphere to Atmosphere):** Plants absorb water from the soil through their roots and release it as water vapour through their leaves into the atmosphere. (The combination of evaporation and transpiration is called **evapotranspiration**).
3. **Condensation (Atmosphere):** As the water vapour rises, it cools and changes back into tiny liquid droplets, forming clouds.
4. **Precipitation (Atmosphere to Lithosphere/Hydrosphere):** When the droplets get too heavy, they fall back to the Earth as rain, snow, or hail.
5. **Runoff and Infiltration (Lithosphere):** Rainwater flows over the ground (runoff) into rivers and eventually back to the oceans, or soaks into the ground (infiltration) to become groundwater. Plants (biosphere) then absorb this water, starting the cycle again.

## Rocks: Types, characteristics and formation
**Objective iii: differentiate between major types of rocks and their characteristics.**
**Objective iv: analyse the processes of rock formation and the resultant features.**

In geography, a **rock** is any naturally occurring solid mass of minerals that makes up the Earth's crust. It does not have to be hard; sand, clay, and gravel are geologically considered rocks. There are three major types of rocks based on how they form: **Igneous, Sedimentary, and Metamorphic**.

### 1. Igneous Rocks
**Formation process:**
1. Deep inside the Earth, temperatures are high enough to melt rock, forming a hot liquid called **magma**.
2. If this magma rises but fails to reach the surface, it cools and solidifies slowly deep inside the crust. This forms **intrusive (or plutonic) igneous rocks**. Because they cool slowly, large crystals have time to form (e.g., **granite**).
3. If the magma erupts onto the Earth's surface through a volcano, it is called **lava**. It cools and solidifies very quickly in the air or under water. This forms **extrusive (or volcanic) igneous rocks**. Because they cool quickly, they have very small crystals or are glassy (e.g., **basalt**, pumice).

**Characteristics of Igneous Rocks:**
- They are **crystalline** (made of interlocking crystals).
- They do **not** contain fossils (the extreme heat destroys any plant or animal remains).
- They are **non-stratified** (they do not occur in horizontal layers).
- They are generally very hard and resistant to erosion.

**Resultant features:**
- **Intrusive features:** Batholiths (massive deep rock bodies), dykes (magma that cuts vertically across rock layers), sills (magma that spreads horizontally between layers).
- **Extrusive features:** Volcanic cones, lava plateaux (e.g., the Biu Plateau in Nigeria, formed when very fluid lava spreads over a large area).

### 2. Sedimentary Rocks
**Formation process:**
Sedimentary rocks are formed from the accumulation and compaction of sediments (fragments of other rocks, plant/animal remains, or chemical precipitates) over a long time. The process is **lithification**.
1. **Weathering and Erosion:** Existing rocks are broken down into small particles (sand, clay, pebbles) by wind, water, or ice.
2. **Transportation and Deposition:** Rivers, glaciers, or wind carry these particles and deposit them in oceans, lakes, or deserts in flat layers called **strata**.
3. **Compaction and Cementation:** Over millions of years, the weight of new layers squeezes the lower layers. Minerals in the water act like cement, binding the particles into solid rock.

**Types of Sedimentary Rocks:**
- **Mechanically formed:** Formed from broken fragments of other rocks (e.g., **sandstone** from sand, **shale** from clay).
- **Organically formed:** Formed from the remains of dead plants and animals. Examples: **limestone** (from shells and corals), **coal** (from ancient forests).
- **Chemically formed:** Formed when dissolved minerals precipitate (fall out of solution) as water evaporates (e.g., **rock salt**, gypsum).

**Characteristics of Sedimentary Rocks:**
- They are **stratified** (arranged in layers or strata) separated by bedding planes.
- They often contain **fossils** (remains of ancient living things trapped between layers).
- They are **non-crystalline**.
- They are generally softer than igneous rocks.

### 3. Metamorphic Rocks
**Formation process:**
Metamorphic rocks are formed when pre-existing rocks (igneous, sedimentary, or even older metamorphic rocks) are changed into new rocks by intense heat, extreme pressure, or both, without melting completely.
1. **Thermal (Contact) Metamorphism:** Occurs when hot magma forces its way into rocks. The intense heat "bakes" the surrounding rocks, changing their minerals.
2. **Dynamic Metamorphism:** Occurs due to intense pressure caused by tectonic movements, such as when mountains are pushed up.
3. **Regional Metamorphism:** A combination of high heat and pressure over a large area, usually deep in the crust.

**Examples of rock changes:**
- Clay or Shale (sedimentary) changes to **Slate**.
- Limestone (sedimentary) changes to **Marble**.
- Sandstone (sedimentary) changes to **Quartzite**.
- Granite (igneous) changes to **Gneiss**.
- Coal (sedimentary) changes to **Graphite**.

**Characteristics of Metamorphic Rocks:**
- They are usually very hard and compact.
- They are often **foliated** or **banded** (minerals are squeezed into parallel lines or distinct bands, like in gneiss).
- Fossils are usually destroyed or heavily distorted.
- They contain recrystallized minerals.

### Comparison of major rock types

| Feature | Igneous Rocks | Sedimentary Rocks | Metamorphic Rocks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Origin** | Cooling and solidification of magma or lava | Compaction and cementation of sediments | Alteration of existing rocks by heat and pressure |
| **Structure** | Crystalline | Non-crystalline | Recrystallized |
| **Layers (Strata)** | Non-stratified | Stratified (in distinct layers) | Foliated or banded, but not truly stratified |
| **Fossils** | None (heat destroys them) | Present (preserves ancient life) | Rarely present (usually destroyed by heat/pressure) |
| **Examples** | Granite, basalt, gabbro, pumice | Sandstone, limestone, shale, coal | Marble, slate, gneiss, quartzite |

## Uses of rocks
**Objective v: indicate the uses of rocks.**

Rocks are of immense economic value in Nigeria and worldwide:
1. **Construction:** Hard igneous and metamorphic rocks (like granite and marble) are quarried for building roads, bridges, and houses. Sedimentary rocks like limestone are the primary raw material for making **cement** (e.g., at Ewekoro in Ogun State, Obajana in Kogi State).
2. **Minerals and Mining:** Rocks contain valuable mineral ores. The granitic rocks of the Jos Plateau contain tin and columbite. Gold is mined from metamorphic rocks in Ilesha.
3. **Sources of Fuel (Energy):** Sedimentary rocks hold all the world's fossil fuels. **Petroleum (crude oil) and natural gas** are found in sedimentary basins like the Niger Delta. **Coal**, a combustible sedimentary rock, is mined in Enugu.
4. **Soil Formation:** All soils are formed from the weathering of parent rocks. The mineral content of the soil depends on the rock it came from (e.g., volcanic rocks often produce very fertile soils).
5. **Tourism and Recreation:** Spectacular rock formations attract tourists and bring in revenue. Examples include the Olumo Rock in Abeokuta, Zuma Rock in Niger State, and the Idanre Hills in Ondo State.
6. **Domestic Uses:** In many rural areas, flat or rounded stones (usually hard igneous rocks) are used as traditional grinding stones for pepper or grains.

## Earth's movement: Tectonic forces
**Objective vi: differentiate between tensional and compressional forces and the resultant landforms.**

The Earth's crust is not a single solid shell; it is broken into massive moving pieces called **tectonic plates**. The forces that move these plates come from within the Earth (endogenetic forces), driven by heat convection in the mantle. These forces are classified into two main types: **tensional forces** and **compressional forces**.

### Compressional Forces
**What they are:** Forces that push crustal rocks towards each other. They squeeze or shorten the crust.

**Resultant processes:**
- **Folding:** When sedimentary rock layers are pushed together, they buckle and bend into wave-like folds, rather than breaking. The upward folds are called **anticlines**, and the downward folds are called **synclines**.
- **Reverse Faulting:** If the pressure is too great, the rock fractures (faults), and one block is pushed up and over the other block.

**Resultant Landforms:**
- **Fold Mountains:** These are the largest and most prominent mountain ranges in the world. As the crust is squeezed, massive layers of sedimentary rock are thrust upwards.
- *Examples:* The Atlas Mountains in North Africa, the Himalayas in Asia, the Alps in Europe, and the Andes in South America.

### Tensional Forces
**What they are:** Forces that pull crustal rocks apart, away from each other. They stretch and lengthen the crust.

**Resultant processes:**
- **Normal Faulting:** As the crust is stretched, it cracks and develops faults. A block of rock slides downwards relative to the blocks on either side.

**Resultant Landforms:**
- **Rift Valleys (Grabens):** A long, narrow depression formed when the block of land between two parallel normal faults sinks downwards.
  - *Example:* The Great East African Rift Valley, which stretches from the Red Sea down through East Africa.
- **Block Mountains (Horsts):** Formed when the block of land between two faults is left standing higher than the surrounding land that has sunk, or when a block is pushed up between faults.
  - *Example:* The Ruwenzori Mountains in East Africa, and the Vosges in France.

### Comparison: Tensional vs Compressional Forces

| Feature | Compressional Forces | Tensional Forces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direction of movement** | Pushing towards each other ($\rightarrow \leftarrow$) | Pulling away from each other ($\leftarrow \rightarrow$) |
| **Effect on the crust** | Squeezes, shortens, and thickens the crust | Stretches, lengthens, and thins the crust |
| **Type of faulting produced** | Reverse faults and thrust faults | Normal faults |
| **Primary process** | Folding (bending of rocks) | Faulting (cracking and displacement) |
| **Resultant landforms** | Fold mountains (anticlines and synclines) | Rift valleys (grabens) and Block mountains (horsts) |

## Major Landforms
**Objective vii: identify and describe the major landforms.**

The surface of the lithosphere is shaped into various features called landforms. They are grouped into mountains, plateaus, plains, coastal landforms, karst topography, and desert landforms.

### 1. Mountains
A mountain is a prominent landform that rises steeply above its surroundings.
- **Fold Mountains:** Formed by compressional forces buckling sedimentary rocks (e.g., Atlas Mountains).
- **Block Mountains:** Formed by tensional forces causing faulting and uplift (e.g., Ruwenzori).
- **Volcanic Mountains (Mountains of Accumulation):** Formed when hot lava, ash, and cinders erupt from a vent and build up into a cone shape over time.
  - *Examples:* Mount Cameroon, Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania, Mount Elgon.
- **Residual Mountains (Mountains of Denudation):** Formed when a previously high area is worn down by agents of erosion (wind, water), leaving behind hard, resistant rocks standing as hills or mountains.
  - *Examples:* The Ahaggar (Hoggar) Mountains in the Sahara, the Idanre Hills in Nigeria.

### 2. Plateaus
A plateau is an extensive area of highland with a relatively flat top and steep sides (often called a tableland).
- **Tectonic Plateaus:** Massive blocks of the Earth's crust uplifted by tectonic forces (e.g., the Deccan Plateau in India).
- **Volcanic Plateaus:** Formed when basic (fluid) lava flows out through long cracks (fissures) in the ground and spreads over a vast area, solidifying to form a flat highland.
  - *Examples:* The Biu Plateau in Borno State, Nigeria; the Columbia Plateau in the USA. (The Jos Plateau in Nigeria is a complex mixture of tectonic uplift, granite intrusions, and volcanic activity).
- **Dissected Plateaus:** Plateaus that have been severely worn down and cut into deep valleys and gorges by rivers over a long time, making them look rugged.
  - *Example:* The Udi-Nsukka Plateau in Enugu State.

### 3. Plains
A plain is an extensive area of flat or gently undulating (rolling) lowland.
- **Structural Plains:** These are structurally depressed areas of the world made of horizontally bedded rocks, undisturbed by tectonic movements.
  - *Example:* The Great Plains of the USA, the Russian Platform.
- **Erosional Plains:** Formed when agents of erosion wear down elevated landforms into a low, almost flat plain known as a **peneplain**.
- **Depositional Plains:** Formed by the deposition of materials brought by rivers, wind, or glaciers.
  - **Alluvial/Flood plains:** Formed by rivers depositing silt and mud during floods (e.g., the Niger-Benue Trough).
  - **Deltaic plains:** Formed at the mouth of a river where it deposits sand and mud into the sea (e.g., the Niger Delta).
  - **Outwash plains:** Formed by sand and gravel deposited by meltwater from glaciers.

### 4. Coastal Landforms
The coast is where the land meets the sea. Waves shape the coast through erosion and deposition.

**Erosional features:**
- **Cliffs:** Steep rock faces plunging into the sea.
- **Wave-cut platform:** A flat rocky area left at the base of a retreating cliff.
- **Headlands and Bays:** Hard rocks stick out into the sea as headlands, while soft rocks are eroded backwards into bays.
- **Caves, Arches, Stacks, and Stumps:**
  1. Waves carve out a hollow at the base of a headland to form a **cave**.
  2. If the cave breaks all the way through the headland, it forms an **arch**.
  3. The roof of the arch eventually collapses, leaving an isolated pillar of rock called a **stack**.
  4. The stack is eroded down to a short rock called a **stump**.

**Depositional features:**
- **Beaches:** Sloping bands of sand or pebbles along the coast, formed by constructive waves (e.g., Bar Beach in Lagos).
- **Spits and Bars:** A spit is a ridge of sand joined to the land at one end and projecting into the sea. If it grows completely across a bay, it forms a **bar**, cutting off the water behind it to form a **lagoon** (e.g., the Lagos Lagoon behind the coastal sandbars).

### 5. Karst Topography
Karst topography is a unique landscape formed in regions with thick **limestone** or chalk bedrock. It is shaped mostly by **chemical weathering (carbonation)**, where rainwater (which is slightly acidic) dissolves the calcium carbonate in the limestone.

**Surface features:**
- **Limestone Pavements:** Bare, flat limestone surfaces marked by deep grooves (grikes) and flat blocks (clints).
- **Swallow holes (Sinkholes) and Dolines:** Depressions or holes in the ground where surface water disappears underground by dissolving the rock.

**Underground features (Caves/Caverns):**
Because water sinks into the rock, rivers flow underground and carve out massive caves. Inside these caves, dripping mineral-rich water creates features:
- **Stalactites:** Icicle-like fingers of limestone hanging down from the **roof** (ceiling) of the cave.
- **Stalagmites:** Mounds of limestone growing upwards from the **floor** of the cave, directly under a stalactite.
- **Pillars (Columns):** Formed when a stalactite and a stalagmite grow until they meet and join.

### 6. Desert Landforms
Deserts are arid regions with very little rainfall. The landscape is shaped primarily by **wind** action, though occasional heavy rains (water action) also play a role.

**Wind Erosion features:**
- **Deflation hollows:** Basins formed when wind blows away dry, loose sand. If they reach down to the groundwater, an **oasis** is formed.
- **Rock Pedestals (Mushroom Rocks):** Formed by wind abrasion (sand-blasting). The wind carries sand near the ground, eroding the base of a rock faster than the top, leaving a mushroom shape.
- **Zeugen and Yardangs:** Ridges and furrows carved out of rock by wind erosion. Zeugen form on horizontal rock layers, yardangs form on vertical rock layers.

**Wind Deposition features:**
- **Sand Dunes:** Hills of wind-blown sand.
  - **Barchans:** Crescent-shaped dunes. The wind blows from the convex (rounded) side, pushing the 'horns' or points forward.
  - **Seifs:** Long, straight ridges of sand parallel to the wind direction.
- **Loess:** Fine, yellowish dust carried great distances by wind and deposited as thick, fertile soil.

**Water action in deserts:**
- **Wadis:** Dry river beds that only fill with water during rare, heavy rainstorms.
- **Inselbergs:** Prominent steep-sided hills of hard rock rising abruptly from a flat plain, left behind after surrounding softer rock was eroded by water and wind.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mixing up Sial and Sima:** Remember "Al" in Si**al** stands for Aluminium (Continental crust, lighter), and "Ma" in Si**ma** stands for Magnesium (Oceanic crust, heavier/basaltic).
- **Intrusive vs Extrusive Igneous rocks:** Magma cools *inside* (Intrusive/Granite); Lava cools *outside* on the exterior (Extrusive/Basalt).
- **Fossils in rocks:** Only **sedimentary** rocks contain fossils. Igneous and metamorphic rocks are subjected to heat/pressure that destroys any organic remains.
- **Confusing tensional and compressional forces:** Tension = stretching, pulling apart = Normal faults, Rift valleys. Compression = squeezing together = Reverse faults, Folds, Fold mountains.
- **Stalactites vs Stalagmites:** A classic exam trap. Stala**c**tites hang from the **c**eiling. Stala**g**mites grow from the **g**round.
- **Fold Mountains vs Block Mountains:** The Atlas, Alps, and Himalayas are Fold mountains. The Ruwenzori is a Block mountain. Do not mix their formation processes.

## Quick summary

1. The Earth's internal structure consists of the Core, the Mantle, and the Crust (divided into Sial and Sima). The external structure includes the Atmosphere, Hydrosphere, and Biosphere, which interact continuously through energy exchange and the water cycle.
2. Rocks are classified into Igneous (cooled magma/lava), Sedimentary (compacted sediments), and Metamorphic (changed by heat/pressure).
3. Sedimentary rocks are stratified and contain fossils; igneous rocks are crystalline; metamorphic rocks are very hard and often foliated.
4. Compressional tectonic forces push crustal rocks together, causing folding and forming Fold Mountains (e.g., Atlas Mountains).
5. Tensional tectonic forces pull crustal rocks apart, causing normal faulting and forming Rift Valleys and Block Mountains.
6. Coastal landforms are shaped by waves. Erosion creates cliffs, caves, arches, and stacks, while deposition creates beaches, spits, and bars.
7. Karst topography develops in limestone areas via chemical weathering, featuring underground caves, stalactites, and stalagmites.
8. Desert landforms are primarily shaped by wind, producing erosional features like rock pedestals and deflation hollows, and depositional features like barchans and seif dunes.

- B. Mesosphere
- C. Sima
- D. Barysphere

**Answer: C.** The Sima (Silica and Magnesium) is the denser lower layer of the crust found mostly beneath oceans. The student who selects A has confused the oceanic crust with the lighter continental crust (Sial).

**2. In the hydrological cycle, the transfer of water from the biosphere into the atmosphere is called**
- A. evaporation
- B. condensation
- C. precipitation
- D. transpiration

**Answer: D.** Transpiration is the process where plants (part of the biosphere) release water vapour into the atmosphere. The student who selects A has confused the release of water from plants with the general heating of water bodies (evaporation).

**3. Which of the following rocks is formed from the cooling and solidification of magma deep within the Earth's crust?**
- A. Basalt
- B. Granite
- C. Limestone
- D. Marble

**Answer: B.** Granite is an intrusive igneous rock that cools slowly underground. The student who selects A has confused magma cooling deep underground with lava cooling quickly on the surface.

**4. A major characteristic of sedimentary rocks is that they**
- A. are crystalline in structure
- B. are formed by extreme heat and pressure
- C. are arranged in distinct layers or strata
- D. never contain fossils

**Answer: C.** Sedimentary rocks are deposited in distinct horizontal layers called strata. The student who selects A or B is confusing sedimentary rocks with igneous or metamorphic rocks, respectively.

**5. When a limestone rock is subjected to intense heat and pressure over a long time, it changes into**
- A. slate
- B. quartzite
- C. gneiss
- D. marble

**Answer: D.** Limestone (a sedimentary rock) metamorphoses into marble. The student who selects A has confused the parent rock of marble with clay or shale, which turns into slate.

**6. Which of the following landforms is the result of tensional forces in the Earth's crust?**
- A. Fold mountain
- B. Rift valley
- C. Anticline
- D. Syncline

**Answer: B.** Tensional forces pull the crust apart, causing normal faults that allow a central block to drop, forming a rift valley. The student who selects A, C, or D is confusing tensional forces with compressional forces.

- A. spit
- B. beach
- C. stack
- D. wave-cut platform

**Answer: C.** A stack is formed when the roof of a coastal arch collapses, leaving an isolated pillar of rock in the sea. The student who selects A or B is confusing an erosional feature with a depositional feature.

**8. Which of the following features is a wind depositional landform found in arid regions?**
- A. Rock pedestal
- B. Barchan
- C. Yardang
- D. Oasis

**Answer: B.** A barchan is a crescent-shaped sand dune formed by wind depositing sand. The student who selects A or C is confusing depositional landforms with wind erosional landforms.

**9. The underground karst feature consisting of a column of calcium carbonate hanging from the roof of a cave is a**
- A. stalagmite
- B. doline
- C. swallow hole
- D. stalactite

**Answer: D.** Stalactites hang from the ceiling (roof) of a cave. The student who selects A has confused the formation hanging from the roof with the one growing from the ground (stalagmite).

**10. Which of the following raw materials is the primary sedimentary rock used in the manufacture of cement in Nigeria?**
- A. Granite
- B. Coal
- C. Limestone
- D. Sandstone

**Answer: C.** Limestone is an organically or chemically formed sedimentary rock that is the essential raw material for making cement. The student who selects A has confused the rock used for cement with the rock crushed for hardcore road building.
