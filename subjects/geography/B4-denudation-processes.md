# JAMB Geography Notes: Denudation Processes in the Tropics

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section II (Physical Geography), Topic D of the JAMB Geography syllabus: Denudation processes in the tropics. It covers four content items:

- i. Weathering
- ii. Erosion
- iii. Mass movement
- iv. Deposition

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Identify the agents of denudation (water, wind and waves).
2. Identify the landforms associated with each process and agent.

**Note:** The syllabus specifically focuses on "the tropics." The tropics are regions that lie between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn. This area includes the hot, wet equatorial forests (like southern Nigeria), the distinct wet-and-dry savanna regions (like northern Nigeria), and the hot deserts (like the Sahara). The climatic conditions in these areas heavily influence which agents and processes of denudation are most active.

## What is Denudation?

The Earth's crust is constantly being shaped by powerful forces. Some forces build the land up (like volcanism and folding), while others tear it down. **Denudation** is the general term for the stripping, laying bare, and wearing away of the Earth's surface. 

Denudation involves four main processes, acting together in sequence:
1. **Weathering:** The breakdown of rocks into smaller particles where they stand (*in situ*).
2. **Mass movement:** The sliding or creeping of these broken rocks down a slope due to gravity.
3. **Erosion:** The wearing away and removal of rock materials by moving agents.
4. **Transportation and Deposition:** The movement of the eroded materials and their eventual dropping (deposition) when the agent loses energy.

## Agents of denudation (water, wind and waves)

For erosion and deposition to happen, there must be a moving force to carry the rock materials away. These moving forces are called **agents of denudation**. The syllabus requires you to identify three primary agents active in the tropics:

1. **Water (Rivers and Running Water):** This is the most powerful and widespread agent of denudation in the humid tropics (such as the equatorial and savanna regions of Nigeria). Rivers carve deep valleys, carry millions of tonnes of mud and sand, and build large deltas (like the Niger Delta).
2. **Wind:** Wind is the dominant agent of denudation in hot, dry tropical environments, such as the Sahara Desert and the Sahel region of West Africa. Because there is very little vegetation or moisture to hold the soil together, wind can easily pick up, blast, and transport loose sand and dust over vast distances.
3. **Waves (Coastal Waters):** Waves, driven by wind, act along the coastlines of the tropics. As they crash against the shore, they break down coastal rocks and shift sand along the beach. The Atlantic coastline of West Africa, including Lagos' Bar Beach, is constantly shaped by wave action.

**Textbook note:** In colder or high-altitude regions outside the tropics, **glaciers (moving ice)** are a major agent of denudation. However, because this topic focuses on the tropics, ice is rarely an agent, except on the very top of high tropical mountains like Mount Kilimanjaro in East Africa.

## Weathering

**Weathering** is the gradual breakdown or decay of rocks at or near the Earth's surface into smaller fragments. The most important fact to remember about weathering is that it happens **in situ** (in place)—it does not involve the large-scale movement of the rock pieces by agents like rivers or wind. 

Weathering is driven by weather elements (temperature, rainfall) and biological activities. It is divided into three main types: physical, chemical, and biological.

### 1. Physical (Mechanical) Weathering
This is the physical breakdown of rocks into smaller pieces without any change in their chemical composition. It is dominant in hot deserts and dry savannas where there are extreme temperature changes between day and night.

- **Temperature Changes (Exfoliation):** In tropical deserts, rocks heat up drastically during the day and expand. At night, temperatures drop rapidly, and the rocks cool and contract. The outer layers of the rock expand and contract more than the inside. Over time, the stress causes the outer layers to peel off like an onion skin. This process is called **exfoliation**. It produces large, rounded rock hills called exfoliation domes (examples include Zuma Rock in Niger State and Olumo Rock in Abeokuta).
- **Block Disintegration:** In rocks that are naturally well-jointed (having cracks), repeated expansion and contraction from temperature changes cause the rock to break apart into large rectangular blocks.
- **Granular Disintegration:** Rocks like granite are made of different minerals (quartz, feldspar, mica) which expand and contract at different rates when heated. This uneven stress causes the rock to crumble into individual grains of sand.
- **Frost Action:** When water enters cracks in rocks and freezes at night, it expands by about 9%, acting like a wedge to split the rock. Though common in cold climates, in the tropics, this only happens on high mountain peaks where temperatures drop below freezing.
- **Salt Crystallization:** In deserts, evaporation draws underground water to the surface. The water evaporates, leaving behind salt crystals in rock crevices. As the crystals grow, they force the rock apart.

### 2. Chemical Weathering
This is the breakdown of rocks caused by chemical reactions that change the actual minerals inside the rock into new substances. It is most rapid in the hot, wet equatorial tropics because chemical reactions speed up in high temperatures and require water.

- **Solution:** Rainwater dissolves soluble minerals in rocks, such as rock salt or gypsum, washing them away.
- **Carbonation:** As rain falls, it absorbs carbon (IV) oxide from the air, forming weak carbonic acid. This weak acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone rocks, dissolving them easily.
- **Oxidation:** Oxygen from the air and water reacts with minerals in the rock, especially iron. This is essentially the "rusting" of rocks. The rock weakens and crumbles. Oxidation is responsible for creating the deep red, iron-rich **laterite soils** very common across Nigeria and tropical Africa.
- **Hydration:** Certain rock minerals absorb water, expand, and become physically weaker, making them crumble easily.
- **Hydrolysis:** Hydrogen ions in water react with rock-forming minerals to create completely new compounds. For example, hydrolysis turns hard feldspar (found in granite) into soft clay.

### 3. Biological Weathering
This is the breakdown of rocks by living organisms (plants, animals, and humans). It involves both physical and chemical actions.

- **Plants:** The roots of trees grow into rock cracks in search of water. As the roots grow thicker, they exert immense pressure, wedging the rocks apart physically. Also, decaying leaves produce humic acids that chemically weather the rocks.
- **Animals:** Earthworms, termites, and burrowing animals dig into the soil, exposing underlying rocks to air and water, which speeds up weathering.
- **Humans:** Human activities like quarrying, mining, road construction, and agriculture physically break rocks apart.

| Feature | Physical (Mechanical) Weathering | Chemical Weathering |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | Breakdown of rocks without altering their chemical composition | Breakdown of rocks involving chemical changes in their minerals |
| **Dominant Climate** | Hot, dry regions (deserts) with high diurnal temperature range | Hot, wet regions (equatorial and tropical rainforests) |
| **Chief Agents** | Temperature changes, salt, (frost in high mountains) | Rainwater, oxygen, carbon (IV) oxide |
| **Key Processes** | Exfoliation, block and granular disintegration | Oxidation, carbonation, hydration, solution, hydrolysis |
| **End Product** | Smaller physical fragments of the original rock | New chemical substances (e.g., clay, lateritic soils) |

## Mass movement

**Mass movement** (or mass wasting) is the movement of weathered rock materials, soil, and mud down a slope under the influence of **gravity**. Unlike erosion, mass movement does not primarily rely on a moving transport agent like a river or wind; gravity is the driving force.

**Factors influencing mass movement:**
1. **Gradient (Slope steepness):** The steeper the slope, the faster and more likely the movement.
2. **Water:** Water acts as a lubricant, making the soil slippery. It also adds weight to the soil, making it more likely to slide.
3. **Vegetation:** Plant roots bind the soil together. Removing trees (deforestation) increases the risk of mass movement.
4. **Human activity:** Cutting roads into hillsides or building heavy structures on slopes can trigger movements.

### Types of Mass Movement
Mass movements are generally classified by how fast they occur and how much water is involved.

**1. Slow Movements:**
- **Soil Creep:** This is a very slow, continuous, and invisible downhill movement of soil. Though you cannot see it happening, you can see its effects: bent tree trunks, leaning fences and telegraph poles, and soil piling up behind walls.
- **Solifluction:** This occurs when the top layer of soil becomes completely saturated with water and slowly flows over a frozen or impermeable subsoil.

**2. Rapid Movements:**
- **Earthflows and Mudflows:** When heavy tropical rains saturate the soil, the mixture of earth and water becomes liquid and flows rapidly down a slope, often channeling into valleys. Mudflows can bury entire villages.
- **Landslides:** The sudden, rapid slipping of a large, dry or slightly wet mass of rock and soil down a steep slope or cliff. It leaves a bare scar on the hillside.
- **Rockfalls:** The free-falling of individual loose rock fragments from a steep cliff face, often triggered by weathering.

## Erosion

**Erosion** is the breaking away and removal of rock materials by moving agents of denudation (water, wind, and waves). While weathering breaks the rock in place, erosion actively wears it away and carries it off.

Each agent of erosion has specific mechanisms (processes) by which it wears away the land.

### Processes of Water (River) Erosion
Rivers use their energy and the load they carry (stones, sand) to erode their channels.
1. **Corrasion (Abrasion):** The river uses the stones, pebbles, and sand it is carrying as tools to scrape, grind, and rub away the riverbed and banks, much like sandpaper.
2. **Attrition:** The rocks and pebbles carried by the river constantly crash into each other as they bounce along. They break into smaller, smoother, and rounder pieces over time.
3. **Hydraulic Action:** The sheer physical force and weight of the moving water lifts loose material and forces air into cracks in the riverbanks. The pressure of the trapped air eventually shatters the riverbank, causing it to collapse.
4. **Solution (Corrosion):** The river water chemically dissolves soluble minerals in the rocks of its bed and banks (e.g., limestone) and washes them away in solution.

### Processes of Wind Erosion
Wind is highly effective in dry tropical deserts where there are no roots to hold the sand.
1. **Deflation:** The wind lifts and blows away loose, dry sand and dust, gradually lowering the land surface and leaving behind bare rock.
2. **Abrasion:** The wind drives airborne sand particles against standing rock formations, sand-blasting them. This process is most intense close to the ground where the wind carries the most sand.
3. **Attrition:** Wind-blown sand grains collide with each other in the air, wearing each other down into finer dust.

### Processes of Wave (Coastal) Erosion
Waves erode the coastline using mechanisms very similar to rivers.
1. **Corrasion:** Waves pick up sand and pebbles and hurl them violently against the base of coastal cliffs, chipping the rock away.
2. **Attrition:** The pebbles carried by the waves smash into one another as they are washed up and down the beach, becoming round and smooth.
3. **Hydraulic Action:** Crashing waves trap and compress air in the cracks of a cliff face. As the wave retreats, the compressed air expands explosively, shattering the rock.
4. **Solvent Action (Corrosion):** Seawater slowly dissolves certain types of coastal rocks, such as limestone cliffs.

## Deposition

**Deposition** is the process where agents of denudation drop or lay down the rock materials (sediments) they have been carrying. Deposition occurs when the transporting agent loses its energy and can no longer carry its load.

### Causes of Deposition
1. **For Rivers:** A river deposits its load when its speed decreases. This happens when the river enters a flat area (reducing its gradient), when the volume of water decreases (due to a dry season or evaporation in the tropics), or when the river flows into a still body of water like a lake or the sea. The heaviest materials (boulders) are dropped first, and the finest (silt and clay) are dropped last.
2. **For Wind:** Wind deposits its sand when its speed is reduced by obstacles such as bushes, large rocks, or fences, or when the wind simply dies down.
3. **For Waves:** Waves deposit sand and pebbles along the coast when they lose energy, particularly in sheltered bays or when gentle, **constructive waves** push material up the beach without enough force to pull it back out to sea.

## Landforms associated with each process and agent

Over thousands of years, the processes of weathering, erosion, and deposition carve out distinct features on the Earth's surface called **landforms**. The syllabus requires you to identify which landforms are created by which agent and process.

### 1. Landforms of Weathering and Mass Movement
Because weathering and mass movement work closely together, their landforms are often found in the same environments.
- **Exfoliation Domes:** Huge, steep-sided, rounded hills of solid rock created by physical weathering (temperature changes peeling the rock). Common in tropical savannas.
- **Tors:** Piles of broken, rectangular blocks of granite standing on top of a hill. They form when chemical weathering decays the rock deep underground along its joints, and later, erosion washes away the soft clay, leaving the hard rectangular core blocks exposed.
- **Talus (Scree):** A sloping pile of jagged, angular rock fragments that have gathered at the base of a cliff due to rockfalls (mass movement) and weathering.
- **Landslide Scars:** Bare patches of rock or earth left on a steep hillside after a landslide has fallen away.

### 2. Landforms of Water (River Action)
Rivers create different landforms depending on whether they are in their upper (youthful), middle (mature), or lower (old age) courses.

**Erosional Landforms of Rivers:**
- **V-shaped Valleys:** In its upper course, a river flows fast down a steep gradient, eroding vertically (downward) to cut a deep valley that looks like a "V" in cross-section.
- **Gorges:** Very deep, narrow valleys with vertical rock sides.
- **Waterfalls:** A sudden, steep drop in the riverbed. Waterfalls form step-by-step:
  1. A river flows over a layer of hard rock that sits on top of softer rock.
  2. The river erodes the softer rock much faster, creating a step.
  3. As the soft rock erodes, the hard rock is left overhanging.
  4. The overhanging rock eventually collapses into a deep pool at the base called a **plunge pool**.
  5. Examples include Victoria Falls on the Zambezi River and Gurara Falls in Niger State.
- **Rapids:** Sections of a river where the water flows violently over a series of hard rock steps.
- **Potholes:** Circular, hollow depressions drilled into the solid rock of the riverbed by pebbles swirling around in powerful water eddies.
- **Ox-bow Lakes (Partially Erosional):** A crescent-shaped lake formed on a river floodplain. 
  1. A river meanders (bends) across a flat plain.
  2. The river erodes the outer bank of the bend and deposits sand on the inner bank.
  3. Over time, erosion narrows the "neck" of land between two bends.
  4. During a flood, the river cuts straight through the narrow neck.
  5. Deposition seals off the ends of the old bend, leaving it isolated as an ox-bow lake.

**Depositional Landforms of Rivers:**
- **Floodplains:** Wide, flat lands on either side of the river in its lower course, built up by layers of fertile silt deposited during floods.
- **Levees:** Raised natural embankments of mud and sand that run parallel to the riverbanks, formed when the river repeatedly floods and drops its coarsest load immediately beside the channel.
- **Braided Channels:** A river network that splits into many smaller channels separated by temporary islands of sand. It happens when the river is overloaded with sediment and drops it in its own path.
- **Deltas:** A large, flat, triangular tract of land formed at the mouth of a river where it meets the sea or a lake. As the river enters the sea, it loses all its energy and deposits its vast load, blocking its own mouth and branching into smaller streams called **distributaries**. A prime example is the Niger Delta in southern Nigeria.

### 3. Landforms of Wind Action (Deserts)
Wind sculpts the hot deserts (like the Sahara) into bizarre and distinct shapes.

**Erosional Landforms of Wind:**
- **Rock Pedestals (Mushroom Rocks):** A pillar of rock with a wide, heavy top and a thin, narrow base. It forms because wind abrasion (sand-blasting) is strongest near the ground (carrying the most sand), eroding the base of the rock faster than the top.
- **Zeugens:** Tabular masses of hard rock sitting on softer rock, forming long ridges separated by deep furrows. They form when wind widens existing cracks in a hard top layer.
- **Yardangs:** Steep-sided ridges of solid rock that run parallel to the direction of the wind, separated by long grooves blown out by the wind.
- **Deflation Hollows:** Broad depressions blown out of the desert floor by deflation. If the wind blows away enough sand to reach the underground water table, water seeps out, creating a fertile **oasis**.
- **Inselbergs:** Steep-sided, isolated hills that rise sharply from a flat plain (like a rocky island in a sea of sand). They are the resistant remnants of long periods of weathering and wind erosion.

**Depositional Landforms of Wind:**
- **Sand Dunes:** Hills or ridges of loose sand deposited by the wind.
  - **Barchans:** Crescent-shaped sand dunes. The wind blows the sand into a crescent, with the two "horns" pointing downwind (in the direction the wind is blowing).
  - **Seif Dunes (Longitudinal Dunes):** Long, narrow, parallel ridges of sand that can stretch for hundreds of kilometers in the direction of the prevailing wind.
- **Loess:** Extensive, very deep deposits of fine, wind-blown yellow dust. Loess soils are extremely fertile.

### 4. Landforms of Wave Action (Coasts)
Waves shape the boundaries where the land meets the sea.

**Erosional Landforms of Waves:**
- **Cliffs:** A steep rock face that rises vertically from the sea, constantly undercut by waves.
- **Wave-cut Platforms:** A gently sloping, flat surface of solid rock found at the base of a cliff. As wave erosion causes the cliff to collapse and retreat inland, the platform is left behind.
- **Caves, Arches, Stacks, and Stumps:** This is a famous sequence of coastal erosion on a headland (a piece of land sticking out into the sea):
  1. Hydraulic action and corrasion widen a crack in the cliff base into a hollow **cave**.
  2. If caves form on both sides of a headland, they erode inwards until they meet, creating a hole right through the rock called an **arch**.
  3. Continued erosion widens the arch, and its roof eventually collapses under gravity, leaving an isolated pillar of rock in the sea called a **stack**.
  4. The sea erodes the base of the stack until it collapses, leaving a small, submerged lump of rock called a **stump**.

**Depositional Landforms of Waves:**
- **Beaches:** A strip of gently sloping land along the coast made of deposited sand, pebbles, or shells. (e.g., Victoria Island Beach, Lagos).
- **Spits:** A long, narrow ridge of sand that is joined to the mainland at one end and projects out into the sea at the other end.
- **Bars:** A ridge of sand and shingle that grows across the mouth of a bay or river, completely blocking it off to form a lagoon behind it.
- **Tombolos:** A ridge of deposited sand that connects an offshore island to the mainland.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse weathering and erosion:** Weathering breaks rocks down where they stand (*in situ*); erosion involves movement and an agent carrying the rock away.
- **Know the difference between Corrasion and Attrition:** Corrasion (or abrasion) is rocks scratching the *bed/banks*; attrition is rocks smashing into *each other*.
- **Remember the climate link:** Chemical weathering rules the hot, wet equatorial zones. Physical weathering rules the hot, dry deserts.
- **Distinguish between Barchans and Rock Pedestals:** Barchans are crescent dunes made by wind *deposition*; rock pedestals are mushroom rocks made by wind *erosion*.
- **Ox-bow lakes involve two processes:** They start with river *erosion* cutting the meander neck, but are completed by *deposition* sealing off the ends.
- **Learn the coastal sequence:** Crack → Cave → Arch → Stack → Stump. This sequence appears frequently in UTME.

## Quick summary

1. Denudation is the wearing away of the Earth's surface through weathering, mass movement, erosion, and deposition.
2. The primary moving agents of denudation in the tropics are running water (rivers), wind, and coastal waves.
3. Weathering is the *in situ* breakdown of rocks. Physical weathering (like exfoliation) dominates deserts, while chemical weathering (like oxidation and solution) dominates wet tropical rainforests.
4. Mass movement is the movement of earth materials down a slope driven by gravity, ranging from slow soil creep to rapid landslides and mudflows.
5. Rivers erode using corrasion, attrition, hydraulic action, and solution, creating V-shaped valleys, waterfalls, and gorges. When they lose energy, they deposit to form floodplains, levees, and deltas.
6. Wind erosion (deflation and abrasion) shapes deserts, creating rock pedestals, zeugens, and oases, while wind deposition creates dunes (barchans) and loess.
7. Coastal waves erode cliffs into caves, arches, and stacks, and deposit sand to form beaches, spits, and bars.

## Practice questions

**1. The breakdown of rocks into smaller particles without any movement by a transporting agent is called?**
- A. Erosion
- B. Weathering
- C. Mass movement
- D. Deposition

**Answer: B.** Weathering is the breakdown of rocks *in situ* (in place), without the involvement of moving agents carrying the material away.

**2. Which of the following landforms is a result of wind deposition?**
- A. Rock pedestal
- B. Yardang
- C. Barchan
- D. Zeugen

**Answer: C.** A barchan is a crescent-shaped sand dune formed when the wind drops its load of sand. The other options are erosional landforms.

**3. The peeling off of the outer layers of a rock due to alternate heating and cooling is known as?**
- A. Carbonation
- B. Oxidation
- C. Hydrolysis
- D. Exfoliation

**Answer: D.** Exfoliation (or onion-peel weathering) is a physical weathering process common in tropical deserts and savannas, driven by wide diurnal temperature ranges.

**4. The slow, unnoticeable downhill movement of soil, often indicated by bent tree trunks and leaning fences, is called?**
- A. Landslide
- B. Mudflow
- C. Soil creep
- D. Rockfall

**Answer: C.** Soil creep is an extremely slow type of mass movement. A common mistake is choosing landslide, but landslides are rapid and very noticeable.

**5. Which of the following chemical weathering processes is responsible for the formation of red lateritic soils in the tropics?**
- A. Oxidation
- B. Solution
- C. Hydration
- D. Carbonation

**Answer: A.** Oxidation occurs when oxygen in the air and water reacts with iron in the rocks, essentially rusting it and turning the soil red.

**6. The process whereby river or wave water traps and compresses air in rock crevices, causing the rock to shatter, is called?**
- A. Attrition
- B. Corrasion
- C. Hydraulic action
- D. Deflation

**Answer: C.** Hydraulic action is the sheer physical force of the water pressing trapped air into cracks until the rock bursts apart.

**7. An ox-bow lake is a landform associated with the action of?**
- A. Wind
- B. Waves
- C. Glaciers
- D. A river

**Answer: D.** An ox-bow lake forms on a river's floodplain when a severe meander loop is cut off from the main channel by erosion and deposition.

**8. Which of the following represents the correct sequence of coastal erosion on a headland?**
- A. Cave → Stack → Arch → Stump
- B. Arch → Cave → Stump → Stack
- C. Cave → Arch → Stack → Stump
- D. Stack → Cave → Stump → Arch

**Answer: C.** A wave widens a crack into a cave. If it punches through, it becomes an arch. The roof of the arch collapses to leave a stack, which is eventually eroded down to a stump.

**9. The Niger Delta is an example of a landform created by?**
- A. Wave erosion
- B. River deposition
- C. Wind deposition
- D. River erosion

**Answer: B.** A delta is formed when a river deposits its massive load of sediment as it enters the sea and loses its energy.

**10. Which of the following is NOT an agent of denudation?**
- A. Wind
- B. Temperature
- C. Waves
- D. Water

**Answer: B.** Temperature causes weathering, but it is not a *moving agent* that carries eroded materials away. Water, wind, and waves are the active transporting agents of denudation.
