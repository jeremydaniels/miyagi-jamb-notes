## Oceans and seas

### Locate oceans and seas on the globe

**Objective i: locate oceans and seas on the globe.**

Water covers about 71% of the earth's surface. The vast majority of this water is found in the oceans and seas. 

An **ocean** is a very large expanse of salt water. The world has five major oceans, from largest to smallest:
1. **Pacific Ocean:** The largest and deepest ocean. It separates Asia and Australia from the Americas.
2. **Atlantic Ocean:** The second-largest ocean, shaped like the letter 'S'. It lies between the Americas on the west and Europe/Africa on the east.
3. **Indian Ocean:** The third-largest ocean, enclosed by Africa to the west, Asia to the north, and Australia to the east.
4. **Southern (Antarctic) Ocean:** Surrounds the continent of Antarctica.
5. **Arctic Ocean:** The smallest and shallowest ocean, located around the North Pole. It is covered by ice for much of the year.

A **sea** is a smaller body of salt water, mostly partially enclosed by land, that is often connected to an ocean. Some important seas include:
- **The Mediterranean Sea:** Located between Southern Europe and North Africa.
- **The Red Sea:** A narrow sea between northeast Africa and the Arabian Peninsula.
- **The Caribbean Sea:** Located in the western Atlantic Ocean, bounded by the Americas.
- **The South China Sea:** A marginal sea of the Western Pacific, bordered by East and Southeast Asian countries.
- **The Gulf of Guinea:** A very important maritime area for West Africa (including Nigeria), forming the northeasternmost part of the tropical Atlantic Ocean.

### Characteristics and uses of oceans and seas

**Objective ii: examine the characteristics and uses of oceans and seas.**
**Content i: Oceans and seas (world distribution, salinity and uses).**

#### Salinity of oceans and seas

**Salinity** refers to the degree of saltiness of the water. The average salinity of ocean water is about **35 parts per thousand ($35‰$)**, which means there are 35 grams of dissolved salts (mostly sodium chloride) in every 1,000 grams of seawater. 

The salinity is not exactly the same everywhere. It varies depending on several factors:
- **Evaporation:** High temperatures and low humidity increase evaporation, which removes pure water and leaves the salt behind, increasing salinity. The highest salinities (e.g., the Red Sea, around $40‰$) are found in hot, dry regions.
- **Precipitation (Rainfall):** Heavy rainfall adds fresh water, which dilutes the seawater and lowers its salinity. Equatorial regions have lower salinity despite high heat because they receive heavy rainfall.
- **River Runoff:** Large rivers pouring fresh water into the sea lower the salinity. The ocean water near the Niger Delta, where the River Niger enters the Gulf of Guinea, has a lower salinity.
- **Melting Ice:** In polar regions, melting glaciers and icebergs release fresh water, which lowers the salinity of the surrounding sea.

#### Other characteristics
- **Temperature:** Surface temperatures range from about $30 °C$ near the equator to $-2 °C$ near the poles. Temperature drops rapidly as depth increases.
- **Density:** Seawater is denser than fresh water because of the dissolved salts, making it easier for ships (and people) to float in the sea.
- **Tides:** The water in oceans and seas rises and falls twice a day due to the gravitational pull of the moon and the sun.

#### Uses of oceans and seas
1. **Transportation:** They provide cheap international highways for large cargo ships (e.g., importing goods to Lagos ports).
2. **Food Supply (Fishing):** They are a major source of protein, providing fish, crabs, and prawns. 
3. **Mineral Resources:** Many oceans contain valuable minerals. A prime example is **crude oil and natural gas** extracted from the continental shelf in the Niger Delta region.
4. **Climate Moderation:** Oceans absorb and release heat slowly, moderating the climate of coastal areas (making them cooler in summer and warmer in winter than inland areas).
5. **Water Cycle:** They are the primary source of water vapour for the global water cycle, which eventually brings rainfall to landmasses.
6. **Recreation and Tourism:** Coastal beaches (like Bar Beach or Tarkwa Bay in Lagos) attract tourists.

## Ocean Currents

**Ocean currents** are continuous, directed movements of ocean water on the surface of the earth. You can think of them as large rivers flowing across the oceans.

### Classify the types of ocean currents

**Objective iii: classify the types of ocean currents.**

Ocean currents are classified into two main types based on their temperature relative to the surrounding water:

| Feature | Warm Ocean Currents | Cold Ocean Currents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Origin** | Equatorial regions | Polar or high-latitude regions |
| **Direction of flow** | Flow from the equator towards the poles | Flow from the poles towards the equator |
| **Temperature** | Warmer than the surrounding water | Colder than the surrounding water |
| **Effect on coastlines** | Bring warming effects and rainfall | Bring cooling effects, fogs, and dry conditions (deserts) |
| **Examples (Atlantic)** | Gulf Stream, North Atlantic Drift, Brazil Current | Canary Current, Benguela Current, Labrador Current |
| **Examples (Pacific)** | Kuroshio Current, East Australian Current | Oyashio Current, California Current, Peru (Humboldt) Current |
| **Examples (Indian)** | Agulhas Current, Mozambique Current | West Australian Current |

### Causes and effects of ocean currents

**Objective v: evaluate the causes and effects of ocean currents.**
**Content ii: Ocean currents: types, distribution, causes and effects.**

#### Causes of ocean currents
Ocean currents are driven by a combination of natural forces:
1. **Planetary Winds:** Prevailing winds like the Trade Winds and the Westerlies constantly blow across the ocean surface, dragging the water with them and setting it in motion.
2. **The Earth's Rotation (Coriolis Effect):** As the earth rotates on its axis from west to east, it creates the **Coriolis force**. This force deflects wind and ocean currents to the **right** in the Northern Hemisphere and to the **left** in the Southern Hemisphere.
3. **Differences in Water Density (Temperature and Salinity):** Cold, salty water is heavy and sinks, while warm, less salty water is lighter and stays on top. This vertical movement causes deep-sea convection currents.
4. **The Shape of Landmasses:** When an ocean current hits a continent, it is forced to deflect and follow the shape of the coastline (e.g., the Equatorial Current hits the Brazilian coast and splits).

#### Effects of ocean currents
1. **Modification of Climate:** Warm currents raise the temperature of the coastal areas they wash (e.g., the North Atlantic Drift keeps ports in Western Europe ice-free in winter). Cold currents lower the temperatures of tropical coasts.
2. **Formation of Coastal Deserts:** Cold currents blowing past western coasts in tropical regions cool the air, causing it to hold less moisture and preventing rain from forming. This leads to the creation of hot deserts. For example:
   - The **Benguela Current** (cold) contributes to the **Namib Desert** in southwestern Africa.
   - The **Canary Current** (cold) contributes to the **Sahara Desert** in northwest Africa.
   - The **Peru/Humboldt Current** (cold) contributes to the **Atacama Desert** in South America.
3. **Fogs:** When a warm current meets a cold current, the warm, moist air is suddenly cooled, forming dense fogs that are highly dangerous to ships. A famous example is off the coast of **Newfoundland** (Canada), where the warm Gulf Stream meets the cold Labrador Current.
4. **Rich Fishing Grounds:** The places where warm and cold currents meet churn up nutrients from the deep sea, encouraging the growth of plankton (fish food). These areas (like Newfoundland and the coast of Japan) are the world's richest fishing grounds.
5. **Navigation:** Ships travel faster and save fuel when travelling with a current, but are slowed down if they sail against it.

### Account for the distribution of ocean currents

**Objective iv: account for the distribution of ocean currents.**

To "account for" means to explain the pattern and reasons behind where currents are found. The distribution of surface ocean currents forms huge circular patterns called **gyres**. 

Because of the Earth's rotation (the Coriolis force) and the prevailing winds, ocean currents are distributed in large loops:
- In the **Northern Hemisphere**, these gyres flow in a **clockwise** direction.
- In the **Southern Hemisphere**, they flow in an **anti-clockwise** direction.

**Step-by-step example in the North Atlantic:**
1. The **North Equatorial Current** (warm) is driven westwards across the Atlantic by the Trade Winds.
2. It hits the Americas, turns north, and becomes the **Gulf Stream** (warm), flowing along the eastern USA.
3. Caught by the Westerlies, it crosses the Atlantic towards Europe as the **North Atlantic Drift** (warm).
4. As it hits Europe and Africa, it is deflected southwards as the **Canary Current** (cold) back to the equator, completing the clockwise gyre.

## Lakes

### Types and location of lakes

**Objective vi: identify the types and location of lakes.**
**Content iii: Lakes: types, distribution and uses.**

A **lake** is a body of water completely surrounded by land. Lakes are generally formed when a depression (hollow) in the earth's surface fills with water. Textbooks usually classify lakes based on **how the depression was formed**.

#### 1. Lakes formed by Earth Movements (Tectonic Lakes)
When the earth's crust fractures and sinks, it forms deep, long, and narrow depressions called Rift Valleys. When these valleys fill with water, they form **Rift Valley Lakes**. 
- **Examples in Africa:** Lake Tanganyika, Lake Malawi, Lake Albert, Lake Edward, and Lake Turkana (all located in the East African Rift Valley). Lake Tanganyika is exceptionally deep.

#### 2. Lakes formed by Volcanic Activity
- **Crater and Caldera Lakes:** When a volcano becomes dormant, its crater (or the larger caldera formed if the top collapses) can fill with rain water.
- **Examples:** Lake Bosumtwi in Ghana, Lake Nyos in Cameroon, and Lake Toba in Indonesia.

#### 3. Lakes formed by Erosion
- **Glacial Lakes:** Moving ice (glaciers) scoops out hollows in the rock in mountainous areas. When the ice melts, water fills the hollows to form lakes called **tarns** or **corrie lakes**. Examples include the Great Lakes of North America.
- **Wind-deflated Lakes:** In deserts, winds can blow away loose sand to create hollows that reach the water table, forming **oases**.

#### 4. Lakes formed by Deposition
Sometimes, materials like silt or sand are deposited across a river valley, blocking the flow of water and creating a lake.
- **Ox-bow lakes:** Formed in the lower course of a river when a meander is cut off by river deposition (explained further under river landforms). Example: Lake Utange on the River Rufiji in Tanzania.
- **Lagoon lakes:** Sandbars deposited by ocean waves enclose a body of shallow coastal water. Example: Lagos Lagoon.

#### 5. Man-made (Artificial) Lakes
These are lakes created by humans, usually by building dams across rivers.
- **Examples:** **Lake Kainji** (on the River Niger in Nigeria), **Lake Volta** (in Ghana), and **Lake Nasser** (on the River Nile in Egypt).

### Characteristics and uses of lakes

**Objective vii: indicate the characteristics and uses of lakes.**

#### Characteristics of lakes
1. **Freshwater vs. Saltwater:** Most lakes are freshwater because rivers constantly flow in and out, flushing away salts (e.g., Lake Victoria). However, if a lake has no outlet to the sea (an area of inland drainage) and experiences high evaporation, it becomes a **salt lake** (e.g., the Dead Sea, the Great Salt Lake).
2. **Permanent vs. Temporary (Ephemeral):** Permanent lakes hold water all year. Temporary lakes may dry up completely during the dry season or drought.
3. **Fluctuating size:** Some shallow lakes expand greatly in the wet season and shrink drastically in the dry season. **Lake Chad** (located where Nigeria, Niger, Chad, and Cameroon meet) is a famous example of a shallow, inland-drainage freshwater lake that fluctuates heavily in size.

#### Uses of lakes
1. **Hydro-electric Power (HEP):** Water from artificial lakes is used to generate electricity (e.g., Kainji Dam generates power for Nigeria).
2. **Irrigation:** Lake water is pumped to farm dry lands, boosting agriculture.
3. **Domestic and Industrial Water Supply:** Lakes provide fresh water for drinking, washing, and factory use.
4. **Fishing:** Lakes are an important source of inland fish (e.g., tilapia and catfish from Lake Chad and Lake Kainji).
5. **Transportation:** Large lakes (like Lake Victoria or the Great Lakes) act as major inland transport routes for ships.
6. **Tourism and Recreation:** Lakes are scenic spots for boating, swimming, and tourist resorts.

## Rivers: Action of running water

### Action of running water

**Content iv: Rivers: Action of running water.**

A river acts as a powerful agent of denudation through three main processes: **Erosion, Transportation, and Deposition.**

#### 1. River Erosion
Erosion is the wearing away of the river channels. It happens in three directions:
- **Vertical erosion:** Downward cutting, deepening the valley (mostly in the upper course).
- **Lateral erosion:** Sideways cutting, widening the valley (mostly in the middle and lower courses).
- **Headward erosion:** Cutting backward at the source, making the river longer.

Rivers erode their channels through four mechanisms:
1. **Corrasion (Abrasion):** The mechanical grinding of the river's bed and banks by the rocks, pebbles, and sand carried by the river.
2. **Attrition:** The boulders and pebbles carried by the river smash against one another, breaking down into smaller, smoother, and rounder pieces.
3. **Corrosion (Solution):** The chemical dissolving of soluble rocks (like limestone) by the slightly acidic river water.
4. **Hydraulic Action:** The sheer force of the fast-flowing water smashes against the river banks, trapping air in cracks, expanding them, and forcing pieces of rock to break off.

#### 2. River Transportation
The materials carried by a river are called its **load**. A river transports its load in four ways:
1. **Traction:** Large boulders and heavy rocks are rolled or dragged along the riverbed.
2. **Saltation:** Smaller pebbles and stones bounce or "jump" along the riverbed in a series of short hops.
3. **Suspension:** Fine, light particles (silt and clay) are held up in the flowing water, often making the river look muddy.
4. **Solution:** Dissolved chemicals (like calcium carbonate) are carried invisibly in the water.

#### 3. River Deposition
Deposition occurs when a river loses its energy and drops its load. This usually happens when:
- The gradient (slope) becomes gentler, so the water slows down.
- The volume of the river decreases (e.g., during a dry season).
- The river enters a calm body of water (a lake or sea).
- The river meets an obstacle.

### Landforms of the different stages of a river course

**Objective viii: identify the landforms of the different stages of a river course.**

A typical river flows from its source (high up in the mountains) down to its mouth (where it meets the sea). This long profile is divided into three stages: Upper, Middle, and Lower.

| Stage | Main Process | Characteristics | Major Landforms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Upper (Youthful) Course** | Vertical Erosion | Steep slope, fast-flowing water, small volume of water, large heavy load. | V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, gorges, waterfalls, rapids, plunge pools, cataracts. |
| **2. Middle (Mature) Course** | Lateral Erosion and Transportation | Gentler slope, wider channel, increased water volume (joined by tributaries), valley widens. | U-shaped valleys, river cliffs, slip-off slopes, meanders. |
| **3. Lower (Old Age) Course** | Deposition | Flat gradient, sluggish flow, huge volume of water, heavy load of fine sediment. | Floodplains, ox-bow lakes, levees, braided channels, deltas. |

#### Landforms of the Upper Course
- **V-shaped Valley:** The fast river cuts vertically downward, while rain and mass wasting wear down the sides, creating a deep, steep 'V' shape.
- **Interlocking Spurs:** As the river winds around hard rock obstacles in its path, the ridges of land on either side interlock like the teeth of a zip.
- **Waterfalls and Rapids:** A waterfall forms when a river plunges vertically over a steep cliff. This usually happens when a band of hard rock lies across the riverbed, on top of softer rock. The river easily erodes the soft rock, undercutting the hard rock until it forms a ledge.
- **Plunge Pool:** At the base of the waterfall, the tremendous force of the falling water and swirling rocks (corrasion and hydraulic action) digs a deep depression called a plunge pool.

Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

#### Landforms of the Middle Course
- **Meanders:** As the river flows over gentler slopes, it starts to swing from side to side in wide loops known as meanders.
- **River Cliffs and Slip-off Slopes:** On the *outside* bend of a meander, the water flows fast, eroding the bank to form a steep **river cliff**. On the *inside* bend, water flows slowly, depositing sediment to form a gently sloping beach called a **slip-off slope**.

#### Landforms of the Lower Course
- **Floodplain:** A wide, flat area of land bordering the river, made entirely of fine silt (alluvium) deposited when the river floods its banks.
- **Levees:** Raised banks of sand and gravel alongside the river channel, built up over time by successive floods dropping their heaviest sediment closest to the river channel.
- **Ox-bow Lakes:** 
  **How an ox-bow lake forms (Step-by-step):**
  1. A meander loop becomes very pronounced.
  2. Erosion on the outer banks of the meander neck makes the neck very narrow.
  3. During a heavy flood, the river cuts straight through the narrow neck, taking the shortest route.
  4. Deposition seals off the ends of the old meander loop.
  5. The cut-off loop of water left behind is the ox-bow lake.
- **Deltas:** A delta is a flat, fan-shaped piece of land at the mouth of a river, formed when the river deposits its remaining load into the sea or a lake faster than the sea currents can wash it away. A classic example is the **Niger Delta** in Nigeria, which is an arcuate (fan-shaped) delta.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Salinity | The amount of dissolved salts in seawater, usually measured in parts per thousand ($35‰$). |
| Ocean Current | A continuous, directed mass movement of ocean water. |
| Gyre | A large circular system of ocean currents. |
| Coriolis Effect | The deflection of wind and ocean currents caused by the earth's rotation. |
| Tectonic Lake | A lake formed when water fills a depression created by movements of the earth's crust (e.g., Rift Valley). |
| Ephemeral Lake | A temporary lake that dries up during periods of drought or dry seasons. |
| Corrasion (Abrasion) | River erosion caused by the grinding action of the rocks and pebbles carried by the water. |
| Hydraulic Action | River erosion caused by the sheer force of water smashing against rock faces and forcing air into cracks. |
| Plunge Pool | A deep basin excavated at the foot of a waterfall by the swirling force of water and rocks. |
| Ox-bow Lake | A crescent-shaped lake formed when a meander is cut off from the main river channel. |
| Delta | A landform built up at the mouth of a river through heavy deposition of sediment. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse corrasion and corrosion:** *Corrasion* (abrasion) is physical grinding with rocks. *Corrosion* is chemical dissolving (solution).
- **Current directions:** Remember that gyres run **clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere** and **anti-clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere**.
- **Cold Currents and Deserts:** A very common JAMB question asks to link a desert with its cold current. Memorize: Benguela -> Namib Desert. Canary -> Sahara. Peru/Humboldt -> Atacama.
- **Lake Chad's uniqueness:** Lake Chad is an area of *inland drainage*, but it is largely freshwater, unlike many inland lakes which become salty.
- **Upper vs. Lower course landforms:** Never mix these up. Waterfalls and V-shaped valleys are strictly upper course (erosion). Deltas and ox-bow lakes are strictly lower course (deposition).
- **Saltation vs Suspension:** Saltation is bouncing along the bottom; suspension is floating within the water body.

## Quick summary

1. The earth has five major oceans. Average ocean salinity is $35‰$, affected by evaporation, precipitation, river runoff, and melting ice.
2. Ocean currents are either warm (flow from equator to poles) or cold (flow from poles to equator). They are caused by winds, earth's rotation, density differences, and landmass shapes.
3. Cold currents lower temperatures, cause coastal deserts, and create rich fishing grounds where they meet warm currents (e.g., Newfoundland).
4. Lakes are classified by their formation: tectonic (Rift Valley lakes in Africa), volcanic (crater lakes), glacial, and depositional (ox-bow lakes).
5. A river undergoes three main processes: erosion (corrasion, attrition, corrosion, hydraulic action), transportation (traction, saltation, suspension, solution), and deposition.
6. A river has three stages: the Youthful upper course (vertical erosion, waterfalls, V-shaped valleys), the Mature middle course (lateral erosion, meanders), and the Old Age lower course (deposition, deltas, ox-bow lakes).

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following factors does NOT decrease the salinity of ocean water?**
- A. Heavy equatorial rainfall
- B. Runoff from large rivers
- C. High rate of evaporation
- D. Melting of polar ice

**Answer: C.** High evaporation removes water but leaves the salt behind, thereby *increasing* salinity. Rainfall, river runoff, and melting ice all add freshwater, diluting the salt and decreasing salinity.

**2. The cold ocean current responsible for the formation of the Namib Desert is the**
- A. Canary Current
- B. Benguela Current
- C. Peru Current
- D. Agulhas Current

**Answer: B.** The cold Benguela Current flows along the southwestern coast of Africa, cooling the air and preventing rainfall, which leads to the formation of the Namib Desert. The Canary Current influences the Sahara.

**3. In the Northern Hemisphere, ocean currents are deflected to the right to form clockwise loops due to**
- A. planetary winds
- B. the earth's rotation on its axis
- C. the gravitational pull of the moon
- D. the differences in water salinity

**Answer: B.** The earth's rotation on its axis creates the Coriolis force, which deflects winds and currents to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere.

**4. A deep, circular basin formed at the base of a waterfall by the swirling action of falling water and rocks is called a**
- A. plunge pool
- B. meander
- C. crater lake
- D. gorge

**Answer: A.** The tremendous force of water hitting the bottom, combined with rocks grinding the bedrock (corrasion), excavates a plunge pool at the foot of a waterfall.

**5. Which of the following lakes is an example of a tectonic lake formed in a rift valley?**
- A. Lake Bosumtwi
- B. Lake Kainji
- C. Lake Tanganyika
- D. Lake Chad

**Answer: C.** Lake Tanganyika lies in the East African Rift Valley, which was formed by faulting (tectonic earth movements). Bosumtwi is a volcanic crater lake, Kainji is man-made, and Chad is a shallow depression/inland drainage lake.

**6. The process by which a river breaks down large rocks into smaller, rounder, and smoother pebbles by knocking them against one another is known as**
- A. corrasion
- B. attrition
- C. hydraulic action
- D. solution

**Answer: B.** Attrition is the wear and tear of the transported rocks themselves as they crash into each other. Corrasion is when the rocks scrape against the riverbed or banks.

**7. Which of the following landforms is characteristic of the lower course of a river?**
- A. Interlocking spurs
- B. V-shaped valley
- C. Waterfall
- D. Ox-bow lake

**Answer: D.** An ox-bow lake is formed in the lower (old age) course through a combination of severe meandering, erosion at the neck, and finally deposition sealing off the loop. The other options are upper course features.

**8. When small pebbles and stones are transported by a river by bouncing along the riverbed, the process is called**
- A. traction
- B. saltation
- C. suspension
- D. corrosion

**Answer: B.** Saltation is the process where smaller stones hop or bounce along the river bottom. Traction involves rolling or dragging large heavy boulders.

**9. Where the cold Labrador Current meets the warm Gulf Stream off the coast of Newfoundland, the result is usually**
- A. the formation of a coastal desert
- B. dense fogs and rich fishing grounds
- C. heavy tropical cyclones
- D. increased salinity in the ocean

**Answer: B.** The mixing of warm moist air from the warm current and cold air from the cold current causes condensation into dense fog. The churning water brings up nutrients, making it excellent for plankton and fish.

**10. A lake formed when ocean waves deposit a sandbar across the mouth of a bay, enclosing shallow sea water, is known as a**
- A. tarn
- B. crater lake
- C. lagoon lake
- D. rift valley lake

**Answer: C.** A lagoon lake (like Lagos Lagoon) is a coastal body of water formed by sand spits or bars deposited by ocean waves, enclosing it from the open sea. A tarn is glacial.
