## Factors controlling the growth of plants

**Objective i: trace the factors controlling the growth of plants; Content i: Factors controlling growth of plants**

**Vegetation** refers to the total plant cover growing in a particular area. The type of plants that grow in a place, how tall they grow, and how dense they are, depend on a combination of several environmental factors. These factors are grouped into four main categories: climatic, edaphic (soil), topographic (relief), and biotic factors.

### 1. Climatic Factors
Climate is the most important factor determining the major vegetation zones of the world.
- **Rainfall (Moisture):** Water is essential for photosynthesis and plant survival. Areas with high, year-round rainfall (over 2,000 mm) support dense forests with tall trees (like the tropical rainforest). Areas with moderate or seasonal rainfall support grasslands (savannas), while areas with very low rainfall (under 250 mm) support only sparse desert vegetation.
- **Temperature:** Plants need a minimum temperature to grow. The hot temperatures of the tropics encourage continuous, rapid plant growth, provided there is water. In cold polar regions, the ground is frozen, and plant growth is restricted to a very short summer season.
- **Sunlight (Light):** Sunlight provides the energy for photosynthesis. In dense forests, trees compete for sunlight, growing very tall and forming a broad canopy, while smaller plants on the dark forest floor must adapt to low light (shade-tolerant plants).
- **Wind:** Strong, continuous winds can increase the rate of transpiration (water loss) and physical damage to plants. In exposed coastal or mountainous areas, trees may grow stunted or bent in the direction of the prevailing wind.

### 2. Edaphic (Soil) Factors
"Edaphic" refers to the influence of soils on plants.
- **Soil Texture and Structure:** Sandy soils drain water too quickly, making it hard for plants to get moisture. Clay soils hold water but can become waterlogged. Loamy soils are best for most plant growth.
- **Soil Nutrients:** Soils rich in humus (decayed organic matter) and essential minerals (like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) support rich, luxuriant vegetation.
- **Soil pH:** Some plants prefer acidic soils, while others prefer alkaline soils. Extreme acidity or alkalinity limits plant growth.

### 3. Topographic (Relief) Factors
The shape of the land affects plant growth indirectly by modifying climate and soil.
- **Altitude (Elevation):** As you climb a high mountain (like Mount Cameroon or Mount Kilimanjaro), the temperature drops. The vegetation changes from tropical forest at the base to temperate grassland, then coniferous trees, and finally bare rock or snow at the top.
- **Slope Steepness:** Steep slopes have rapid water runoff and high soil erosion, leading to thin soils that support only poor vegetation. Gentle slopes or flat lands have deep, rich soils that support thick vegetation.
- **Aspect (Slope Direction):** In the northern hemisphere, south-facing slopes receive more direct sunlight and are warmer, supporting different plant species than the cooler, shaded north-facing slopes.

### 4. Biotic Factors
These are the influences of living organisms.
- **Animals:** Grazing animals (like cattle, goats, and antelopes) eat grasses and young tree shoots. Overgrazing can destroy vegetation and turn a grassland into a desert. Insects and birds aid in pollination and seed dispersal.
- **Other Plants:** Plants compete with each other for light, water, and space. Parasitic plants and climbing vines (lianas) rely on other trees for support or nutrients.
- **Human Activities:** Humans have the greatest biotic impact through farming, logging, bush burning, and urbanization (discussed fully later in these notes).

## The process of vegetation development

**Objective ii: analyse the process of vegetation development; Content ii: The concept of vegetation e.g., plant communities and succession**

Vegetation is not static; it changes over time. A **plant community** is a collection of different plant species living together in a particular environment. The progressive, step-by-step replacement of one plant community by another in a given area over time is called **ecological succession**.

### Types of Succession
There are two main types of succession, depending on how the process starts:

| Feature | Primary Succession | Secondary Succession |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Starting point** | Bare, lifeless area with no soil (e.g., bare rock, new volcanic lava, sand dunes). | An area where the previous vegetation was destroyed, but soil remains (e.g., abandoned farmland, burnt forest). |
| **Speed** | Very slow (takes hundreds or thousands of years because soil must form first). | Much faster (takes decades or a few centuries because soil and seeds are already present). |
| **Pioneer species** | Lichens and mosses. | Grasses, weeds, and fast-growing shrubs. |

### Step-by-Step Process of Primary Succession

1. **Bare Surface:** The process begins on a barren surface, such as a newly formed volcanic rock, where no life or soil exists.
2. **Pioneer Stage:** Hardy organisms called **pioneer species** (usually lichens and mosses) are the first to colonize the bare rock. They release mild acids that slowly break the rock down into tiny particles. When they die, they decay, adding organic matter to the rock particles to form a thin layer of primitive soil.
3. **Herbaceous (Seral) Stage:** With a thin layer of soil now present, seeds of grasses, ferns, and small herbaceous plants blow in and take root. Their roots break the rock further, and their dead leaves add more humus, making the soil deeper and richer.
4. **Shrub Stage:** As the soil deepens and holds more moisture, larger woody plants and shrubs begin to grow. They shade out the smaller pioneer plants, taking over the area.
5. **Climax Community:** Finally, large trees take root and grow into a mature, stable forest. This final, stable plant community that is in perfect balance with the local climate and soil is called the **climax community**. Unless there is a major climate change or human interference, the climax community will remain relatively unchanged.

## Major types of vegetation, their characteristics and distribution

**Objective iii: identify the types, their characteristics and distribution; Content iii: Major types of vegetation, their characteristics and distribution**

The world is divided into major vegetation zones (biomes) based primarily on climate. In JAMB, you are expected to know the major types, where they are found, and their key characteristics.

### 1. Tropical Equatorial Rainforest
- **Distribution:** Found between 0° and 5° to 10° North and South of the Equator. Major areas include the Amazon Basin (South America), the Congo Basin (Central Africa), Southeast Asia (Malaysia, Indonesia), and the southern coastal belt of Nigeria.
- **Climate:** Hot and wet all year round. Mean monthly temperature of about 27 °C and high annual rainfall (over 2,000 mm).
- **Characteristics:**
  - **Evergreen:** The trees do not shed all their leaves at the same time, so the forest is green all year.
  - **Stratification (Layering):** The forest has three distinct layers:
    1. *Emergent layer:* The tallest trees (up to 40–50 metres) that break through the canopy (e.g., mahogany, iroko, obeche).
    2. *Canopy layer:* A dense, continuous roof of leaves and branches (20–30 metres high) that blocks sunlight from reaching the ground.
    3. *Undergrowth (Ground layer):* Sparse, consisting of shade-tolerant ferns, fungi, and shrubs because very little sunlight penetrates.
  - **Lianas and Epiphytes:** Climbing woody vines (lianas) twist around trunks to reach sunlight. Epiphytes (like orchids and ferns) grow on the branches of other trees for support, absorbing moisture from the air.
  - **Buttress Roots:** Because the soil is thin and nutrients are mostly near the surface, tall trees have large, wide base roots (buttress roots) to support their massive weight.
  - **Hardwoods:** The dominant trees are broad-leaved hardwoods, which are highly valuable for timber.

### 2. Tropical Grassland (Savanna)
- **Distribution:** Found between 5° and 15° North and South of the Equator, bordering the rainforests. Major areas include West, East, and Central Africa, the Llanos of Venezuela, and the Campos of Brazil.
- **Climate:** Distinct wet and dry seasons. Annual rainfall is moderate (500 mm to 1,500 mm), and temperatures remain high (21 °C to 32 °C).
- **Characteristics:**
  - **Tall Grasses:** The dominant vegetation is tall, coarse grass (e.g., elephant grass, which can grow up to 3 metres high). The grass turns brown and dormant during the dry season and grows rapidly in the wet season.
  - **Scattered Trees:** Trees are scattered rather than forming a closed canopy. Typical trees include the baobab, acacia, and shea butter tree.
  - **Adaptations to Drought and Fire:** Trees are deciduous (shed leaves in the dry season to reduce water loss through transpiration). They have thick bark to protect against natural and human-induced bush fires, and long taproots to reach deep underground water.
  - **Nigerian Savanna Zones:** In Nigeria, the savanna is sub-divided from south to north as rainfall decreases:
    1. *Guinea Savanna:* Tallest grasses and most trees (closest to the rainforest).
    2. *Sudan Savanna:* Shorter grasses and fewer, more drought-resistant trees.
    3. *Sahel Savanna:* Very short, sparse grasses and thorny shrubs (borders the Sahara Desert).

### 3. Tropical Deserts and Semi-Deserts
- **Distribution:** Found on the western sides of continents between 15° and 30° North and South. Examples include the Sahara and Kalahari (Africa), Atacama (South America), Great Australian Desert, and the Arabian Desert.
- **Climate:** Extremely arid (dry). Annual rainfall is less than 250 mm. Very hot days and cold nights.
- **Characteristics (Xerophytes):**
  - Plants are highly adapted to survive extreme drought (xerophytes).
  - **Roots:** Extremely long taproots to tap deep groundwater, or widespread shallow roots to quickly absorb rare rainfall.
  - **Leaves:** Leaves are reduced to spines or thorns (e.g., cacti, acacia) to minimize water loss via transpiration and to deter grazing animals.
  - **Stems:** Thick, fleshy, succulent stems designed to store water.
  - **Seeds:** Seeds can remain dormant in the sand for years, germinating and completing their life cycle in just a few days after a sudden rainstorm.

### 4. Mediterranean Vegetation
- **Distribution:** Found on the western margins of continents between 30° and 45° North and South. Examples include the lands around the Mediterranean Sea (North Africa, Southern Europe), California, Central Chile, southwestern Australia, and the southwestern tip of South Africa (Cape Town area).
- **Climate:** Hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters.
- **Characteristics:**
  - Plants must adapt to survive the hot, dry summer.
  - Trees are mostly evergreen but have small, leathery, waxy, or hairy leaves to reduce transpiration (e.g., olive, cork oak).
  - Deep taproots and thick bark (like the cork oak).
  - Much of the original forest has been cleared and replaced by sweet-smelling shrubs and bushes, known locally as *Maquis* (Europe), *Chaparral* (California), or *Fynbos* (South Africa).

### 5. Temperate Grasslands
- **Distribution:** Found in the interiors of continents in the mid-latitudes, far from the influence of the sea.
- **Local Names:** You must know the local names for these grasslands:
  - **Prairies:** North America (Canada and USA)
  - **Pampas:** South America (Argentina)
  - **Steppes:** Eurasia (Russia and Central Asia)
  - **Veld:** South Africa
  - **Downs:** Australia
- **Climate:** Warm summers and very cold winters. Low to moderate rainfall (250 mm to 500 mm), mostly falling in summer.
- **Characteristics:**
  - Almost entirely treeless plains. The rainfall is insufficient to support trees but enough for grass.
  - The grasses are shorter, softer, and more nutritious than tropical savanna grasses.
  - Highly fertile soils (like the Chernozems or black earths) make these regions the world's most productive areas for wheat farming and cattle rearing.

### 6. Coniferous Forest (Taiga)
- **Distribution:** A broad belt across the high northern latitudes of North America (Canada) and Eurasia (Russia, Scandinavia). It is absent in the southern hemisphere due to the lack of landmass at those latitudes.
- **Climate:** Long, severely cold winters (temperatures below freezing for over 6 months) and short, cool summers. Precipitation is mainly in the form of snow.
- **Characteristics:**
  - Dominated by evergreen, cone-bearing trees (conifers) such as pine, fir, and spruce.
  - **Needle-like leaves:** Leaves are narrow and tough to prevent water loss (since water is frozen in winter) and can withstand freezing.
  - **Conical shape:** The trees are shaped like a cone with flexible branches, allowing heavy snow to slide off without breaking them.
  - **Softwoods:** The timber is soft, light, and easily cut, making this biome the world's major supplier of paper, pulp, and construction timber.

### 7. Tundra (Polar Vegetation)
- **Distribution:** North of the Arctic Circle, bordering the Arctic Ocean (Northern Canada, Greenland, Northern Russia).
- **Climate:** Extremely cold. Winters are long and dark. The subsoil is permanently frozen all year round, a condition known as **permafrost**.
- **Characteristics:**
  - Completely treeless. The permafrost prevents deep roots from growing.
  - Vegetation is limited to mosses, lichens, and small, stunted shrubs.
  - Plants only grow during the very short summer (about 2–3 months) when the top few centimetres of soil thaw.

## Importance of vegetation

**Objective v: identify the importance of vegetation.**

Vegetation plays a vital role in both the natural environment and human economy.

### 1. Economic Importance
- **Timber and Construction:** Forests (both tropical hardwoods like mahogany and temperate softwoods like pine) provide timber for building houses, making furniture, and constructing boats.
- **Industrial Raw Materials:** Softwoods from the Taiga are pulped to make paper. Tropical forests provide rubber, gums, resins, tannins, and dyes.
- **Food and Cash Crops:** Vegetation provides wild fruits, nuts, berries, and mushrooms. Many wild forest plants have been domesticated into vital crops (e.g., cocoa, rubber, and oil palm originated in tropical forests).
- **Medicinal Value:** Over 25% of modern medicines originate from plants. For example, quinine (for treating malaria) comes from the bark of the Cinchona tree, and many traditional Nigerian herbal medicines rely on local forest and savanna plants.
- **Grazing and Livestock:** Tropical and temperate grasslands provide vast pastures for grazing livestock (cattle, sheep, goats), supporting the meat, milk, and leather industries (e.g., cattle rearing by the Fulani in the Nigerian savanna).
- **Tourism and Wildlife:** Vegetation provides habitats for wildlife. National parks and forest reserves (like Yankari Game Reserve in Nigeria or the Maasai Mara in Kenya) attract tourists, generating foreign exchange.

### 2. Ecological Importance
- **Oxygen Production and Carbon Sink:** Through photosynthesis, plants absorb carbon (IV) oxide (CO₂, a greenhouse gas) and release oxygen. Forests act as massive "carbon sinks," helping to regulate global climate and reduce global warming.
- **Soil Conservation:** The roots of plants bind the soil together, preventing erosion by wind and water. The canopy breaks the force of heavy raindrops. When leaves fall and decay, they add humus, improving soil fertility.
- **The Hydrological (Water) Cycle:** Trees absorb water from the soil and release it into the atmosphere as water vapour through **transpiration**. This moisture eventually condenses to form rain. Large forests help maintain local rainfall patterns.

## Impact of human activities on vegetation

**Objective iv: assess the impact of human activities on vegetation; Content iv: Impact of human activities on vegetation**

Human activities have drastically altered natural vegetation globally, often leading to environmental degradation, though some activities aim at conservation.

### Negative Impacts
1. **Deforestation:** This is the large-scale removal of forest cover. In Nigeria and worldwide, forests are cleared for commercial logging, expanding farmland, and cutting firewood for domestic cooking. Deforestation destroys wildlife habitats, increases soil erosion, and reduces transpiration, which can cause local rainfall to drop.
2. **Bush Burning:** Common in the tropical savanna regions (like northern Nigeria) during the dry season. Hunters burn grass to flush out animals, and farmers burn it to clear land or stimulate the growth of new grass for livestock. Repeated burning destroys young trees, kills soil microorganisms, and eventually impoverishes the soil.
3. **Overgrazing:** Keeping too many livestock in a small area causes them to eat the grass faster than it can grow back. The soil becomes bare and is easily blown away by the wind or washed away by rain, leading to **desertification** (the spread of desert-like conditions, a major problem in the Sahel region).
4. **Urbanization and Industrialization:** As populations grow, forests and grasslands are permanently destroyed to build houses, roads, factories, and dams (e.g., Kainji Dam).
5. **Mining and Oil Exploration:** Surface mining involves stripping away all topsoil and vegetation. In the Niger Delta region of Nigeria, frequent **oil spills** pollute the soil and water, killing mangroves, rainforest trees, and aquatic plant life in a process that takes decades to recover from.

### Positive Impacts
1. **Afforestation and Reforestation:** **Afforestation** is planting trees in an area that never had a forest, while **reforestation** is replanting trees in an area where the forest was cut down. These practices help restore soil, control erosion, and combat desertification. A key example is the "Great Green Wall" initiative in northern Nigeria and other Sahel countries, which involves planting a massive belt of trees to stop the Sahara Desert from expanding southwards.
2. **Establishment of Forest Reserves:** Governments set aside protected areas where logging and hunting are strictly prohibited by law, preserving the climax vegetation and biodiversity (e.g., Okomu National Park in Edo State).
3. **Controlled Grazing and Farming:** Using modern farming techniques like crop rotation and establishing designated grazing reserves helps preserve natural grasses and soils.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Vegetation** | The total plant cover growing in a particular area. |
| **Biome** | A large naturally occurring community of flora and fauna occupying a major habitat (e.g., rainforest or tundra). |
| **Ecological succession** | The progressive, step-by-step replacement of one plant community by another over time. |
| **Pioneer species** | The first hardy organisms (like lichens) to colonize a barren environment in primary succession. |
| **Climax community** | The final, stable plant community that is in balance with the environment. |
| **Edaphic factors** | Factors related to the soil (texture, nutrients, pH) that affect plant growth. |
| **Xerophyte** | A plant adapted to survive in an environment with very little water, such as a desert. |
| **Epiphyte** | A plant that grows on another plant for support but is not parasitic (e.g., orchids). |
| **Deciduous** | Trees that shed all their leaves during a specific season (usually the dry or cold season) to conserve water. |
| **Deforestation** | The permanent removal of trees to make room for something besides forest. |
| **Afforestation** | The planting of trees in an area where there was no previous forest. |
| **Permafrost** | A thick subsurface layer of soil that remains frozen throughout the year, occurring chiefly in polar regions. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Confusing primary and secondary succession:** Primary succession starts on bare rock (NO soil); secondary succession starts where vegetation was destroyed but soil STILL EXISTS (e.g., an abandoned farm). Secondary is much faster.
- **Names of Temperate Grasslands:** JAMB frequently asks you to match the grassland to the continent. Memorize: Prairies = North America, Pampas = South America, Steppes = Eurasia, Veld = South Africa, Downs = Australia.
- **Rainforest Structure:** Remember the three layers: Emergent (tallest, isolated crowns), Canopy (solid block of green), and Undergrowth (sparse, shade-tolerant).
- **Edaphic vs. Topographic:** If a question asks about soil influence on plants, the answer is *edaphic*. If it asks about altitude or slope, it is *topographic* (relief).
- **Climate Values:** Know the rough thresholds. Rainforest = >2000 mm rainfall, ~27°C. Savanna = 500-1500 mm rainfall, distinct dry/wet season. Desert = <250 mm rainfall.
- **Nigerian Savanna Order:** From the rainforest moving north: Guinea Savanna -> Sudan Savanna -> Sahel Savanna. (The climate gets progressively drier).

## Quick summary

1. Plant growth is controlled by climatic (rainfall, temperature), edaphic (soil), topographic (altitude, slope), and biotic (human and animal) factors.
2. Vegetation develops over time through a process called ecological succession, starting with pioneer species and ending with a stable climax community.
3. The Tropical Rainforest is characterized by tall, broad-leaved evergreen hardwoods with buttress roots, arranged in three layers (stratification) due to heavy year-round rain.
4. The Tropical Savanna is dominated by tall grasses and scattered, fire-resistant, deciduous trees (like the baobab) adapted to distinct wet and dry seasons.
5. Desert plants (xerophytes) have long roots, thick fleshy stems, and thorn-like leaves to survive extreme dryness.
6. Temperate grasslands (Prairies, Steppes, Pampas, Veld, Downs) are treeless plains with rich soils, crucial for global wheat and livestock production.
7. Vegetation provides immense economic value (timber, medicine, tourism) and ecological value (oxygen production, soil conservation, regulating the water cycle).
8. Human activities such as deforestation, overgrazing, and bush burning degrade vegetation, while afforestation and the creation of reserves help conserve it.

## Practice questions

**1. The progressive replacement of one plant community by another until a stable community is reached is known as**

- A. plant stratification
- B. ecological succession
- C. vegetation climax
- D. afforestation

**Answer: B.** Ecological succession is the step-by-step process where one plant community replaces another over time, culminating in a stable climax community.

**2. Which of the following is an edaphic factor that controls plant growth?**

- A. Altitude
- B. Rainfall
- C. Soil texture
- D. Sunlight

**Answer: C.** Edaphic factors refer specifically to soil characteristics such as texture, pH, and nutrient content. Altitude is topographic, while rainfall and sunlight are climatic.

**3. In primary succession starting on bare rock, the pioneer species are usually**

- A. tall grasses
- B. shrubs and weeds
- C. lichens and mosses
- D. broad-leaved trees

**Answer: C.** Lichens and mosses are hardy enough to grow on bare rock. They break the rock down to begin soil formation. Grasses and weeds appear later in the seral stages.

**4. A biome characterized by distinct wet and dry seasons, tall grasses, and scattered drought-resistant trees is the**

- A. Tropical rainforest
- B. Tropical savanna
- C. Temperate grassland
- D. Mediterranean vegetation

**Answer: B.** The tropical savanna has distinct seasons, tall coarse grasses, and scattered trees that adapt to the dry season by shedding leaves (deciduous).

**5. Which of the following pairs of temperate grasslands and their locations is INCORRECT?**

- A. Prairies - North America
- B. Pampas - South America
- C. Veld - Australia
- D. Steppes - Eurasia

**Answer: C.** The Veld is located in South Africa. The temperate grassland in Australia is called the Downs.

**6. Plants that have adapted to survive in areas with very little rainfall by developing thick, fleshy stems and thorn-like leaves are called**

- A. Epiphytes
- B. Xerophytes
- C. Parasites
- D. Hardwoods

**Answer: B.** Xerophytes are drought-tolerant plants with adaptations like fleshy stems (succulents) to store water and thorns to reduce transpiration, common in deserts.

**7. Moving from the southern coast of Nigeria towards the northern border, the correct sequence of vegetation zones is**

- A. Rainforest, Sudan Savanna, Guinea Savanna, Sahel Savanna
- B. Rainforest, Sahel Savanna, Sudan Savanna, Guinea Savanna
- C. Guinea Savanna, Rainforest, Sudan Savanna, Sahel Savanna
- D. Rainforest, Guinea Savanna, Sudan Savanna, Sahel Savanna

**Answer: D.** As rainfall decreases from south to north in Nigeria, the dense rainforest gives way to the tall-grass Guinea savanna, then the drier Sudan savanna, and finally the semi-desert Sahel savanna.

**8. The dominant tree species in the Taiga (Coniferous forest) biome have**

- A. broad leaves and buttress roots
- B. needle-like leaves and a conical shape
- C. thick, fleshy stems and long taproots
- D. leathery, waxy leaves and thick bark

**Answer: B.** Conifers (like pine and spruce) have needle-like leaves to survive extreme cold and prevent water loss, and a conical shape to let heavy snow slide off.

**9. Which of the following human activities contributes positively to the conservation of vegetation?**

- A. Afforestation
- B. Commercial logging
- C. Bush burning
- D. Overgrazing

**Answer: A.** Afforestation is the planting of trees in an area to create a forest, which restores vegetation, prevents erosion, and combats desertification.

**10. In a tropical rainforest, the layer of vegetation consisting of a continuous, dense roof of leaves and branches that blocks most sunlight from reaching the ground is the**

- A. Emergent layer
- B. Canopy layer
- C. Undergrowth
- D. Epiphyte layer

**Answer: B.** The canopy is the middle layer (about 20-30m high) forming a continuous roof. The emergent layer consists of solitary trees that break above the canopy.
