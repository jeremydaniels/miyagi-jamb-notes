## Definition and properties of soils

**Soil** is the loose, weathered, uppermost layer of the earth's crust in which plants grow. It is a complex mixture of broken rock particles, decayed organic matter, water, and air, providing mechanical support and nutrients for vegetation.

An ideal soil for plant growth (a loam) has four main components:
1. **Mineral matter (45%):** Formed from the weathering of parent rocks. It provides the soil with its structural framework and inorganic nutrients (like calcium and potassium).
2. **Soil water (25%):** Holds dissolved nutrients so plant roots can absorb them.
3. **Soil air (25%):** Fills the empty pore spaces not occupied by water. It provides oxygen for plant roots and soil organisms to respire.
4. **Organic matter (5%):** Formed from the remains of dead plants and animals. When fully decomposed, it turns into **humus**, a dark substance that binds the soil and holds nutrients.

### Classifying soils and their properties

Soils are classified and understood through their physical and chemical properties.

**1. Physical properties**
* **Soil texture:** This refers to the relative proportions of sand, silt, and clay in a soil. Texture determines how well the soil holds water and air. Sand has the largest particles, silt is medium-sized, and clay has microscopic, fine particles.
* **Soil structure:** This describes how soil particles group or clump together into aggregates called **peds**. Common structures include crumb (granular and loose, excellent for farming), blocky, platy, and prismatic.
* **Soil colour:** Colour indicates the soil's mineral content and drainage condition. Dark brown or black soils are rich in organic matter (humus). Red or yellow soils contain high amounts of iron oxides (common in the tropics). Grey or mottled (patchy) colours indicate waterlogged, poorly drained soils.
* **Soil porosity:** The volume of empty spaces (pores) between soil particles. It determines how much air and water the soil can hold.
* **Soil temperature:** Determines the rate of seed germination and the activity of soil microorganisms. Dark soils absorb more heat than light-coloured soils.

**2. Chemical properties**
* **Soil pH:** This is a measure of the acidity or alkalinity of the soil, measured on a scale from 1 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral. Less than 7 is acidic, and more than 7 is alkaline. High rainfall areas often have acidic soils because soluble nutrients are washed away, while dry areas often have alkaline soils. Most crops thrive in slightly acidic to neutral soils (pH 6.0 to 7.5).
* **Nutrient content:** The presence of essential chemical elements such as Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K), which plants need to grow.

**Classification based on texture**
We classify soils primarily by their texture into three broad types:

| Soil Texture Class | Particle Size and Drainage | Agricultural Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sandy Soil** | Coarse, large particles with large pore spaces. Excellent drainage and well-aerated, but dries out very quickly. Fails to hold nutrients (they wash away easily). | Poor for most agriculture unless heavily irrigated and fertilised. It is a "light soil," meaning it is very easy to plough. |
| **Clayey Soil** | Microscopic, fine particles packed tightly together. Drains water very poorly and easily becomes waterlogged. Sticky when wet and bakes hard when dry. | Very fertile because it holds nutrients tightly. However, it is a "heavy soil," making it difficult for farmers to plough and for roots to penetrate. |
| **Loamy Soil** | A balanced mixture of sand, silt, clay, and organic matter. Drains well but still retains enough moisture. | The ideal soil type for agriculture. It combines the aeration of sand with the nutrient-holding capacity of clay. |

**Common mistake:** Students often confuse soil *texture* (the proportion of sand/silt/clay particles) with soil *structure* (how those particles clump together into shapes). Remember: texture is about particle size, structure is about how they are arranged.

## Factors and processes of soil formation

Soil formation (pedogenesis) is a very slow process. It takes hundreds to thousands of years to form a few centimetres of mature soil. 

### Factors of soil formation

The Russian scientist Vasily Dokuchaev, and later Hans Jenny, identified five interacting factors that determine how a soil forms:

1. **Climate (The most active factor):** Rainfall and temperature dictate the rate of weathering and the type of biological activity. High temperatures and heavy rainfall in the equatorial regions speed up chemical weathering and leaching, creating deep soils rapidly. In dry deserts, soil formation is very slow.
2. **Living Organisms (Biosphere):** Vegetation drops leaves that decay into organic matter (humus). Soil organisms like earthworms, termites, and microbes break down this organic matter, mix the soil, and allow air to enter through their burrows. 
3. **Parent Material:** This is the underlying rock from which the soil is formed. It acts as a passive factor. The parent rock determines the soil's mineral content, natural colour, and initial texture. For example, weathering sandstone produces a sandy soil, while shale produces a clayey soil.
4. **Topography (Relief):** The shape of the land affects drainage and erosion. Steep mountain slopes suffer fast surface runoff and severe erosion, resulting in very thin, immature soils. Flat valleys and plains collect deposits, leading to deep, poorly drained, or highly fertile alluvial soils.
5. **Time:** Soil formation requires vast amounts of time. A "young" soil (like recent river deposits) has not had time to develop distinct layers. A "mature" soil has been undisturbed for thousands of years and shows clear, deep layers.

### Processes of soil formation

While the factors set the conditions, the actual physical and chemical changes occur through specific processes:

1. **Weathering:** The physical breakdown and chemical decay of the parent rock into small mineral particles.
2. **Humification:** The breakdown and decay of raw plant and animal remains into humus by microorganisms.
3. **Eluviation (Leaching):** The downward washing of soluble minerals (like calcium and magnesium) and fine clay particles from the upper layers of the soil by rainwater.
4. **Illuviation:** The accumulation (washing in) of the materials leached from above into the lower layers of the soil.
5. **Capillary action:** In dry climates where evaporation is high, water from deep underground is drawn upwards to the surface through tiny soil pores, bringing dissolved salts with it.

**Climate-specific processes:**
* **Laterization:** Occurs in hot, wet tropical climates (like the Equatorial Rainforest). Heavy rainfall causes extreme leaching (eluviation) of silica and soluble minerals. Insoluble iron and aluminium oxides are left behind, giving the soil a deep red colour. When exposed to air and dried, this soil can harden into a brick-like crust called **laterite**.
* **Calcification:** Occurs in dry, semi-arid regions (like the Sahel). Because evaporation exceeds rainfall, capillary action draws calcium compounds up to the surface layers, creating alkaline soils.
* **Salinization:** Similar to calcification but occurs in arid desert regions or poorly drained irrigated lands. Intense evaporation leaves high concentrations of toxic sodium and potassium salts near the surface, forming a white crust.
* **Podzolization:** Occurs in cool, humid temperate climates (like coniferous forests). Organic acids from pine needles severely leach iron and aluminium, leaving behind an ash-grey layer of silica. (This is generally not found in the tropics, but it is useful to know for contrast).

**Common mistake:** Confusing *eluviation* with *illuviation*. Remember that **E**luviation means **E**xiting (washing out from the top), while **I**lluviation means coming **I**n (accumulating in the subsoil).

## Soil profiles

If you dig a deep trench in the ground down to the solid rock, you will see that the soil is arranged in distinct horizontal layers. 

A **soil profile** is a vertical section through the soil showing the different layers from the ground surface down to the unweathered bedrock. Each individual layer is called a **soil horizon**.

[IMAGE: A cross-section diagram of a mature soil profile showing five distinct horizontal layers. Top layer (O horizon): thin, dark, covered in leaf litter and grass. Second layer (A horizon): thicker, dark brown topsoil with plant roots. Third layer (B horizon): lighter reddish-brown subsoil with accumulated clay and deep roots. Fourth layer (C horizon): yellowish-grey layer filled with large chunks of weathered rock. Bottom layer (R horizon): solid, unbroken grey bedrock. Labels must point to each horizon: "O horizon (Organic)", "A horizon (Topsoil / Zone of Eluviation)", "B horizon (Subsoil / Zone of Illuviation)", "C horizon (Weathered parent rock)", "R or D horizon (Bedrock)". Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

### Differentiating soil horizons

In Nigerian secondary school geography, a standard mature soil profile is typically divided into the following horizons:

| Horizon | Name | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **O** | Organic layer | The very top surface layer consisting of fresh, undecomposed leaf litter and decaying organic matter (humus). It is very dark. |
| **A** | Topsoil | A dark-coloured layer rich in humus mixed with mineral particles. This is the zone of **eluviation**, where rainwater washes soluble minerals and clay downwards. Biological activity (roots, worms, insects) is highest here. |
| **B** | Subsoil | A lighter-coloured, denser layer. This is the zone of **illuviation**, where the clay, iron, and aluminium leached from the A horizon accumulate. It contains very little organic matter. |
| **C** | Weathered rock | Made up of partially broken down fragments of the parent rock. It has no organic matter and is largely unaffected by biological activity. |
| **D (or R)** | Bedrock | The solid, continuous, unweathered parent rock underlying the soil. (Note: Many textbooks refer to this as the R horizon, but Nigerian syllabuses sometimes use D). |

**Note on textbook variations:** Some modern soil science textbooks include an "E" horizon (standing for Eluviation) sitting between the A and B horizons. However, in standard UTME Geography, the A horizon itself is primarily treated as the zone of eluviation. 

## Major tropical soil types

Tropical soils vary widely depending on the climate (rainfall and evaporation rates), natural vegetation, and local relief.

### Comparing tropical soil types

| Soil Type | Main Characteristics | Natural Fertility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lateritic Soils (Laterites)** | Reddish-brown colour due to high iron and aluminium oxides. Deeply weathered and heavily leached of nutrients and silica. Acidic. Forms a hard crust when exposed and dried. | **Very low.** Nutrients are easily washed away. The lush rainforest it supports survives only by rapidly recycling nutrients in the thin top organic layer. |
| **Tropical Ferruginous Soils** | Rich in iron (ferruginous) but much less leached than laterites because they form in areas with a distinct dry season. Often sandy-loam in texture. | **Moderate to High.** Better at holding nutrients and less acidic than lateritic soils. |
| **Tropical Black Earths (Vertisols)** | Dark-coloured, heavy clay soils. They expand, become sticky, and are easily waterlogged in the rainy season. In the dry season, they shrink and form deep, wide cracks. | **High.** They retain moisture very well during dry periods and are rich in bases like calcium. |
| **Desert Soils (Aridisols)** | Sandy, highly porous, light grey or yellow. Almost no organic matter (humus). Often highly alkaline or saline due to intense evaporation and calcification. | **Low.** Very poor for agriculture naturally, but can be highly productive if irrigated, as they have not been leached of their minerals. |
| **Alluvial Soils** | Young, immature soils composed of silt, sand, and clay deposited by floodwaters. They lack distinct horizons. | **Very High.** Constantly replenished with fresh mineral deposits from rivers. |

### Distribution and uses of soils

Understanding where these soils are found in Africa and Nigeria helps explain their agricultural uses.

**1. Lateritic Soils**
* **Distribution:** Found in regions with heavy rainfall, such as the Equatorial Rainforest and the Guinea Savanna zones. In Nigeria, they cover much of the southern states (e.g., Edo, Ondo, Rivers). Across Africa, they dominate the Congo Basin.
* **Uses:** Because of low natural fertility, they require heavy applications of fertilisers or organic manure for continuous food crop farming. However, they are suitable for deep-rooted tree crops like oil palm, rubber, and cocoa. The hardened laterite crust is widely excavated for road construction and making mud blocks for building.

**2. Tropical Ferruginous Soils**
* **Distribution:** Found in the Sudan Savanna and northern Guinea Savanna, where rainfall is lower and the dry season is long. In Nigeria, they cover large parts of the north (e.g., Kano, Sokoto).
* **Uses:** These are some of the most agriculturally important soils in West Africa. They are extensively used to grow cereals (sorghum, millet, maize) and legumes (groundnuts, cowpeas).

**3. Tropical Black Earths (Black Cotton Soils / Vertisols)**
* **Distribution:** Found in tropical continental margins and flat inland basins. In Nigeria, they are prominent in the Lake Chad Basin in Borno State (locally called *firki* soils). Also found in the Gezira plain of Sudan and the Deccan plateau of India.
* **Uses:** Because they retain water deep into the dry season, they are excellent for cultivating cotton, wheat, and sugarcane. However, their heavy clay nature makes them very difficult to plough using traditional hand hoes.

**4. Desert and Semi-Desert Soils**
* **Distribution:** Found in the Sahara Desert, the Kalahari Desert, and the extreme northern Sahel fringes of Nigeria (e.g., northern Yobe and Katsina).
* **Uses:** Naturally, they only support nomadic pastoralism (livestock grazing) because rainfall is too low for crops. However, with massive irrigation schemes (such as along the Nile Valley in Egypt), these soils can support diverse and intense crop farming.

**5. Alluvial Soils**
* **Distribution:** Found strictly along the floodplains of major rivers, in river valleys, and in deltas. In Nigeria, they dominate the Niger Delta, the Niger-Benue trough, and the floodplains (fadamas) of the Sokoto and Rima rivers.
* **Uses:** They are heavily utilised for intensive agriculture because they are exceptionally fertile and have a good supply of water. They are the primary soils for cultivating swamp rice, sugarcane, and dry-season vegetables.

## Human activities on soils

Humans depend on soil for agriculture, construction, and mining. Our activities can either degrade the soil (reduce its quality) or conserve and improve it.

### Assessing the impact of human activities on soils

**Negative impacts (Soil degradation):**
1. **Deforestation:** Cutting down forests removes the protective canopy and root systems. This exposes the soil to the direct impact of heavy raindrops, leading to severe sheet and gully erosion. This is heavily evident in south-eastern Nigeria (e.g., Agulu-Nanka gullies).
2. **Overgrazing:** In the savanna and Sahel zones, nomadic herdsmen (like the Fulani) graze large herds of cattle. The animals eat the protective grasses down to the roots and their hooves trample and compact the soil, destroying its structure. This leaves the soil bare and highly vulnerable to wind erosion, accelerating desertification.
3. **Bush burning:** A common practice in savanna regions to clear land for farming or flush out game during the dry season. The intense heat destroys the organic humus, kills beneficial soil organisms (like earthworms and nitrogen-fixing microbes), and leaves essential nitrogen to escape as a gas. The remaining ash is easily washed or blown away.
4. **Continuous cropping (Overcultivation):** As populations grow, the traditional system of shifting cultivation (leaving land fallow to recover) is abandoned. Farming the same land year after year exhausts the soil's specific nutrients, leading to barren, unproductive land.
5. **Mining and quarrying:** Extracting minerals severely destroys soil profiles. Open-cast tin mining on the Jos Plateau has left behind a heavily degraded landscape of deep artificial gullies, craters, and barren spoil heaps. In the Niger Delta, petroleum extraction has led to crude oil spills that pollute alluvial soils, making them highly toxic to plant and animal life.

**Positive impacts (Soil conservation and improvement):**
1. **Application of fertilisers:** Adding organic manure (cow dung, compost) improves soil structure and water retention, while inorganic fertilisers (NPK) quickly replace exhausted mineral nutrients.
2. **Afforestation and reforestation:** Planting trees binds the soil together, acts as a windbreak, and increases humus. In northern Nigeria, shelterbelts (rows of trees) are planted to protect the soil from wind erosion and halt desert encroachment.
3. **Terracing and contour ploughing:** On steep slopes, cutting step-like terraces or ploughing horizontally across the slope (rather than up and down) slows down the flow of surface runoff, drastically reducing water erosion.
4. **Irrigation and drainage:** Supplying artificial water to dry soils (e.g., the Kano River Irrigation Project) turns unproductive land into fertile agricultural zones. Conversely, draining swamps allows waterlogged soils to be aerated and farmed.
5. **Cover cropping and crop rotation:** Planting spreading crops (like melon or cowpea) covers the soil, preventing direct raindrop impact and reducing evaporation. Rotating different crops prevents the total exhaustion of specific soil nutrients.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Humus** | Dark, fully decomposed organic matter that improves soil fertility and structure. |
| **Soil Texture** | The relative proportions of sand, silt, and clay particles in a soil. |
| **Soil Structure** | The way individual soil particles group together to form larger aggregates or peds. |
| **Soil Profile** | A vertical cross-section of the soil showing all its distinct layers from the surface to the bedrock. |
| **Soil Horizon** | A specific horizontal layer within a soil profile, characterised by distinct physical and chemical properties. |
| **Eluviation** | The washing out and downward movement of soluble minerals and clay from the upper soil layers (A horizon). |
| **Illuviation** | The accumulation of leached materials (minerals and clay) in the lower soil layers (B horizon). |
| **Laterization** | A soil-forming process in hot, wet tropics where extreme leaching removes silica, leaving iron and aluminium oxides that turn the soil red. |
| **Calcification** | A process in dry regions where evaporation draws calcium salts up to the soil surface via capillary action. |
| **Vertisols** | Heavy, dark clay soils (black cotton soils) that expand when wet and crack deeply when dry. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Climate is the most active factor:** If a JAMB question asks for the most important or most active factor in soil formation, the answer is usually **climate**.
* **Eluviation vs Illuviation:** Eluviation happens in the A horizon (washing out). Illuviation happens in the B horizon (accumulating).
* **Laterite soils are not naturally fertile:** Because they are found in lush rainforests, students assume lateritic soils are highly fertile. In reality, they are heavily leached, acidic, and poor in nutrients. The forest survives by relying on the rapid decay of leaf litter on the surface.
* **Texture vs Structure:** Texture is about particle size (sand, silt, clay). Structure is about arrangement (crumb, blocky). "Porosity" refers to the empty spaces.
* **Black Cotton Soil behaviour:** Memorise the phrase "expands/sticky when wet, shrinks/cracks when dry". This is the defining trait of a Vertisol (Tropical black earth).
* **Bush burning kills, not cures:** While farmers use bush burning to clear land, JAMB geography almost always tests it as a **negative** practice because it destroys humus and soil organisms.

## Quick summary

1. Soil is a mixture of mineral matter (45%), water (25%), air (25%), and organic matter (5%).
2. Soil properties include texture (proportion of sand, silt, clay), structure (clumping into peds), colour, and pH. Loam is the ideal texture for farming.
3. The five factors of soil formation are climate (most active), living organisms, parent material, topography, and time.
4. Soil formation processes include weathering, humification, leaching (eluviation), illuviation, laterization, and calcification.
5. A typical soil profile consists of the O (organic), A (topsoil/eluviation), B (subsoil/illuviation), C (weathered rock), and D/R (bedrock) horizons.
6. Major tropical soils include laterites (leached, red, infertile), tropical ferruginous soils (less leached, good for cereals), black cotton soils (cracking clays, good for cotton), desert soils (sandy, alkaline), and alluvial soils (highly fertile river deposits).
7. Human activities can degrade soils through overgrazing, deforestation, continuous cropping, mining, and bush burning. Soils can be conserved through terracing, contour ploughing, afforestation, and the use of fertilisers.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following components makes up the largest percentage of an ideal loam soil?**
- A. Organic matter
- B. Soil air
- C. Soil water
- D. Mineral matter

**Answer: D.** Mineral matter makes up 45% of an ideal soil, while water and air each make up 25%, and organic matter accounts for 5%.

**2. The physical property of a soil that refers to the relative proportions of sand, silt, and clay is its**
- A. structure
- B. texture
- C. profile
- D. porosity

**Answer: B.** Soil texture is defined by particle sizes (sand, silt, clay). A common mistake is choosing structure, which refers to how these particles clump together into aggregates.

**3. The two most active factors in the process of soil formation are**
- A. parent material and time
- B. climate and living organisms
- C. topography and time
- D. parent material and topography

**Answer: B.** Climate (rainfall and temperature) and living organisms (vegetation and microbes) actively drive the chemical and physical changes that create soil from the passive parent material.

**4. The downward washing of soluble minerals and fine clay particles from the upper layer of the soil is called**
- A. illuviation
- B. calcification
- C. eluviation
- D. salinization

**Answer: C.** Eluviation is the exiting or washing out of materials from the topsoil. A common mistake is choosing illuviation, which is the exact opposite (the washing *in* or accumulation of these materials in the subsoil).

**5. In a typical soil profile, the zone of accumulation of leached materials is the**
- A. O horizon
- B. A horizon
- C. B horizon
- D. C horizon

**Answer: C.** The B horizon (subsoil) is the zone of illuviation, where clay, iron, and aluminium leached from the A horizon accumulate.

**6. The red or reddish-brown colour of lateritic soils in the tropics is mainly due to the high concentration of**
- A. decomposed organic matter
- B. calcium carbonate
- C. sodium chloride
- D. iron and aluminium oxides

**Answer: D.** Intense leaching (laterization) in hot, wet climates washes away silica, leaving behind insoluble iron and aluminium oxides, which give the soil a red or rusty colour.

**7. Which of the following tropical soil types expands heavily when wet and develops deep cracks during the dry season?**
- A. Desert soil
- B. Alluvial soil
- C. Tropical black earth (Vertisol)
- D. Tropical ferruginous soil

**Answer: C.** Tropical black earths (black cotton soils) contain a specific type of heavy clay that swells tremendously when it absorbs water and shrinks deeply when water evaporates.

**8. Highly fertile, young soils deposited by floodwaters in regions such as the Niger-Benue trough and the Niger Delta are known as**
- A. laterites
- B. alluvial soils
- C. podzols
- D. aridisols

**Answer: B.** Alluvial soils are formed by silt, sand, and clay deposited by rivers. Because they are young and constantly replenished, they lack distinct horizons and are exceptionally fertile.

**9. A common negative human impact on soils in the savanna regions of Nigeria, which destroys humus and kills soil microorganisms, is**
- A. bush burning
- B. contour ploughing
- C. afforestation
- D. crop rotation

**Answer: A.** Bush burning generates intense heat that burns up organic matter (humus) and destroys the beneficial biological life in the topsoil, leaving the soil bare and infertile. 

**10. Severe soil degradation in the form of deep gullies and barren spoil heaps on the Jos Plateau is primarily caused by**
- A. overgrazing by cattle
- B. petroleum exploration
- C. continuous cropping
- D. open-cast tin mining

**Answer: D.** Decades of open-cast tin mining on the Jos Plateau involved digging up large areas of land, destroying the soil profile and leaving behind large craters and mounds of sterile rock (spoil heaps). Petroleum exploration degrades soils primarily in the Niger Delta.
