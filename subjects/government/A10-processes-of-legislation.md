## Legislative Enactments

A **legislative enactment** is a law made by a body that has the authority to make laws. In government, the names of laws change depending on **who** makes them and the **type of government** (civilian or military) in power at the time.

The syllabus requires you to understand five main types of legislative enactments: Acts, Decrees, Edicts, Bye-laws, and Delegated Legislation.

### 1. Acts of Parliament

An **Act** is a law passed by the central or federal legislature (parliament) in a democratic, civilian government, and signed by the Head of State.
- **Who makes it:** The supreme legislative body. In Nigeria, this is the **National Assembly** (composed of the Senate and the House of Representatives). In Britain, it is the Parliament.
- **When is it made:** During democratic, civilian rule.
- **Status:** An Act applies to the whole country (or specific parts if stated). Under democratic rule, Acts are subordinate to the Constitution. If an Act contradicts the Constitution, the courts can declare it null and void.
- **Example:** The Electoral Act (which guides how INEC conducts elections in Nigeria).

### 2. Decrees

A **Decree** is a law made by the Federal Military Government.
- **Who makes it:** The highest ruling military council (for example, the Supreme Military Council or Armed Forces Ruling Council during Nigeria's past military regimes). It is signed by the Military Head of State.
- **When is it made:** Only during military rule.
- **Status:** Under a military regime, a Decree is the **supreme law of the land**. It is superior even to the Constitution (parts of the Constitution are usually suspended or modified by military rulers). A Decree cannot be challenged in a court of law.
- **Example:** The Land Use Decree of 1978.

### 3. Edicts

An **Edict** is a law made by a State Military Government.
- **Who makes it:** The Military Governor or Military Administrator of a state.
- **When is it made:** Only during military rule.
- **Status:** An Edict applies only within that specific state. It is subordinate to a federal Decree. If an Edict contradicts a Decree, the Edict becomes invalid.
- **Example:** A state environmental sanitation Edict during military rule.
- **Note:** In a civilian democracy, laws made by the State House of Assembly are simply called **State Laws**, not Edicts.

### 4. Bye-laws

A **Bye-law** is a law made by a local government authority or a public corporation/parastatal for its specific local area or to regulate its specific services.
- **Who makes it:** Local Government Councils, statutory corporations (like the Nigerian Railway Corporation), or university governing councils.
- **Status:** Bye-laws apply only to the local area or to the specific institution. They are subordinate to Acts (federal laws) and State Laws. They must not conflict with higher laws.
- **Example:** A local government bye-law banning street trading, or a regulation demanding motor park levies.

### Summary comparison of legislative enactments

| Feature | Act | Decree | Edict | Bye-law |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Made by** | Parliament / National Assembly | Federal Military Government | State Military Government | Local Government / Public Corporation |
| **System of Government** | Democratic (Civilian) | Military | Military | Both Civilian and Military |
| **Jurisdiction** | The whole country | The whole country | A specific state | A specific local area or institution |
| **Supremacy** | Subordinate to the Constitution | Supreme (overrides the Constitution) | Subordinate to a Decree | Subordinate to Acts and State Laws |

### 5. Delegated Legislation

**Delegated legislation** (also known as subordinate legislation) is law made by bodies other than the legislature, using powers given to them by the legislature.

Because the parliament (legislature) cannot make every single rule for the country, it passes a general law called an **Enabling Act** (or Parent Act). This Act grants power to ministers, local governments, or public corporations to make detailed rules, regulations, and orders to carry out the law.

#### Types of Delegated Legislation

1. **Statutory Instruments / Ministerial Orders:** Rules and regulations made by government Ministers or department heads. For example, the Minister of Health can make rules regarding drug manufacturing standards under a Health Act.
2. **Bye-laws:** As explained above, rules made by local governments or public corporations for their local areas.
3. **Orders-in-Council:** These are orders issued by the Head of State or the executive council (the cabinet), usually during a state of emergency or for matters of national security.
4. **Provisional Orders:** Temporary rules made by a minister or agency that must be confirmed by the parliament later before they become permanent.

#### Reasons for Delegated Legislation

Why does the parliament give its law-making power to others?
- **To save parliamentary time:** Parliament has too much work and cannot debate every minor administrative rule. It focuses on general principles and leaves the details to the executive.
- **Technicality of modern laws:** Many modern issues are highly technical (e.g., aviation safety, taxation rates, chemical standards). Members of parliament may not be experts, so they leave the technical details to experts in the ministries and agencies.
- **Emergencies:** Parliament takes a long time to pass laws. In an emergency (like an epidemic or natural disaster), ministers need the power to make rules immediately.
- **Flexibility:** It is much faster and easier for a minister to amend a regulation to suit changing conditions than it is for parliament to amend an Act.

#### Disadvantages of Delegated Legislation

- **Undemocratic:** Laws are made by civil servants and experts who are not elected by the people.
- **Loss of parliamentary control:** Parliament may accidentally give away too much power to the executive branch, making the executive too powerful.
- **Lack of publicity:** Unlike debates in parliament, which are public, delegated legislation is often made privately in government offices. Citizens might break rules they did not know existed.
- **Sub-delegation:** A minister given power by parliament might pass that power down to a junior civil servant, further removing the process from democratic accountability.

#### Control of Delegated Legislation

To prevent the abuse of delegated power, it must be controlled:
1. **Parliamentary Control:** Parliament controls it by passing the Enabling Act. Parliament can amend or revoke this Act if the power is abused. Also, rules made by ministers often have to be "laid on the table" in parliament for review before they take effect.
2. **Judicial Control (The Courts):** This is the most important control. If a minister or agency makes a rule that goes beyond the powers given in the Enabling Act, a citizen can take them to court. The court can declare the rule ***ultra vires*** (a Latin term meaning "beyond the powers") and strike it down as null and void.
3. **Executive / Ministerial Control:** Ministers supervise the civil servants and boards under their ministries to ensure they do not abuse rule-making powers.
4. **Public Opinion and the Press:** Outcry from the media and pressure groups can force the government to withdraw an unpopular regulation.

## The Process of Making Laws (How a Bill Becomes a Law)

**Objective i: analyse the processes involved in the making of laws.**

In a democratic government, the process of making an Act of Parliament is careful and deliberate to ensure a proposed law is thoroughly examined before it binds the citizens.

A proposed law presented to the legislature for debate and approval is called a **Bill**. Only after a bill is passed by the legislature and signed by the Head of State does it become an **Act** (a law).

### Types of Bills

1. **Public Bill:** A bill introduced by the executive branch (usually a Minister) that deals with matters of general public interest affecting the whole country (e.g., a National Minimum Wage Bill).
2. **Private Member’s Bill:** A bill introduced by an ordinary member of the legislature (a senator or representative) who is not a government minister. It also deals with public matters but originates from a lawmaker, not the executive cabinet.
3. **Private Bill:** A bill that affects a specific individual, a specific group, or a specific local authority, rather than the general public (e.g., a bill to give a specific public corporation special powers).
4. **Money Bill:** A bill that deals with taxation, government spending, or the management of public funds. The most common example is the **Appropriation Bill** (the national budget).

### The Stages of Passing a Bill

The process below describes how a bill becomes a law in a typical democratic legislature, using Nigeria's National Assembly (which is bicameral, meaning it has two houses: the Senate and the House of Representatives) as the standard example.

#### Stage 1: Drafting the Bill
Before a bill goes to parliament, it must be written in legal language. This is usually done by legal experts (legal draftsmen) in the Ministry of Justice or by the legal department of the legislature.

#### Stage 2: First Reading
This is the formal introduction of the bill to the legislature. The Clerk of the House reads out the **short title** of the bill. Copies of the bill are distributed to the lawmakers so they can study it. **No debate takes place at this stage.**

#### Stage 3: Second Reading
This is the most critical stage. The person sponsoring the bill explains its purpose, benefits, and general principles. The lawmakers **debate the general merits and demerits** of the bill. After the debate, a vote is taken. If the majority votes in favour, the bill moves to the next stage. If they vote against it, the bill "dies" and goes no further.

#### Stage 4: Committee Stage
The bill is sent to a committee for detailed, microscopic examination. The committee could be a "Standing Committee" (dealing with specific subjects like Health or Education), a "Select Committee" (formed specially for the bill), or occasionally a "Committee of the Whole House" (where all members act as a committee for very important bills).
- The committee examines the bill **clause by clause** (line by line).
- The committee has the power to make **amendments** (changes), add new clauses, or remove bad ones.
- In Nigeria and many democracies, the committee often holds **Public Hearings**, inviting experts, pressure groups, and ordinary citizens to give their opinions (memoranda) on the bill.

#### Stage 5: Report Stage
The committee reports back to the whole House, presenting the amended version of the bill. The lawmakers examine the committee's report and the amendments. Further amendments can be made by the whole House at this stage.

#### Stage 6: Third Reading
This is the final stage of the bill in the House. The lawmakers look at the final, polished version of the bill. **No major debates or significant changes** are allowed at this stage; only minor verbal or spelling corrections can be made. A final vote is taken. If it passes, the bill has completed its journey in that House.

#### Stage 7: Concurrence (Passage in the Second Chamber)
In a **bicameral legislature** (like Nigeria, with a Senate and House of Representatives), a bill must pass through **both** houses. If a bill starts and passes in the House of Representatives, it must be sent to the Senate, where it goes through the same stages (First Reading to Third Reading).
- **Joint Conference Committee:** If the Senate and the House of Representatives pass different versions of the same bill, a Joint Conference Committee (made up of members from both houses) is formed to harmonize the differences and create one agreed-upon version.

#### Stage 8: Presidential Assent
Once both chambers pass the exact same version of the bill, a clean copy is sent to the Head of State (the President). If the President agrees with the bill, they sign it. This signature is called **Assent**. The moment the President gives assent, the bill officially becomes an **Act of Parliament** (a law).

#### Veto and Overriding a Veto
- **Veto:** If the President dislikes the bill, they can refuse to sign it. This refusal is known as the power of veto.
- **Overriding the Veto:** The parliament is not helpless if the President uses a veto. If the President refuses to sign, the parliament can pass the bill again. In Nigeria, if a **two-thirds (2/3) majority** of both the Senate and the House of Representatives vote to pass the bill again, it overrides the President's veto. The bill becomes law automatically, even without the President's signature.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Enactment** | A law passed by a legislative body or authority. |
| **Act** | A law passed by the parliament (legislature) in a democratic government. |
| **Decree** | The supreme law made by the Federal Military Government during military rule. |
| **Edict** | A law made by a State Military Government, subordinate to a federal Decree. |
| **Bye-law** | A rule made by a local government or public corporation for its specific area or function. |
| **Delegated Legislation** | Law made by bodies other than the legislature (e.g., ministers), using powers granted by an Enabling Act. |
| **Bill** | A proposed law presented to the legislature for debate and approval. |
| **Assent** | The signing of a passed bill by the Head of State, turning it into law. |
| **Veto** | The refusal of the Head of State to sign a bill into law. |
| ***Ultra vires*** | A Latin term meaning "beyond the powers"; used by courts to strike down delegated legislation that exceeds legal limits. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Decrees vs. Edicts:** A very common mistake is confusing these two. Always remember: **Decrees** are Federal (made by the Head of State/military council), while **Edicts** are State-level (made by the Military Governor). Both apply only to military regimes.
- **Public Bill vs. Private Member's Bill:** A "Public Bill" is introduced by the government (the executive/Ministers). A "Private Member's Bill" is introduced by an ordinary lawmaker. Do not let the word "Private" trick you into thinking it is for a private citizen.
- **First Reading trick:** JAMB often asks what happens at the First Reading. Remember that **no debate** takes place; the title is merely read.
- **Second Reading vs. Committee Stage:** Know the difference perfectly. The **Second Reading** is for debating the **general principles**. The **Committee Stage** is for **detailed, clause-by-clause** examination and amendments.
- ***Ultra vires*:** This is the key term for the judicial control of delegated legislation. If you see a question about courts stopping ministers from abusing law-making power, look for *ultra vires*.

## Quick summary

1. Legislative enactments take different names depending on the government: Acts (democratic parliament), Decrees (federal military), Edicts (state military), and Bye-laws (local governments).
2. Delegated legislation allows bodies like government ministers and agencies to make rules, saving parliamentary time and handling technical details.
3. Delegated legislation is controlled by parliament, the executive, and the courts (who can declare it *ultra vires* if it exceeds granted powers).
4. A Bill is a proposed law. Public bills are brought by ministers, while Private Member's bills are brought by ordinary lawmakers.
5. The law-making process goes through several stages: First Reading (formal introduction), Second Reading (debate on principles), Committee Stage (detailed scrutiny), Report Stage, and Third Reading (final passage).
6. In a bicameral legislature, both houses must pass the exact same version of the bill.
7. A bill officially becomes an Act when the President signs it (Assent). The legislature can override a presidential Veto with a two-thirds majority.

## Practice questions

**1. A law made by the Federal Military Government in Nigeria is called a**

- A. Constitution
- B. Decree
- C. Bye-law
- D. Edict

**Answer: B.** A Decree is the supreme law made by the highest military authority at the federal level. Option D is a common misunderstanding; an Edict is made by a state military government, not federal.

**2. Which of the following is a primary reason for the use of delegated legislation?**

- A. To make the executive arm more powerful than the legislature
- B. To save parliamentary time
- C. To prevent the courts from acting on technical issues
- D. To ensure laws are made in total secrecy

**Answer: B.** Parliament has a heavy workload, so it delegates the power to make detailed rules to ministers and agencies to save time and focus on major issues.

**3. The stage in the passage of a bill where its general principles and merits are fiercely debated is the**

- A. First reading
- B. Committee stage
- C. Second reading
- D. Third reading

**Answer: C.** The Second Reading is the crucial stage where lawmakers debate the general advantages and disadvantages of the proposed law. Students often confuse this with the Committee stage (Option B), which is for detailed line-by-line checks, not general debate.

**4. A proposed law introduced by a Minister on behalf of the executive branch is called a**

- A. Private bill
- B. Public bill
- C. Private member's bill
- D. Money bill

**Answer: B.** A Public Bill is sponsored by the executive (government ministers) and deals with national issues. A Private member's bill (Option C) is introduced by an ordinary member of parliament, which is a frequent point of confusion.

**5. The power of the judiciary to declare delegated legislation null and void because it exceeds statutory limits is based on the principle of**

- A. Habeas corpus
- B. Ultra vires
- C. Mandamus
- D. Rule of law

**Answer: B.** *Ultra vires* means "beyond the powers." If a minister makes a rule outside the powers granted by the Enabling Act, the court uses this principle to cancel it.

**6. The refusal of the President to sign a bill passed by the legislature is known as**

- A. Prorogation
- B. Filibustering
- C. Assent
- D. Veto

**Answer: D.** A Veto is the executive's power to refuse to sign a bill. Assent (Option C) is the exact opposite—signing the bill into law.

**7. A law enacted by a local government council for its area of authority is known as a**

- A. Decree
- B. Bye-law
- C. Edict
- D. Statutory instrument

**Answer: B.** Local governments and statutory corporations make Bye-laws to regulate their specific areas, such as markets and motor parks.

**8. Detailed, clause-by-clause examination of a bill takes place at the**

- A. First reading
- B. Second reading
- C. Committee stage
- D. Report stage

**Answer: C.** In the Committee stage, a smaller group of lawmakers examines the bill line by line and can make specific amendments.

**9. Under a democratic system of government, an Act of Parliament is subordinate only to the**

- A. Supreme Court
- B. President
- C. Constitution
- D. Executive cabinet

**Answer: C.** The Constitution is the supreme law of the land in a democracy. Any Act of Parliament that contradicts the Constitution is invalid.

**10. What happens if the President refuses to give assent to a bill, but the legislature still wants it to become law?**

- A. The bill is completely abandoned and cannot be brought back
- B. The Supreme Court must sign it on the President's behalf
- C. The legislature can override the veto with a two-thirds majority vote
- D. The citizens must vote on the bill in a national referendum

**Answer: C.** The legislature is not helpless against a veto. If both houses pass the bill again with a two-thirds majority, it automatically becomes law without the President's signature.
