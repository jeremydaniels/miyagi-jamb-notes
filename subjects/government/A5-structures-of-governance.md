# JAMB Government Notes: Structures of Governance

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A, Topic 5 of the JAMB Government syllabus: Structures of Governance. It covers the geographic distribution of power within a state.

This file covers the following content items:
- **a. Unitary** – features, reasons for adoption, merits and demerits
- **b. Federal** – features, reasons for adoption, merits and demerits
- **c. Confederal** - features, reasons for adoption, merits and demerits

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
1. compare the various political structures of governance; (Objective i)
2. understand the reasons for the adoption of any of these structures of governance. (Objective ii)

**Note on textbook variations:** In political science, terms like "forms", "systems", and "structures" of government are sometimes used differently by different authors. Some textbooks call unitary and federal systems "types of constitution" or "systems of government". However, for your JAMB UTME, always follow the syllabus classification: Unitary, Federal, and Confederal are grouped as **Structures of Governance**. This refers to how political power is shared geographically between a central national government and its component parts (states, regions, or provinces).

## What is a Structure of Governance?

A structure of governance describes how political power and authority are distributed geographically within a country. It looks at the relationship between the **central (national) government** located in the capital and the **component units** (which might be called states, regions, provinces, or local governments). 

Depending on where the ultimate power (sovereignty) lies, a country's structure will fall into one of three categories:
1. **Unitary:** Power is concentrated at the center.
2. **Federal:** Power is shared between the center and the component units.
3. **Confederal:** Power is concentrated in the component units, which create a weak center.

## Unitary Structure of Governance

**Content a: Unitary – features, reasons for adoption, merits and demerits**

A **unitary structure of governance** is one in which all constitutional powers and authority are concentrated in a single central government. The central government is supreme. It may choose to create local or regional governments and delegate (give) some powers to them for administrative convenience, but it can take those powers back or abolish the local governments at any time.

Examples of countries with a unitary structure include **Britain (the UK), France, Ghana, The Gambia, and Cameroon**.

### Features of a Unitary Structure
1. **Concentration of power:** All governmental power (executive, legislative, and judicial) is concentrated in the central government.
2. **Subordinate component units:** Local authorities or regions exist only at the mercy of the central government. They do not derive their powers directly from the constitution.
3. **Single constitution:** The country operates under one constitution that applies uniformly across the entire nation.
4. **Flexible constitution:** Unitary constitutions are often flexible (easy to amend) or even unwritten, as seen in Britain.
5. **No constitutional sharing of power:** There are no "Exclusive" or "Concurrent" legislative lists sharing power between the center and states.
6. **Single citizenship:** Citizens owe allegiance only to the central national government.

### Reasons for adopting a Unitary Structure
**Objective ii: understand the reasons for the adoption of any of these structures of governance.**

1. **Small geographical size:** A small country (like The Gambia or Ghana) can be effectively administered from one central location without needing complex state governments.
2. **Homogeneous population:** Countries where the people share a common language, culture, religion, and history (a homogeneous society) do not need regional autonomy to protect minority identities.
3. **Need for strong national unity:** A unitary system prevents ethnic divisions and the threat of secession, forcing all citizens to look to the center.
4. **Lack of resources:** Poorer countries may adopt it because it is much cheaper to run one central government than to fund multiple state governments.
5. **Colonial legacy:** Many African countries retained the unitary structure handed down to them by their colonial masters (e.g., France and Britain are unitary, and they governed their colonies as such).

### Merits of a Unitary Structure
- **Cheaper to run:** There is no duplication of government ministries, legislatures, or civil services at the regional level, saving public funds.
- **Promotes national unity:** A single set of laws, policies, and educational systems helps unify the citizens and reduces tribal or regional loyalties.
- **Quick decision-making:** Because power is centralized, the government can pass laws and respond to emergencies rapidly without consulting regional state governments.
- **Uniformity of laws and development:** Policies and laws are uniform across the country, meaning a citizen receives the same standard of services regardless of where they live.
- **No border or resource disputes:** Since there are no sovereign states within the country, there are no internal disputes over state boundaries or resource control.

### Demerits of a Unitary Structure
- **Risk of dictatorship:** Concentrating all power in one central authority can lead to authoritarian rule, as there are no strong state governments to check the center's power.
- **Overburdened central government:** The central government can become overworked handling both national issues (like foreign policy) and local issues (like filling potholes), leading to inefficiency.
- **Ignores minority interests:** In countries with diverse populations, a unitary system may force the majority culture on minorities, leading to marginalization and conflict.
- **Slow local development:** Because decisions are made in a distant capital, the unique developmental needs of rural or distant areas may be neglected.
- **Kills local initiative:** Citizens at the grassroots level have little say in how they are governed, which discourages local political participation.

## Federal Structure of Governance

**Content b: Federal – features, reasons for adoption, merits and demerits**

A **federal structure of governance (federalism)** is a political arrangement where constitutional power is divided between a central (federal) government and component units (such as states, regions, or provinces). In this system, neither the central government nor the state governments can destroy the other; both derive their powers directly from a supreme constitution.

Examples of countries with a federal structure include **Nigeria, the United States of America (USA), Canada, Australia, and Germany**.

### Features of a Federal Structure
1. **Constitutional division of power:** Power is shared using legislative lists. Usually, there is an **Exclusive List** (for the federal government only, e.g., defense, currency), a **Concurrent List** (for both federal and state governments, e.g., education, agriculture), and a **Residual List** (powers not listed, usually reserved for the states).
2. **Written and rigid constitution:** To prevent one level of government from easily changing the rules to cheat the other, the constitution must be written down and very difficult to amend (rigid).
3. **Supremacy of the constitution:** The constitution is the highest law of the land. Any law made by the federal or state government that contradicts it is invalid.
4. **Independent judiciary:** There is a supreme court with the power of judicial review to settle disputes between the federal government and the states.
5. **Bicameral legislature:** The central legislature usually has two houses (e.g., the Senate and the House of Representatives in Nigeria). The upper house ensures that all states are represented equally, regardless of their population.
6. **Duplication of government organs:** There is an executive, legislature, and judiciary at both the federal and state levels.

### Reasons for adopting a Federal Structure
**Objective ii: understand the reasons for the adoption of any of these structures of governance.**

1. **Large geographical size:** A vast country (like the USA, Canada, or Nigeria) is too large to be governed effectively from one single capital city.
2. **Heterogeneous population (Diversity):** Countries with diverse ethnicities, languages, and religions use federalism to allow different groups to govern their local affairs while remaining part of one country.
3. **Fear of domination:** Federalism protects minority groups from being dominated by larger ethnic groups by giving them their own states and local autonomy.
4. **Economic advantages:** Independent regions may decide to federate to pool their resources together for greater economic power and a larger shared market.
5. **Security and defense:** Weak independent states may come together to form a strong federal union to defend themselves against external aggression.
6. **To bring government closer to the people:** State and local governments understand local problems better than a distant federal capital.

### Merits of a Federal Structure
- **Caters to local differences:** It allows diverse groups to preserve their unique cultures, laws, and traditions within their own states.
- **Prevents dictatorship:** Power is dispersed. The federal government cannot easily become a dictatorship because the states have their own constitutional powers and armed forces (in some federations like the USA).
- **Encourages healthy competition:** States can compete in terms of economic development, education, and infrastructure, which drives national progress.
- **Brings government closer to the people:** Citizens can easily participate in state and local politics.
- **Reduces the burden on the center:** The central government handles only major national issues (defense, foreign affairs), leaving local issues to the states.

### Demerits of a Federal Structure
- **Very expensive to run:** Duplicating the executive, legislature, judiciary, and civil service across 36 states and the federal government (as in Nigeria) consumes a massive amount of national wealth.
- **Causes friction and conflicts:** Constant disputes arise between the central government and states over issues like revenue allocation, resource control, and boundary adjustments.
- **Slow decision-making:** Passing nationwide laws or amending the constitution requires the agreement of the federal legislature and a majority of the state legislatures, which takes a very long time.
- **Threat of secession:** Component states may become too powerful and attempt to break away from the country (e.g., the Biafran attempt to secede from Nigeria in 1967).
- **Uneven development:** Because states have different resources and leadership, some states will develop rapidly while others remain poor, leading to inequality among citizens.

## Confederal Structure of Governance

**Content c: Confederal - features, reasons for adoption, merits and demerits**

A **confederal structure of governance (Confederation)** is a loose political union in which sovereign, independent states come together for a specific, limited purpose (such as mutual defense, foreign policy, or trade). 

In a confederation, the **component states are supreme**, and the central government is very weak. The central authority only has the powers that the states willingly lend to it, and it usually relies on the states for its budget and military forces. 

*Note on examples:* True confederations are very rare today because they usually collapse or evolve into federations. The **United States** started as a confederation (1781–1789) before becoming a federation. In Africa, the **Senegambia Confederation** (a union between Senegal and The Gambia from 1982 to 1989) is a historical example. Organizations like **ECOWAS** and the **African Union (AU)** operate on confederal principles, though they are international organizations, not single countries. Switzerland is historically called a confederation but operates as a federal state today.

### Features of a Confederal Structure
1. **Weak central authority:** The central government has very little power and cannot enforce its laws directly on the citizens. It must go through the state governments.
2. **Supremacy of component units:** The member states retain their full sovereignty and independence.
3. **Right of secession:** Any member state is legally free to leave (secede from) the confederation at any time.
4. **Voluntary contributions:** The central government has no direct power to collect taxes from citizens. It relies on voluntary financial contributions from the member states.
5. **No direct allegiance:** Citizens owe their primary allegiance to their individual states, not to the central confederal government.
6. **Separate armed forces:** Each member state usually maintains its own independent military.

### Reasons for adopting a Confederal Structure
**Objective ii: understand the reasons for the adoption of any of these structures of governance.**

1. **Mutual defense:** Sovereign states may form a confederation to create a combined military alliance against a common, powerful enemy.
2. **Economic cooperation:** States may unite to remove trade barriers, use a common currency, or share a larger market (e.g., the original goal of the Senegambia Confederation).
3. **Desire to retain sovereignty:** States that want the benefits of a union (like free trade) but refuse to give up their political independence and identity will choose a confederation over a federation.
4. **A transitional step:** Sometimes, states form a confederation as a temporary testing ground to see if they can eventually trust each other enough to form a stronger federal union.

### Merits of a Confederal Structure
- **Prevents central dictatorship:** It is impossible for the central government to become dictatorial because it has no independent army and no direct tax revenue.
- **Protects local identities:** Member states keep their complete independence, distinct cultures, and full control over their internal affairs.
- **No forced unity:** Because there is a constitutional right of secession, states are not forced to remain in a union that no longer benefits them.
- **Economic benefits without political loss:** States can enjoy the benefits of pooled economic resources and free trade without losing their sovereignty.

### Demerits of a Confederal Structure
- **Extreme political instability:** Because states can veto central decisions or secede at any time, confederations are highly unstable and tend to collapse quickly (e.g., Senegambia).
- **Financial weakness of the center:** The central government is always poor because it cannot levy direct taxes and states often fail to pay their agreed contributions.
- **Poor enforcement of laws:** The central government cannot arrest or punish citizens directly; it must beg the state governments to enforce confederal policies.
- **Lack of unified foreign policy:** Member states often pursue conflicting foreign policies, making the confederation look weak and divided on the international stage.

## Comparing the Political Structures

**Objective i: compare the various political structures of governance.**

The table below contrasts how power is handled in the three structures. Understanding these differences is highly tested in the JAMB exam.

| Feature | Unitary | Federal | Confederal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Location of Sovereignty** | Concentrated in the central government. | Shared between the center and the states. | Vested in the independent component states. |
| **Nature of the Constitution** | Usually flexible, sometimes unwritten (e.g., UK). | Must be written and rigid. | Written, often in the form of a treaty. |
| **Status of Component Units** | Subordinate. Created by the center and can be abolished by it. | Coordinate. Derive power from the constitution, cannot be abolished by the center. | Supreme. They create the center and can dissolve it. |
| **Right of Secession** | Not allowed (illegal). | Not allowed (illegal). | Fully allowed (constitutional right). |
| **Focus of Citizen Allegiance** | Citizens owe allegiance to the central national government. | Dual allegiance (to both state and federal governments). | Allegiance is to the individual component state. |
| **Division of Powers (Lists)** | None (no constitutional sharing). | Strict division (Exclusive, Concurrent, Residual). | None (States hold all power, delegate specific tasks to the center). |
| **Best suited for** | Small, homogeneous countries. | Large, diverse (heterogeneous) countries. | Independent nations seeking temporary/limited alliances. |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Structure of Governance** | The way political power is shared geographically between the central government and component units. |
| **Unitary System** | A system where all constitutional power is concentrated in a single central government. |
| **Federalism** | A system where power is constitutionally shared between a central government and component states. |
| **Confederation** | A loose union of sovereign states with a very weak central authority. |
| **Component Units** | The smaller geographical divisions of a country (states, regions, provinces, or local governments). |
| **Exclusive List** | A list of powers in a federal constitution reserved strictly for the central (federal) government. |
| **Concurrent List** | A list of powers in a federal constitution that both the central and state governments can exercise. |
| **Residual List** | Powers not listed in the exclusive or concurrent lists, usually left for the state governments in a federation. |
| **Secession** | The act of a state or region breaking away from a country to form its own independent nation. |
| **Rigid Constitution** | A constitution that requires a long, complex, and difficult process to amend. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Federal vs. Confederal:** A very common mistake is confusing these two. Remember: in a **Federation**, the center is strong and states *cannot* legally secede. In a **Confederation**, the states are strong (sovereign), the center is very weak, and states *can* secede.
- **Unitary governments have local councils:** Do not think unitary countries have no local government. They do (like counties in the UK), but those local governments only exist because the central government allows them to, not because they have constitutional power.
- **Division of power is a federal feature:** If a JAMB question asks which system uses "Exclusive and Concurrent lists", the answer is always **Federalism**.
- **Reason for adoption:** Heterogeneous (diverse) societies = Federal. Homogeneous (uniform) societies = Unitary.
- **Secession is illegal in Federal and Unitary systems:** Only Confederal systems recognize a legal right to secede.

## Quick summary

1. A structure of governance describes how power is geographically distributed between the national government and local/state governments.
2. In a Unitary structure, all constitutional power is held by the central government. It is best for small, homogeneous countries because it is cheap and promotes unity, but it risks dictatorship and ignores minorities.
3. In a Federal structure, power is constitutionally shared between the center and states using legislative lists. It is best for large, diverse countries (like Nigeria) to prevent domination, though it is very expensive and complex to run.
4. Federal constitutions must be written and rigid, with an independent judiciary to settle disputes between the federal and state governments.
5. In a Confederal structure, independent sovereign states form a loose union. The states are stronger than the center and have the right to secede. It is used for defense or economic cooperation but is highly unstable.
6. A major difference among the three is where sovereignty lies: at the center (Unitary), shared (Federal), or in the component states (Confederal).

## Practice questions

**1. A political system in which power is concentrated at the center is known as a**
- A. confederal system
- B. unitary system
- C. federal system
- D. presidential system

**Answer: B.** A unitary system concentrates all constitutional power and authority in a single central government.

**2. Which of the following is a key feature of a federal structure?**
- A. The central government can abolish state governments.
- B. The constitution is usually unwritten and flexible.
- C. Constitutional division of powers between the center and component units.
- D. Component units have the constitutional right to secede.

**Answer: C.** Federalism is defined by the constitutional sharing of power between the federal government and state governments. (Option D describes a confederation).

**3. A country with a highly heterogeneous population and a large landmass is best suited for a**
- A. unitary system
- B. monarchical system
- C. federal system
- D. fascist system

**Answer: C.** A federal system accommodates diverse ethnicities and manages large territories by allowing local groups to govern their own affairs while remaining united.

**4. In a confederation, sovereignty resides with the**
- A. central government
- B. component states
- C. armed forces
- D. electorate only

**Answer: B.** In a confederal structure, the component states are sovereign and supreme. They only delegate limited powers to a weak central authority.

**5. One major disadvantage of a federal system of government is that it**
- A. leads to dictatorship of the center
- B. is very expensive to operate
- C. forces a single religion on the citizens
- D. makes it easy for the military to take over

**Answer: B.** Federalism is expensive because it duplicates government institutions (executives, legislatures, civil services) at both the federal and state levels.

**6. Which of the following countries operates a unitary structure of government?**
- A. Nigeria
- B. United States of America
- C. Ghana
- D. Canada

**Answer: C.** Ghana operates a unitary system, where power is centralized. Nigeria, the USA, and Canada are classic examples of federal systems.

**7. In which structure of governance is the right of secession constitutionally guaranteed?**
- A. Unitary
- B. Federal
- C. Confederal
- D. Parliamentary

**Answer: C.** A confederation is a voluntary union of sovereign states, and member states retain the constitutional right to secede (leave) at any time.

**8. The Concurrent Legislative List in a federal constitution contains matters that**
- A. only the federal government can legislate on
- B. are entirely reserved for traditional rulers
- C. both the federal and state governments can legislate on
- D. only local governments can handle

**Answer: C.** The Concurrent List holds powers shared by both the federal and state governments (e.g., education and agriculture). The mistake students make is confusing it with the Exclusive List, which is for the federal government only.

**9. A major reason for adopting a unitary system of government is to**
- A. promote strong national unity
- B. allow component units to develop at their own pace
- C. encourage ethnic competition
- D. satisfy the demands of traditional rulers

**Answer: A.** Unitary systems enforce uniform laws and policies across the entire country, which helps blend citizens together and promotes strong national unity, preventing tribal or regional divisions.

**10. Unlike a federal system, local authorities in a unitary system**
- A. derive their powers directly from the constitution
- B. have their own independent judiciary
- C. are created and can be abolished by the central government
- D. possess the power to collect import duties

**Answer: C.** In a unitary system, local governments exist only at the mercy of the central government, which delegates power to them for administrative convenience and can take it back at will.
