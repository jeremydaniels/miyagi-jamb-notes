## Meaning of a Constitution

**Content item: Meaning**  
**Objective i: Define constitutions**

A **constitution** is a body of agreed rules, regulations, principles, and conventions that outline how a state is governed. It defines the structure of the government, the powers and limitations of the various arms of government, and the fundamental rights and duties of the citizens.

Think of a constitution as the "rulebook" of a country. Just as a football match needs a rulebook to state how many players are allowed, what counts as a foul, and the referee's powers, a state needs a constitution to guide its leaders and citizens. In Nigeria, the supreme law of the land is the **1999 Constitution (as amended)**. Every other law in Nigeria must align with it; if any other law contradicts the constitution, that law is considered null and void.

## Functions of a Constitution

**Objective i: identify functions of constitutions.**

Why does a state need a constitution? A constitution performs several essential functions:

1. **Defines the structure of government:** It states whether the country will operate a federal or unitary system, and a presidential or parliamentary system.
2. **Allocates powers:** It clearly shares power among the arms of government (Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary) and between different levels of government (Federal, State, and Local).
3. **Protects fundamental human rights:** It lists the rights of the citizens (such as the right to life, freedom of speech, and freedom of religion) and provides legal backing to defend these rights against abuse.
4. **Limits the powers of leaders:** By clearly stating what the government can and cannot do, the constitution prevents leaders from becoming dictators. This principle is known as **constitutionalism**.
5. **States the duties of citizens:** Just as it gives rights, it lists what the country expects from its citizens, such as paying taxes, obeying laws, and defending the nation.
6. **Provides for peaceful change of government:** It sets the rules for elections, tenure of office, and how power is transferred from one administration to the next.
7. **Serves as a symbol of sovereignty:** A constitution shows that a state is independent and capable of governing itself without foreign control.

## Sources of a Constitution

**Content item: Sources**  
**Objective i: identify sources of constitutions.**

Constitutions do not just appear out of nowhere. The rules and principles they contain are drawn from various historical, legal, and cultural sources. The major sources of a constitution include:

### 1. Customs and Traditions
Before modern laws were written, communities were guided by customs and traditions. Many unwritten rules about marriage, inheritance, and traditional leadership that communities have practiced for centuries are incorporated into the constitution.

### 2. Past Constitutions and Historical Documents
New constitutions often borrow heavily from older ones. For example, Nigeria's 1999 Constitution borrowed many features from the 1979 Constitution. Globally, historical documents like the Magna Carta (1215) in Britain strongly influenced the development of human rights in constitutions worldwide.

### 3. Acts of Parliament (Statutes)
These are laws made by the legislature over the years. Important laws dealing with elections, creation of agencies, and citizenship often form part of the constitutional framework, especially in countries with an unwritten constitution.

### 4. Judicial Precedents (Case Laws)
When judges interpret laws in landmark court cases, their judgments become a reference for future cases. These major court decisions (precedents) help shape constitutional law.

### 5. Constitutional Conferences and Drafting Committees
Many modern constitutions are the product of meetings where representatives of the people gather to decide how they want to be governed. For instance, the Nigerian 1979 Constitution was drafted by a Constitution Drafting Committee (CDC) and reviewed by a Constituent Assembly. 

### 6. Conventions
Conventions are unwritten rules, traditions, and political practices that have been accepted as binding over time, even though they are not strictly written in law. In Britain, for instance, many government actions are based purely on convention.

### 7. Intellectual Works of Political Thinkers
The writings and theories of philosophers have greatly influenced constitution-makers. For example, the French philosopher **Baron de Montesquieu** introduced the concept of the *Separation of Powers*, which is a major part of the Nigerian and American constitutions. **A.V. Dicey** popularized the concept of the *Rule of Law*.

### 8. International Treaties and Agreements
When a country signs international agreements (like the UN Declaration of Human Rights or African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights), it often adjusts its constitution to reflect these international standards.

## Types and Nature of Constitutions

**Objective ii: compare the nature of constitutions.**

Constitutions are classified based on their **form** (whether they are in a single document or not) and their **method of amendment** (how easily they can be changed).

### 1. Written Constitution

A **written constitution** is one in which the fundamental laws, principles, and structures of government are codified (written down) into a **single, single document**. 
* **Examples:** Nigeria, the United States of America (USA), Ghana, India.

**Merits (Advantages):**
* **Clear and accessible:** Because it is in one document, citizens and lawyers can easily refer to it when rights are violated or powers are disputed.
* **Reduces ambiguity:** It leaves little room for leaders to twist the rules because everything is clearly spelled out.
* **Protects minority interests:** It clearly states the rights of every group in a diverse country.
* **Promotes stability:** It is usually stable and difficult to change on a whim.

**Demerits (Disadvantages):**
* **Inflexibility:** It can be too rigid, making it difficult to adapt quickly to emergencies or changing times.
* **Frequent litigation:** Because it is written out precisely, politicians and citizens constantly go to court over the exact meaning of words.

### 2. Unwritten Constitution

An **unwritten constitution** is one in which the fundamental laws and principles of government are **not contained in a single document**. 

**Textbook variation / Common mistake:** "Unwritten" does **not** mean that there are no written laws at all. It simply means the rules are scattered across various sources—such as Acts of Parliament, historical documents, judicial precedents, and unwritten conventions—rather than being bound together in one master book called "The Constitution".
* **Examples:** Great Britain (the United Kingdom), New Zealand.

**Merits (Advantages):**
* **Highly flexible:** It can easily adapt to new social changes and emergencies without a long, complex amendment process.
* **Quick decision-making:** The government can easily pass new laws to deal with urgent situations.
* **Grows with the people:** It evolves naturally from the customs, history, and conventions of the people.

**Demerits (Disadvantages):**
* **Ambiguous and uncertain:** Because there is no single document, it can be difficult for ordinary citizens to know their exact rights.
* **Prone to abuse:** A dictatorial government can easily manipulate the laws because they are scattered and easy to change.
* **Poor protection for minorities:** It does not provide the strong, visible guarantees that a written constitution offers to minority groups.

#### Comparison: Written vs. Unwritten Constitutions

| Feature | Written Constitution | Unwritten Constitution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Form** | Contained in a single, codified document. | Scattered across multiple documents, conventions, and customs. |
| **Origin** | Usually drafted by a special assembly or committee at a specific point in time. | Evolves gradually over centuries. |
| **Certainty** | Very clear and precise; easy to consult. | Can be ambiguous; requires deep legal/historical knowledge to consult. |
| **Flexibility** | Usually difficult to amend (rigid). | Very easy to amend (flexible). |
| **Examples** | Nigeria, USA, Ghana. | Great Britain, New Zealand. |

---

### 3. Rigid Constitution

A **rigid constitution** is one that is very difficult to change or amend. It requires a special, strict, and often complicated procedure before any part of it can be altered.
* **Example:** In Nigeria, amending the 1999 Constitution requires a **two-thirds (2/3) majority vote** in both the Senate and the House of Representatives, and it must also be approved by a two-thirds majority of all the State Houses of Assembly in the federation. 
* **Other Examples:** USA, Australia.

**Merits (Advantages):**
* **Prevents arbitrary changes:** A ruling party cannot just change the rules to keep themselves in power.
* **Protects minorities:** The tough amendment process ensures that the majority cannot easily erase the rights of minorities.
* **Fosters stability:** The country's political system remains steady and predictable.
* **Essential for federalism:** In a federal state, a rigid constitution prevents the central government from easily taking away the powers of the state governments.

**Demerits (Disadvantages):**
* **Too slow for emergencies:** The long process can prevent the country from passing urgent, desperately needed reforms.
* **Can lead to political tension or revolution:** If the people desperately want a change but the rigid rules block it, it may cause unrest or military intervention.
* **Expensive and time-consuming:** The amendment process costs the state a lot of time and money.

### 4. Flexible Constitution

A **flexible constitution** is one that is very easy to amend. It does not require any special procedures. It can be changed by the legislature using the same simple process used to pass ordinary, everyday laws (usually a simple majority vote).
* **Examples:** Great Britain, New Zealand.

**Merits (Advantages):**
* **Highly adaptable:** It allows the government to respond quickly to social changes, economic crises, or technological advancements.
* **Prevents revolutions:** Because changes can be made smoothly and easily when the people demand them, there is less frustration that could lead to violence.
* **Cheap and fast:** Amending it does not waste the state's time or resources.

**Demerits (Disadvantages):**
* **Encourages dictatorship:** A charismatic leader or a ruling party with a simple majority can easily rewrite the constitution to serve their own selfish interests.
* **Political instability:** The fundamental laws can change every time a new political party comes into power.
* **Inadequate for federalism:** It cannot properly protect the division of powers between the central and state governments, as the central legislature could easily change the power-sharing arrangement.

#### Comparison: Rigid vs. Flexible Constitutions

| Feature | Rigid Constitution | Flexible Constitution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Amendment Process** | Requires a special, complicated procedure (e.g., 2/3 majority, state approvals). | Requires only the ordinary law-making process (simple majority). |
| **Pace of Change** | Slow to adapt to new situations. | Quick to adapt to new situations. |
| **Stability** | Very stable; difficult to manipulate by rulers. | Unstable; easily altered by the ruling party. |
| **Suitability** | Best suited for diverse, Federal states (e.g., Nigeria, USA). | Best suited for homogeneous, Unitary states (e.g., Britain). |
| **Protection of Minorities**| Strong protection, as changes require widespread agreement. | Weak protection, as a simple majority can change the rules. |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Constitution** | A body of agreed rules and principles that state how a country is governed. |
| **Constitutionalism** | The principle that government power should be strictly limited by the constitution to prevent dictatorship. |
| **Conventions** | Unwritten political practices, traditions, and habits that are accepted as binding rules. |
| **Statute** | A law passed by the legislature (an Act of Parliament). |
| **Judicial Precedent** | A past court decision that serves as a guide or rule for deciding future cases. |
| **Written Constitution** | A constitution codified into one single document. |
| **Unwritten Constitution** | A constitution not contained in a single document, but derived from various sources. |
| **Rigid Constitution** | A constitution that requires a special and difficult procedure to amend. |
| **Flexible Constitution** | A constitution that is easy to amend, using the ordinary law-making process. |
| **Amendment** | The formal process of changing or adding to a constitution. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mistake:** Assuming that an "unwritten constitution" means literally *nothing* is written down. 
  **Correction:** Laws are written down in an unwritten constitution; they are just scattered across various acts, historical documents, and court rulings rather than being bound in a *single document*.
- **Mistake:** Confusing "Written/Unwritten" with "Rigid/Flexible". 
  **Correction:** Written/Unwritten is about **form** (is it in one book?). Rigid/Flexible is about **amendment** (how easy is it to change?). 
- **Exam Tip:** In JAMB, always associate Great Britain (the UK) with the standard example of both an **Unwritten** and a **Flexible** constitution.
- **Exam Tip:** Associate Nigeria and the USA with the standard examples of both a **Written** and a **Rigid** constitution.
- **Exam Tip:** Remember that a rigid constitution is an absolute necessity for a **Federal system** of government, to ensure the central government cannot easily take away the powers of the state governments.

## Quick summary

1. A constitution is the fundamental rulebook of a state, defining government structure, powers, and citizens' rights and duties.
2. The constitution functions to limit arbitrary government power (constitutionalism), share power, and ensure peaceful political succession.
3. Constitutions originate from several sources, including customs, past constitutions, acts of parliament, judicial precedents, conventions, and international treaties.
4. A written constitution is codified in a single document (e.g., Nigeria, USA), making it clear but sometimes inflexible.
5. An unwritten constitution is drawn from scattered sources rather than a single document (e.g., Britain), making it highly flexible but potentially ambiguous.
6. A rigid constitution requires special, difficult procedures (like a two-thirds majority) to amend, which promotes stability and protects minorities.
7. A flexible constitution can be amended easily by a simple majority like an ordinary law, which allows quick adaptation but can be easily abused by dictatorial leaders.

## Practice questions

**1. A constitution whose provisions are not contained in a single document is said to be**
- A. rigid
- B. flexible
- C. unwritten
- D. written

**Answer: C.** An unwritten constitution has its laws and principles scattered across various historical documents, statutes, and conventions, rather than codified in a single document. (Do not confuse it with "flexible", which refers to the amendment process).

**2. One of the main advantages of a written constitution is that it**
- A. can be amended easily by the ruling party
- B. is contained in a single document, reducing ambiguity
- C. is derived mostly from unwritten conventions
- D. is best suited for a unitary state

**Answer: B.** A written constitution is codified in a single document. This makes it clear, accessible, and less likely to be misinterpreted or abused by leaders.

**3. Which of the following countries operates an unwritten constitution?**
- A. United States of America
- B. Great Britain
- C. Nigeria
- D. Ghana

**Answer: B.** Great Britain (the UK) is the standard example of a country with an unwritten and flexible constitution.

**4. The primary purpose of a rigid constitution is to**
- A. ensure that laws are passed very quickly
- B. prevent arbitrary and hasty changes to fundamental laws
- C. allow the executive arm to act without judicial interference
- D. make the document very bulky

**Answer: B.** A rigid constitution has a difficult amendment process to ensure that ruling parties cannot easily alter the fundamental rules of the state for their own selfish interests.

**5. A constitution is flexible if it**
- A. can be amended by a two-thirds majority of the legislature
- B. requires a referendum before any change is made
- C. can be amended through the ordinary law-making process
- D. contains laws that are completely unwritten

**Answer: C.** A flexible constitution requires no special procedures for amendment; it can be changed with a simple majority, just like passing an ordinary everyday law.

**6. Which of the following is NOT a source of a constitution?**
- A. Judicial precedents
- B. Acts of Parliament
- C. Personal manifestos of politicians
- D. Customs and conventions

**Answer: C.** Personal manifestos are promises made by political candidates during elections, not sources of fundamental state law. Judicial precedents, Acts of Parliament, and customs are all valid sources.

**7. A federal system of government MUST have a constitution that is**
- A. written and flexible
- B. unwritten and rigid
- C. written and rigid
- D. unwritten and flexible

**Answer: C.** A federal system requires a written constitution so the division of powers is clear, and a rigid constitution so the central government cannot easily alter the sharing formula to oppress state governments.

**8. When a past court judgment is used as a basis for determining constitutional rules, this source is known as**
- A. an Act of Parliament
- B. a convention
- C. a judicial precedent
- D. a constitutional conference

**Answer: C.** Judicial precedents (or case laws) occur when the decisions of judges in landmark cases establish a rule or interpretation that guides future legal and constitutional issues.

**9. The principle that government should be bound by the dictates of the constitution is known as**
- A. centralization
- B. constitutionalism
- C. the rule of law
- D. sovereignty

**Answer: B.** Constitutionalism is the political principle that government power should be strictly limited by a constitution, preventing dictatorship and arbitrary rule. (While related to the Rule of Law, constitutionalism specifically focuses on limiting government via a constitution).

**10. Which of the following is a major disadvantage of a flexible constitution?**
- A. It causes unnecessary delays during national emergencies
- B. It makes the country overly dependent on foreign laws
- C. It can easily be manipulated by a dictatorial leader in power
- D. It gives too much power to the judiciary

**Answer: C.** Because a flexible constitution is very easy to amend, a charismatic leader or a ruling party with a simple majority can quickly rewrite the fundamental laws to favor themselves or silence opposition.
