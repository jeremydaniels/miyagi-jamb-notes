# JAMB Government Notes: The Military in Nigerian Politics

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B, Topic 12 of the JAMB Government syllabus: The Military in Nigerian Politics. It covers:

*   **a.** Factors that led to military intervention.
*   **b.** Structure of military regimes.
*   **c.** Impact of military rule – political (e.g., creation of states, NYSC, introduction of unitary system / Unification Decree No. 34) and economic (e.g., SAP, DFRRI, Peoples Bank, MAMSER).
*   **d.** Processes of military disengagement.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
1.  Evaluate the reasons given for military intervention.
2.  Assess the achievements of military rule.
3.  Determine the conditions that necessitated withdrawal from the political scene.

Nigeria has a long history of military rule. Out of the first 39 years of its independence (1960 to 1999), the military ruled for 29 years. The military took over power through a **coup d'état** (a sudden, often violent overthrow of a government) on several occasions, starting on January 15, 1966.

## Factors that led to military intervention (Reasons for coups)

**Objective i: evaluate the reasons given for military intervention; Content a: Factors that led to military intervention.**

When the military takes over power, they usually broadcast reasons to the public to justify their actions. However, political scientists evaluate these interventions by looking at both the *stated* reasons and the *hidden* (underlying) factors.

### 1. Political crises and violence
In the First Republic (1960–1966), Nigeria faced severe political crises that threatened to destroy the country. 
*   **The Action Group Crisis (1962):** A split in the ruling party of the Western Region led to riots in the regional parliament, forcing the federal government to declare a state of emergency.
*   **The Census Crisis (1962/1963):** The results of the population census were heavily disputed and rejected by southern politicians, who accused the north of inflating figures to maintain political control.
*   **Election violence (1964 and 1965):** The 1964 federal elections and the 1965 Western Region elections were marked by massive rigging, thuggery, arson and murder (often called *Operation Wetie*). The civilian government lost control of law and order, prompting the military to step in.

### 2. Corruption and mismanagement of funds
Military leaders almost always cite the high level of corruption, bribery, and embezzlement of public funds by civilian politicians as a primary reason for taking over. Politicians were seen as using public office to enrich themselves at the expense of the masses. 

### 3. Ethnic and regional politics
Political parties in the First and Second Republics were largely based on ethnic and regional lines (e.g., NPC for the North, AG for the West, NCNC for the East). Politicians appealed to ethnic sentiments rather than national interests. The military claimed they intervened to prevent ethnic divisions from tearing the nation apart, though the military itself later fell victim to ethnic rivalries (as seen in the July 1966 counter-coup).

### 4. Low level of professionalism and personal ambition
Sometimes, coups are driven by the personal ambition of military officers who taste power and want more. Furthermore, the promotion of officers based on ethnic quotas rather than merit led to a decline in military professionalism, causing junior officers to rebel against their superiors and civilian leaders.

### 5. Economic mismanagement
Economic hardship, high inflation, and failure to provide basic amenities often made civilian governments unpopular. The military usually takes advantage of public dissatisfaction, stepping in with promises to fix the economy. (For example, the 1983 coup that ousted President Shehu Shagari cited the severe economic crisis and unpaid salaries).

**Evaluating the reasons:** While the military always claimed they came to save the country from corrupt and inept politicians, history shows that military regimes themselves eventually became highly corrupt, abused human rights, and mismanaged the economy. Coups were often "corrective" in name only.

## Structure of military regimes

**Content b: Structure of military regimes.**

A military government is fundamentally different from a democratic one. It is **authoritarian**, **hierarchical**, and **centralised**. The constitution is usually suspended or heavily modified, and political parties are banned. 

Laws made by the federal military government are called **Decrees**, while those made by state military governments are called **Edicts**. Decrees cannot be challenged in a court of law.

### Key organs of a military government

| Organ | Description and Function |
| :--- | :--- |
| **The Head of State** | The highest authority, acting as the Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces. He chairs the highest ruling council and the executive council. (Note: General Ibrahim Babangida was the only military leader in Nigeria to use the title "President"). |
| **The Supreme Legislative Council** | The highest law-making and policy-making body. It approves Decrees and major national policies. Its name changed under different regimes: <br> - **Supreme Military Council (SMC):** Used by Ironsi, Gowon, Murtala/Obasanjo, and Buhari.<br> - **Armed Forces Ruling Council (AFRC):** Used by Babangida.<br> - **Provisional Ruling Council (PRC):** Used by Abacha and Abubakar. |
| **The Federal Executive Council (FEC)** | The cabinet, made up of military officers and civilian ministers appointed by the Head of State. They are responsible for implementing policies and running government ministries. |
| **State Military Governors** | Appointed by the Head of State to govern the states. They make laws through **Edicts** (which must not conflict with federal Decrees) and chair the State Executive Councils. |
| **The Judiciary** | The court system is retained to interpret laws and handle civil and criminal cases. However, its independence is severely limited because military regimes issue "ouster clauses" that prevent courts from questioning the legality of Decrees. |

## Impact of military rule: Political achievements and policies

**Objective ii: assess the achievements of military rule; Content c: Impact of military rule – political.**

Despite their undemocratic nature, military regimes implemented major political and administrative changes in Nigeria, some of which still define the country today.

### 1. Introduction of a Unitary System (Unification Decree No. 34)
When Major General J.T.U. Aguiyi-Ironsi took power after the January 1966 coup, he believed that Nigeria's strong regional divisions were the root of its problems. In May 1966, he promulgated the **Unification Decree No. 34**.
*   **What it did:** It abolished Nigeria's federal structure and regions, turning the country into a unitary state. It also unified the regional civil services into a single National Public Service.
*   **Impact:** The decree caused massive fear, especially in the North, where people felt the more educated South (particularly the Igbo, since Ironsi was Igbo) would dominate the civil service and the country. This led to violent riots in the North and directly triggered the bloody counter-coup of July 1966, where Ironsi was killed. The incoming leader, Yakubu Gowon, quickly reversed the decree and restored federalism.

### 2. Creation of States
To weaken regional powers, correct minority fears of domination, and ensure even development, the military broke Nigeria down from 4 regions into the current 36 states. 
*   **1967 (Yakubu Gowon):** Created **12 states** (partly to isolate the secessionist Eastern Region before the Civil War).
*   **1976 (Murtala Mohammed):** Increased the states to **19**.
*   **1987 (Ibrahim Babangida):** Created 2 new states, bringing the total to **21**.
*   **1991 (Ibrahim Babangida):** Created 9 more states, bringing the total to **30**.
*   **1996 (Sani Abacha):** Created 6 more states, resulting in the current **36 states**.

### 3. The National Youth Service Corps (NYSC)
After the devastating Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970), General Yakubu Gowon established the **NYSC** in **1973**. 
*   **Purpose:** To promote national unity, reconciliation, and integration by posting university and polytechnic graduates to serve in states other than their own for one year. It forces youths to mix with other ethnic groups and understand their cultures.

### 4. Local Government Reforms of 1976
The Obasanjo military regime introduced a uniform local government system across Nigeria in 1976, officially recognizing local government as the **third tier of government** with specific functions and statutory funding.

### 5. Negative Political Impacts
The military also had severely negative political impacts:
*   They established a culture of violence and the use of force.
*   They suspended the constitution and abused human rights (e.g., detaining activists without trial, killing journalists).
*   They destroyed democratic institutions and the political culture by constantly banning political parties.

## Impact of military rule: Economic and social programmes

**Objective ii: assess the achievements of military rule; Content c: Impact of military rule – economic.**

Military regimes introduced various economic and social policies, heavily tested in JAMB. Most of these specific agencies and programmes were introduced during the regime of General Ibrahim Babangida in the late 1980s.

### 1. Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP)
Introduced in **1986** by Ibrahim Babangida.
*   **Reason:** Nigeria's economy crashed when global oil prices fell in the early 1980s. SAP was introduced with the backing of the World Bank and IMF to restructure and diversify the economy away from its over-dependence on oil.
*   **What it involved:** Devaluing the Naira, reducing government spending, cutting subsidies, and privatizing public corporations.
*   **Impact:** While it encouraged some local sourcing of raw materials, it caused massive inflation, widespread poverty, loss of jobs, and severe economic hardship. It made the military highly unpopular and led to the "Anti-SAP riots" of 1989.

### 2. DFRRI (Directorate of Food, Roads and Rural Infrastructure)
Established in **1986** by Babangida.
*   **Purpose:** To open up rural areas and improve the quality of life for rural dwellers, thereby stopping the massive migration of youth to cities.
*   **Impact:** DFRRI built rural feeder roads, provided rural electrification, and sunk boreholes. However, many of the projects were poorly executed, and the agency was plagued by corruption and lack of maintenance.

### 3. Peoples Bank
Established in **1989** by the Babangida regime.
*   **Purpose:** To provide microcredit (small loans) to low-income earners, rural women, petty traders, and artisans who could not provide the collateral demanded by commercial banks. 
*   **Impact:** It initially helped many small-scale businesses, but eventually collapsed due to bad management, corruption, and the failure of borrowers to repay loans.

### 4. MAMSER 
MAMSER stands for **Mass Mobilization for Self Reliance, Social Justice, and Economic Recovery**. Introduced in **1987** by Babangida.
*   **Purpose:** It was an ethical and political orientation programme designed to educate Nigerians on their civic rights, promote patriotism, encourage mass participation in politics (like voting), and mobilize the public for economic self-reliance.
*   **Impact:** It raised political awareness and helped mobilize people for the transition to civil rule, though critics viewed it as a propaganda tool for the military.

### General Economic Assessment
While military regimes achieved some infrastructural development (building highways, the Third Mainland Bridge, and moving the capital to Abuja), they heavily mismanaged the oil boom of the 1970s. The lack of transparency led to massive corruption, institutionalized kickbacks, and the collapse of the agricultural sector.

## Processes of military disengagement

**Objective iii: determine the conditions that necessitated withdrawal from the political scene; Content d: Processes of military disengagement.**

Military disengagement refers to the process by which the military hands over power back to an elected civilian government. 

### Conditions that necessitated withdrawal 
Why did the military eventually leave power?

1.  **Internal domestic pressure:** Civil society groups, labour unions (NLC), students, and human rights activists organized massive strikes and protests. A key example is **NADECO** (National Democratic Coalition), which fought fiercely for the restoration of democracy after the military annulled the June 12, 1993, elections.
2.  **International pressure and sanctions:** The international community became hostile to military dictatorships. Following the execution of environmental activist Ken Saro-Wiwa in 1995 by the Abacha regime, Nigeria was suspended from the **Commonwealth**. The UN, US, and EU placed economic and travel sanctions on Nigeria.
3.  **Unpopularity and loss of legitimacy:** Due to severe economic hardship, corruption, and human rights abuses (such as the assassinations of journalists and activists), Nigerians totally rejected military rule.
4.  **Desire to restore military professionalism:** Many senior officers realized that involvement in politics was destroying the discipline, unity, and professionalism of the armed forces.
5.  **The death of General Sani Abacha:** Abacha's sudden death in June 1998 removed the biggest obstacle to democracy at the time, paving the way for General Abdulsalami Abubakar to quickly organize a transition.

### The process of disengagement (Transition Programmes)
Military regimes do not leave overnight. They usually outline a "transition to civil rule" programme. The process typically involves:
1.  **Setting up a constitution drafting committee:** To write a new constitution for the incoming civilian government (e.g., the 1979 and 1999 Constitutions).
2.  **Lifting the ban on political activities:** Allowing citizens to form or register political parties.
3.  **Establishing an electoral commission:** To register voters and organize elections (e.g., FEDECO in 1979, INEC in 1999).
4.  **Conducting elections:** Holding local, state, and federal elections.
5.  **The final handover:** Swearing in the elected civilian President.

**Successful transitions in Nigeria:**
*   **1979:** General Olusegun Obasanjo handed over to President Shehu Shagari, beginning the Second Republic.
*   **1999:** General Abdulsalami Abubakar handed over to President Olusegun Obasanjo, beginning the Fourth Republic.

*(Note: General Babangida's transition programme in the early 1990s—often called the Third Republic—was aborted when he annulled the June 12, 1993, presidential election, which was widely believed to have been won by Chief M.K.O. Abiola).*

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Coup d'état** | The sudden, violent, and illegal overthrow of a government, usually by the military. |
| **Decree** | A law made by the Federal Military Government, which cannot be challenged in court. |
| **Edict** | A law made by a State Military Government. |
| **Unitary system** | A system of government where all power is concentrated at the central (national) level. |
| **SAP** | Structural Adjustment Programme; an economic policy aimed at diversifying the economy and reducing imports, which caused severe hardship. |
| **MAMSER** | Mass Mobilization for Self Reliance, Social Justice, and Economic Recovery; an agency for ethical orientation and civic education. |
| **DFRRI** | Directorate of Food, Roads and Rural Infrastructure; an agency meant to develop rural areas. |
| **NADECO** | National Democratic Coalition; a powerful pressure group formed to force the military out of power after the annulment of the 1993 elections. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Decrees vs. Edicts:** A common JAMB question asks what laws made by state military governors are called. The answer is **Edicts**. Federal laws are **Decrees**.
*   **Decree 34:** Remember that the **Unification Decree No. 34** (which introduced a unitary system) was promulgated by **Aguiyi-Ironsi**. This is frequently tested.
*   **Supreme Ruling Bodies:** Know the differences! SMC (Gowon, Murtala, Buhari), AFRC (Babangida), PRC (Abacha, Abubakar).
*   **Who created what?** 
    *   Gowon: NYSC (1973), 12 states (1967).
    *   Babangida: SAP, DFRRI, MAMSER, Peoples Bank.
*   **Common mistake:** Do not confuse MAMSER with NYSC. NYSC is for graduate national integration; MAMSER was for public ethical and political mobilization.
*   **Conditions for withdrawal:** Questions often frame this as "external" or "internal" factors. Commonwealth suspension is external; NADECO and NLC strikes are internal.

## Quick summary

1.  Military regimes took over power in Nigeria citing corruption, election violence, census crises, and ethnic politics, though their underlying motives often included personal ambition.
2.  Military governments are hierarchical and rule by Decrees (Federal) and Edicts (State). The Head of State chairs a supreme legislative council (SMC, AFRC, or PRC).
3.  Politically, the military created the current 36 states, introduced the NYSC to foster unity, reformed local governments, but also briefly imposed a unitary system (Decree 34) and abused human rights.
4.  Economically, the military (specifically Babangida) introduced SAP, DFRRI, the Peoples Bank, and MAMSER. While some infrastructure was built, corruption and economic hardship worsened.
5.  Internal pressure from civil society (like NADECO), external sanctions (e.g., from the Commonwealth), and deep unpopularity forced the military to disengage.
6.  Military disengagement involves drafting a constitution, lifting the ban on politics, conducting elections, and handing over (as seen in 1979 and 1999).

## Practice questions

**1. A major reason often cited by the military for overthrowing civilian governments in Nigeria is**
- A. to change the country's capital city
- B. to introduce a unitary system of government
- C. corruption and mismanagement by politicians
- D. to join international organizations

**Answer: C.** Corruption, election violence, and economic mismanagement are the primary reasons cited by the military to justify their intervention. 

**2. Laws promulgated by a state military governor are known as**
- A. Constitutions
- B. Decrees
- C. Bye-laws
- D. Edicts

**Answer: D.** Laws made by state military administrators/governors are Edicts, while those made by the federal military government are Decrees.

**3. The Unification Decree No. 34 of 1966 was promulgated by**
- A. Yakubu Gowon
- B. J.T.U. Aguiyi-Ironsi
- C. Murtala Mohammed
- D. Ibrahim Babangida

**Answer: B.** Major General J.T.U. Aguiyi-Ironsi introduced Decree No. 34, which temporarily abolished federalism and introduced a unitary system.

**4. Which of the following bodies was the highest law-making organ during the regime of General Ibrahim Babangida?**
- A. Supreme Military Council (SMC)
- B. Armed Forces Ruling Council (AFRC)
- C. Provisional Ruling Council (PRC)
- D. Federal Executive Council (FEC)

**Answer: B.** Babangida replaced the SMC with the Armed Forces Ruling Council (AFRC). The PRC was used by Abacha and Abubakar.

**5. The National Youth Service Corps (NYSC) was established in 1973 primarily to**
- A. provide cheap labour for government schools
- B. prepare youths for military service
- C. promote national integration and unity
- D. reduce youth unemployment

**Answer: C.** After the Civil War, Gowon established the NYSC to force youths to mix with different ethnic groups, thereby fostering national unity and reconciliation.

**6. The agency established by the military to open up rural areas through the provision of roads, water, and electricity was**
- A. MAMSER
- B. NYSC
- C. SAP
- D. DFRRI

**Answer: D.** The Directorate of Food, Roads and Rural Infrastructure (DFRRI) was specifically created to develop rural areas. 

**7. Which of the following was a severe negative consequence of the Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP)?**
- A. It led to the creation of too many states
- B. It caused massive inflation and severe economic hardship
- C. It resulted in Nigeria's suspension from the Commonwealth
- D. It caused the immediate collapse of the banking sector

**Answer: B.** SAP involved devaluing the currency and removing subsidies, which led to a massive increase in the cost of living, inflation, and public protests. (Nigeria's suspension from the Commonwealth was due to the execution of Ken Saro-Wiwa, not SAP).

**8. In the process of military disengagement, the transition programme usually begins with**
- A. the swearing-in of a civilian president
- B. the suspension of the constitution
- C. the setting up of a constitution drafting committee
- D. the banning of political parties

**Answer: C.** To hand over to civilians, the military first needs a constitution to guide the incoming democracy. Banning parties and suspending the constitution happen at the *beginning* of military rule, not during disengagement.

**9. A major domestic factor that hastened the withdrawal of the military from Nigerian politics in the 1990s was the**
- A. threat of invasion by ECOWAS
- B. pressure from pro-democracy groups like NADECO
- C. sudden discovery of crude oil
- D. introduction of the Peoples Bank

**Answer: B.** Domestic pressure from civil society, human rights activists, and groups like NADECO made the country ungovernable and forced the military to commit to a democratic transition. 

**10. How many states were created by General Yakubu Gowon in 1967?**
- A. 12
- B. 19
- C. 21
- D. 36

**Answer: A.** Gowon broke the 4 regions into 12 states in 1967. Murtala made it 19 in 1976; Babangida raised it to 21 then 30; Abacha finalized the 36 states in 1996.
