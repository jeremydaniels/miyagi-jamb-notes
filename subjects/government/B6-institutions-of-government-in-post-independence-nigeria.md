## The Legislature in Post-Independence Nigeria

**Content a: The Legislative – structures, functions and workings.**

The legislature is the law-making arm of government. In Nigeria, its structure and workings have changed significantly depending on whether the country was under civilian rule (a parliamentary or presidential system) or military dictatorship.

### Structure of the Nigerian Legislature

Nigeria has experimented with both parliamentary and presidential systems of government, which directly affected the structure of its legislature.

| Era | System of Government | Legislative Structure (Federal) | Legislative Structure (Regional/State) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **First Republic (1960–1966)** | Parliamentary (British model) | **Bicameral:** The Federal Parliament consisted of the Senate (upper house) and the House of Representatives (lower house). | **Bicameral:** Each region had a House of Assembly and a House of Chiefs (traditional rulers). |
| **Military Rule (1966–1979, 1983–1999)** | Military Dictatorship | **Suspended:** The parliament was dissolved. Law-making was done by the Supreme Military Council (SMC) or Armed Forces Ruling Council (AFRC). | **Suspended:** State laws (Edicts) were made by the Military Governor. |
| **Second Republic (1979–1983)** | Presidential (American model) | **Bicameral:** The National Assembly consisted of the Senate and the House of Representatives. | **Unicameral:** Each state had only one house: the State House of Assembly. |
| **Fourth Republic (1999–present)** | Presidential (American model) | **Bicameral:** The National Assembly (Senate and House of Representatives). | **Unicameral:** The State House of Assembly in all 36 states. |

**The Current Structure (Under the 1999 Constitution, as amended):**
*   **The Senate:** The upper chamber has **109 members**. Each of the 36 states elects 3 Senators (one for each senatorial district), and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) elects 1 Senator. It is headed by the President of the Senate.
*   **The House of Representatives:** The lower chamber has **360 members**, representing federal constituencies based on population. It is headed by the Speaker.
*   **State House of Assembly:** A unicameral legislature in each state, headed by a Speaker. The number of members varies by state (usually between 24 and 40) depending on population.

### Functions of the Legislature

1.  **Law-making:** This is the primary function. The National Assembly makes laws for the peace, order, and good government of the Federation on matters in the **Exclusive Legislative List** (e.g., defense, currency, foreign affairs) and the **Concurrent Legislative List** (e.g., education, health). 
2.  **Control of Public Funds (Power of the Purse):** The executive cannot spend public money without legislative approval. The legislature debates and passes the annual budget (Appropriation Bill).
3.  **Oversight Functions:** The legislature monitors the executive branch to prevent abuse of power, corruption, and inefficiency. It can summon ministers and heads of government agencies to answer questions.
4.  **Approval of Appointments:** The Senate must screen and confirm major appointments made by the President, such as Ministers, Ambassadors, the Chief Justice, and the Governor of the Central Bank.
5.  **Impeachment:** The legislature has the constitutional power to remove the President, Vice President, State Governors, or Deputy Governors for "gross misconduct" (a serious violation of the constitution).
6.  **Ratification of Treaties:** Treaties negotiated by the executive with other nations must be domesticated (enacted into law) by the National Assembly before they take effect in Nigeria.
7.  **Amending the Constitution:** The National Assembly, along with State Houses of Assembly, holds the power to amend the Constitution. (Requires a two-thirds majority in the National Assembly and approval by two-thirds of the State Houses of Assembly).

### Workings of the Legislature

The operations of the Nigerian legislature involve strict procedures:

*   **Passing a Bill:** A bill (a proposed law) must pass through several stages in both chambers. 
    1.  **First Reading:** The title of the bill is read.
    2.  **Second Reading:** General debate on the principles and merits of the bill.
    3.  **Committee Stage:** The bill is sent to a specific committee (e.g., Committee on Education) for detailed study and public hearings.
    4.  **Report Stage:** The committee reports its findings and amendments back to the whole house.
    5.  **Third Reading:** Final review and passage.
    6.  **Harmonization:** If the Senate and House pass different versions, a Joint Conference Committee harmonizes them.
    7.  **Assent:** The bill is sent to the President to sign into law (assent). If the President refuses (veto), the National Assembly can override the veto with a two-thirds majority vote.
*   **The Committee System:** Because the full house cannot study every issue in detail, the legislature relies heavily on committees. These include **Standing Committees** (permanent, e.g., Defense, Finance) and **Ad-hoc Committees** (temporary, created for a specific investigation).
*   **Sitting Requirements:** The 1999 Constitution mandates that the National Assembly must sit for a minimum of **181 days** in a year.

## The Executive in Post-Independence Nigeria

**Content b: The Executive – structure, functions and workings.**

The executive is the arm of government responsible for implementing laws, formulating policies, and managing the day-to-day affairs of the state. 

### Structure of the Nigerian Executive

Like the legislature, the executive has taken different forms depending on the constitutional era.

| Era | System of Government | Executive Structure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **First Republic (1960–1966)** | Parliamentary | **Dual Executive:** Power was shared. The **Head of State** (Governor-General, later President Nnamdi Azikiwe) had ceremonial duties. The **Head of Government** (Prime Minister Tafawa Balewa) held real executive power and headed the cabinet. |
| **Military Era** | Military Dictatorship | **Centralized Military Command:** The Head of State (e.g., Gen. Gowon, Gen. Murtala, Gen. Abacha) had absolute executive power, assisted by a Supreme Military Council (or AFRC) and a Federal Executive Council made up of military officers and civilian appointees. State Military Governors answered directly to the Head of State. |
| **Second & Fourth Republics (1979–1983, 1999–present)** | Presidential | **Single (Executive) President:** The President is both Head of State and Head of Government, and Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces. The executive includes the Vice President, Ministers, and heads of Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs). At the state level, the Governor is the chief executive. |

**The Current Structure (Under the 1999 Constitution, as amended):**
*   **The Presidency:** Consists of the President and the Vice President, who run on a joint ticket.
*   **The Cabinet (Federal Executive Council - FEC):** Composed of Ministers appointed by the President. The constitution requires the President to appoint at least one Minister from each of the 36 states to ensure the **Federal Character Principle** (preventing domination by one ethnic group or region).
*   **The Civil Service and Agencies:** The bureaucracy of permanent workers who implement government policies.

### Functions of the Executive

1.  **Policy Formulation and Implementation:** The primary duty is to execute the laws passed by the legislature and run the government daily.
2.  **Maintenance of Law and Order:** Through agencies like the Police Force and the Armed Forces, the executive protects the lives and property of citizens.
3.  **Preparation of the Budget:** The executive drafts the annual budget outlining expected revenue and expenditure, and presents it to the National Assembly.
4.  **Foreign Relations:** The executive directs Nigeria's foreign policy, appoints ambassadors, receives foreign diplomats, and signs international treaties.
5.  **Defense of the Nation:** The President, as Commander-in-Chief, directs the armed forces to defend Nigeria from external aggression and internal insurrection.
6.  **Prerogative of Mercy (Pardon):** The President (and State Governors) can grant pardons, reduce sentences, or grant reprieves to people convicted of crimes.

### Workings of the Executive

*   **Decision Making:** In the current presidential system, the President holds all executive power. Ministers act as advisers and delegates of the President. The Federal Executive Council (FEC) meets regularly (usually weekly) to approve contracts, discuss policies, and advise the President, but the President is not legally bound to follow their advice.
*   **Delegation:** Because the President cannot run the country alone, power is delegated to Ministers (who head Ministries like Health, Education, and Defense) and Directors-General (who head agencies and parastatals). 
*   **Tenure:** In the presidential system, the President and Governors are elected for a fixed term of **four years** and can serve a maximum of **two terms**.

## The Judiciary in Post-Independence Nigeria

**Content c: The Judiciary – structure, functions and workings.**

The judiciary is the arm of government that interprets laws, settles disputes, and punishes offenders. It is often described as the "last hope of the common man."

### Structure of the Nigerian Judiciary (Hierarchy of Courts)

Before 1963, the highest court of appeal for Nigeria was the **Privy Council** in London. When Nigeria became a Republic in 1963, appeals to the Privy Council were abolished, and the **Supreme Court of Nigeria** became the final court of the land.

The modern hierarchy of regular courts in Nigeria is as follows:

1.  **The Supreme Court of Nigeria:** The highest court. It sits in Abuja. Its decisions are final and cannot be appealed. It is headed by the **Chief Justice of Nigeria (CJN)**. It handles appeals from the Court of Appeal and has original jurisdiction in disputes between states, or between a state and the federal government.
2.  **The Court of Appeal:** Hears appeals from the High Courts, Sharia Courts of Appeal, and Customary Courts of Appeal. Headed by the President of the Court of Appeal.
3.  **The Federal High Court / State High Courts / FCT High Court:** These have wide original jurisdiction to hear major civil and criminal cases. 
4.  **Sharia Court of Appeal and Customary Court of Appeal:** These exist in states that require them (mostly in the North for Sharia, and the South/Middle Belt for Customary). They hear appeals on Islamic personal law and customary (traditional) law, respectively.
5.  **Magistrate Courts and District Courts:** Lower courts that handle minor civil and criminal cases. They are created by state laws, not directly by the Constitution.
6.  **Customary / Area / Grade C Courts:** The lowest level of courts, dealing with local traditions, minor disputes, and customary marriages.

*Note on Military Era:* During military regimes, the regular courts were often bypassed. The military created **Special Military Tribunals** to try politicians and civilians for corruption, treason, and armed robbery. These tribunals did not follow normal judicial rules, did not allow appeals to the Supreme Court, and heavily undermined the judiciary's structure.

### Functions of the Judiciary

1.  **Interpretation of Law:** Explaining what the constitution and statutes mean when there is a dispute.
2.  **Settlement of Disputes:** Resolving conflicts between individuals, between citizens and the state, and between different tiers of government (e.g., Federal vs. State).
3.  **Protection of Fundamental Human Rights:** Safeguarding citizens from executive oppression and illegal detention.
4.  **Judicial Review:** The power to declare actions of the executive or laws passed by the legislature as **unconstitutional** and therefore null and void.
5.  **Election Petitions:** Special tribunals and regular courts hear disputes arising from elections, deciding whether a politician was validly elected.

### Workings of the Judiciary

*   **Appointment:** To maintain independence, the executive cannot just appoint anyone. Under the 1999 Constitution, the **National Judicial Council (NJC)**—a body mostly made up of senior judges—recommends qualified individuals. The President (or Governor) appoints them, and the Senate (or State House of Assembly) confirms the highest appointments.
*   **Independence:** For the judiciary to work fairly, it must be independent. This is safeguarded by charging judges' salaries directly to the **Consolidated Revenue Fund** (so the executive cannot withhold their pay), making it difficult to sack a judge (requires a two-thirds legislative majority), and granting judges immunity from prosecution for actions taken in their official capacity.
*   **Tenure:** Judges of the High Court and Court of Appeal retire at age 65, while Supreme Court Justices retire at age 70. *(Note: Proposals to unify the retirement age to 70 for all superior court judges have been part of recent constitutional amendment discussions.)*

## Operations and Evaluation of Government Agencies

**Objective i: evaluate the operations of the Arms of government and their agencies, e.g., the Civil Service, Armed Forces, Police, Courts and others.**

While the legislature, executive, and judiciary are the main arms, they rely on agencies to function. A critical part of the JAMB syllabus is evaluating how well these agencies have operated in post-independence Nigeria.

### 1. The Civil Service

The Civil Service is the body of permanent, professional officials employed by the government to advise on and implement policies.

*   **Operations:** They execute government policies, collect revenue, prepare budgets, and ensure continuity in government when political administrations change. They operate on principles of permanence, anonymity (civil servants are not blamed or praised publicly; ministers take the credit or blame), neutrality (they must not engage in partisan politics), and merit.
*   **Evaluation of its performance:**
    *   *Positives:* The civil service was the glue that kept Nigeria functioning during the turbulent years of the 1960s, the Civil War, and frequent military coups. It has provided continuity and stability.
    *   *Negatives/Challenges:* Over the years, the Nigerian civil service has been heavily criticized for **bureaucracy** (red tape and delays), **corruption**, and inefficiency. 
    *   *Impact of Quota System:* The introduction of the "Federal Character Principle" and quota system, while meant to ensure national unity, has often compromised merit, bringing in unqualified personnel.
    *   *Politicization:* Successive regimes, especially during the mass purges by Gen. Murtala Mohammed in 1975 and the 1988 Civil Service Reforms (which briefly made Directors-General political appointees), severely damaged the security of tenure and morale of civil servants.

### 2. The Armed Forces (Army, Navy, Air Force)

The Armed Forces are responsible for defending Nigeria's territorial integrity and sovereignty.

*   **Operations:** Their constitutional duty is strictly defense against external threats and, when called upon by the President, suppressing internal insurrection.
*   **Evaluation of its performance:**
    *   *Positives:* The military successfully kept Nigeria united during the Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970). They have also performed exceptionally well in international peacekeeping operations (e.g., ECOMOG in Liberia and Sierra Leone, and UN missions). Recently, they have been the primary force fighting the Boko Haram insurgency in the North-East.
    *   *Negatives/Challenges:* Historically, the Nigerian Armed Forces failed in their duty to remain politically neutral. They staged violent **coups d'état** and ruled Nigeria for 29 out of its first 39 years of independence. Military rule suspended the constitution, violated human rights, centralized the federal structure (damaging Nigerian federalism), and entrenched corruption. The military's over-involvement in internal security has also led to accusations of human rights abuses against civilians.

### 3. The Nigeria Police Force (NPF)

The Police Force is the primary agency of the executive responsible for internal security, maintaining law and order, and preventing and detecting crime.

*   **Operations:** The NPF is a single, national force under the command of the **Inspector-General of Police (IGP)**, who is appointed by the President. State Governors are technically the chief security officers of their states, but the state Police Commissioners take ultimate orders from the IGP in Abuja.
*   **Evaluation of its performance:**
    *   *Positives:* The police play a daily role in traffic management, crime investigation, and maintaining public peace despite difficult working conditions.
    *   *Negatives/Challenges:* The NPF is widely evaluated as underperforming. It suffers from **chronic underfunding**, poor welfare, inadequate equipment, and lack of modern forensic technology. 
    *   *Corruption and Brutality:* The force has faced severe allegations of bribery, extortion, and brutality, which famously culminated in the **#EndSARS protests in 2020** against a rogue police unit.
    *   *The State Police Debate:* Because the centralized police structure struggles to secure a massive country like Nigeria, there is an ongoing, heavy debate about amending the constitution to allow for **State Police**, which many argue would improve local intelligence and security.

### 4. The Courts (Judiciary as an Agency of Justice)

*   **Operations:** As detailed above, courts adjudicate disputes and interpret the law.
*   **Evaluation of its performance:**
    *   *Positives:* The Nigerian judiciary has occasionally shown great courage. The Supreme Court has delivered landmark judgments that stabilized democracy, such as reversing illegal impeachments of governors, resolving disputes over offshore oil revenue, and forcing political parties to obey their own rules.
    *   *Negatives/Challenges:* The judicial system is notoriously **slow**. Cases can take over a decade to reach the Supreme Court. Furthermore, the judiciary has faced damaging allegations of **corruption** and "cash-and-carry" judgments, especially regarding election petitions. Finally, **executive interference** (e.g., the controversial removal of Chief Justice Walter Onnoghen in 2019) frequently threatens judicial independence.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Bicameral Legislature** | A law-making body with two houses or chambers (e.g., Senate and House of Representatives). |
| **Unicameral Legislature** | A law-making body with only one house (e.g., Nigerian State Houses of Assembly). |
| **Exclusive Legislative List** | Matters that only the Federal Government (National Assembly) can legislate on (e.g., currency, defense). |
| **Federal Character** | A constitutional principle requiring government appointments to reflect the diverse ethnic and regional makeup of Nigeria. |
| **Appropriation Bill** | The proposed annual budget presented by the Executive to the Legislature for approval. |
| **Judicial Review** | The power of the courts to declare a law or executive action unconstitutional and void. |
| **National Judicial Council (NJC)** | The body responsible for recommending the appointment and disciplining of judicial officers in Nigeria. |
| **Consolidated Revenue Fund** | The main government bank account; judges' salaries are drawn directly from it to protect their independence. |
| **Anonymity** | The civil service principle that civil servants should not be publicly praised or blamed; ministers take responsibility. |
| **Decree / Edict** | Laws made by military governments. Decrees are made by the Federal military government; Edicts are made by State military governors. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **First vs. Second/Fourth Republic Executive:** A very common UTME trick is to mix these up. Remember: The First Republic (1960-1966) had a **dual executive** (Prime Minister and ceremonial President). The Second (1979) and Fourth (1999) Republics have an **executive President** (Head of State and Government combined).
*   **State Legislatures:** Under the 1999 Constitution, the Federal legislature (National Assembly) is **bicameral**, but all 36 State legislatures are **unicameral**. Do not say states have a Senate.
*   **Highest Court transition:** The Supreme Court became Nigeria's highest court in **1963** (when Nigeria became a Republic), replacing the British Privy Council. It was not the highest court at independence in 1960.
*   **Military law-making:** When the military takes over, they immediately suspend the **Legislature** and the **Constitution**. They rule by Decrees (Federal) and Edicts (States).
*   **Civil Service principles:** If a question asks why a civil servant isn't mentioned in the news for a failed policy, the answer is the principle of **anonymity**. If it asks why they don't lose their jobs when a new president is elected, the answer is **permanence**.

## Quick summary

1.  In post-independence Nigeria, the legislative structure changed from a parliamentary bicameral system (1960) to a suspended body during military rule, and to a presidential bicameral National Assembly (Senate and House of Reps) with unicameral state assemblies (1979, 1999).
2.  The National Assembly's functions include law-making, approving budgets, overseeing the executive, and confirming appointments.
3.  The executive structure evolved from a dual system (Prime Minister and Ceremonial President) in the First Republic to an Executive Presidency in the Second and Fourth Republics, where the President is both Head of State and Head of Government.
4.  The Judiciary is structured hierarchically, from Customary/Magistrate courts up to the Supreme Court, which became the highest court of appeal in 1963.
5.  To protect judicial independence, judges are appointed on the recommendation of the National Judicial Council (NJC), and their salaries are drawn from the Consolidated Revenue Fund.
6.  The Civil Service is permanent and implements policy, but has historically struggled with red tape, corruption, and the negative effects of politicization and the quota system.
7.  The Armed Forces are meant for defense but heavily disrupted Nigeria's democracy by ruling for 29 years; the Police force maintains internal security but is challenged by underfunding, corruption, and over-centralization.

## Practice questions

**1. The highest court of appeal in Nigeria today is the**
- A. Privy Council
- B. Federal High Court
- C. Supreme Court
- D. Court of Appeal

**Answer: C.** The Supreme Court of Nigeria is the apex court. It replaced the Privy Council as the highest court in 1963 when Nigeria became a Republic.

**2. Under the 1999 Constitution of Nigeria, the legislature at the state level is**
- A. bicameral
- B. unicameral
- C. tricameral
- D. parliamentarian

**Answer: B.** While the National Assembly at the federal level is bicameral, every State House of Assembly in Nigeria is unicameral (has only one chamber).

**3. In the First Republic, the executive power of the Nigerian government was vested in the**
- A. Governor-General
- B. Executive President
- C. Prime Minister and his cabinet
- D. Supreme Military Council

**Answer: C.** The First Republic operated a parliamentary system with a dual executive. Actual executive power was held by the Head of Government, the Prime Minister (Tafawa Balewa).

**4. The principle that civil servants should not publicly defend government policies or be blamed for their failures is known as**
- A. permanence
- B. impartiality
- C. meritocracy
- D. anonymity

**Answer: D.** Anonymity ensures that civil servants work behind the scenes. The political head (the Minister) takes public praise for successes and blame for failures.

**5. Which of the following is a major reason for the criticism of the Nigerian Police Force?**
- A. They frequently stage military coups
- B. Over-centralization, underfunding, and corruption
- C. Refusal to apply the Federal Character principle
- D. They possess too much judicial power

**Answer: B.** The NPF is heavily criticized for being too centralized (leading to calls for state police), chronically underfunded, and plagued by corruption and brutality.

**6. During military regimes in Nigeria, the legislative arm of government is usually**
- A. merged with the judiciary
- B. replaced by the Senate
- C. suspended
- D. allowed to operate independently

**Answer: C.** Upon taking power, military regimes immediately suspend the constitution and dissolve the legislature, placing law-making power in the hands of a military council (e.g., SMC or AFRC).

**7. The body responsible for recommending the appointment and discipline of judges in Nigeria is the**
- A. Judicial Service Commission
- B. Federal Executive Council
- C. National Judicial Council
- D. Ministry of Justice

**Answer: C.** The 1999 Constitution created the National Judicial Council (NJC) to shield the judiciary from executive control in matters of appointment and discipline.

**8. The upper chamber of the Nigerian National Assembly is composed of how many members?**
- A. 360
- B. 109
- C. 774
- D. 36

**Answer: B.** The Senate (upper chamber) has 109 members: 3 from each of the 36 states, plus 1 from the Federal Capital Territory (FCT). (360 is the number of members in the House of Representatives).

**9. The power of the legislature to declare actions of the executive as illegal or to remove the President for gross misconduct is known as**
- A. Judicial review
- B. Impeachment
- C. Assent
- D. Prerogative of mercy

**Answer: B.** Impeachment is the legislative power to remove a sitting executive for constitutional violations. (Judicial review is a power of the courts, not the legislature).

**10. One negative effect of the quota system and Federal Character Principle on the Nigerian civil service is that it**
- A. encourages military intervention
- B. ensures ethnic dominance
- C. often compromises the principle of merit
- D. prevents the creation of new states

**Answer: C.** While Federal Character aims to ensure all regions are represented (preventing ethnic dominance), a major criticism is that it often forces the hiring of less qualified individuals over more qualified ones just to fill a regional quota, sacrificing merit.
