## Differences between centralized and non-centralized states (Objective iv)

Before studying the specific groups in the Nigeria area, you must understand the two main types of political systems they operated: **centralized states** and **non-centralized (acephalous) states**. 

### Centralized States
A centralized state is a political system where power and authority are concentrated in a single supreme ruler (a king, emperor, or monarch) and a central government. The ruler governs a large territory using a network of titled chiefs, a bureaucracy (government officials like tax collectors and judges), and often a standing army. 
- **Examples:** Kanem-Borno, the Hausa States, the Oyo Empire (Yoruba), the Benin Empire (Edo), Nupe, and Igala.

### Non-Centralized (Acephalous) States
The word **acephalous** literally means "without a head" or "headless." These societies did not have a single powerful king ruling over a large kingdom. Instead, power was shared among small, independent units, such as families, lineages (extended families), or villages. Decisions were made by consensus (general agreement) among elders, age grades, title societies, and secret societies. They are also known as "segmentary" or "stateless" societies.
- **Examples:** The Igbo, Tiv, Ibibio, early Ijo, and some Idoma groups.

### Comparison of Centralized and Non-Centralized States

| Feature | Centralized States | Non-Centralized (Acephalous) States |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Leadership** | Governed by a single powerful monarch (e.g., Oba, Alaafin, Mai, Sarki). | Governed by councils of elders, village assemblies, or family heads. No supreme king. |
| **Administration** | Complex bureaucracy with specialized officials (ministers, tax collectors). | Decentralized; administrative functions handled by age grades, title holders, and secret societies. |
| **Territory** | Large, often expanding through conquest to include different ethnic groups. | Small, usually restricted to a village, clan, or a group of related lineages. |
| **Military** | Maintained a standing army or a highly organized military structure for defense and expansion. | No standing army; all able-bodied young men (often in age grades) fought when the community was attacked. |
| **Economy and Taxation** | Organized taxation systems, tribute from conquered areas, and controlled trade routes. | Little or no formal taxation; wealth was individual, based on farming, local trade, or voluntary contributions. |
| **Law and Order** | Formal courts, judges (e.g., Alkali courts in the north), and royal edicts. | Laws based on customs and religion; enforced by elders, secret societies (like Ekpe), or oracles. |

## Relating peoples to their traditions of origin (Objective i)

Historians study how the various ethnic groups in Nigeria explain their own origins. These explanations are called **traditions of origin**. They generally fall into two categories:
1. **Autochthony (Creation myths):** The people believe they were created exactly where they live by a Supreme Being, and did not come from anywhere else. (e.g., the Yoruba myth of Oduduwa creating land at Ile-Ife).
2. **Migration traditions:** The people believe their ancestors traveled from elsewhere (sometimes from the Middle East or neighboring African regions) to settle in their present location. (e.g., the Kanuri migrating from Kanem, or the Hausa Bayajidda legend).

**Factors influencing migration:**
When groups migrated, they did so for specific reasons:
- **Environmental factors:** Searching for fertile land for farming or water for cattle.
- **Security:** Fleeing from wars, slave raids, or oppressive rulers (e.g., Urhobo leaving Benin).
- **Dynastic disputes:** Princes losing a fight for the throne and leaving to found their own kingdoms (e.g., Tsoede of Nupe, Ginuwa of Itsekiri).
- **Economic factors:** Moving closer to trade routes, such as the rivers or the coast.

*Note on literal interpretations:* Textbooks sometimes report legends (like Bayajidda coming from Baghdad or Oduduwa from Mecca) exactly as the oral traditions state them. However, modern historians view these "Middle Eastern" origins as myths created later to link the rulers to Islamic prestige. These legends usually symbolize the slow migration of smaller groups who mixed with the local people and introduced new, centralized forms of government.

## Accounting for social and political organizations (Objective iii)

The social and political organizations of the Nigerian peoples were heavily influenced by their environment, economy, and history.
- **In the Savanna (Central Sudan):** The open terrain made it easy to use horses (cavalry). This allowed ambitious leaders to conquer vast areas, leading to large, highly centralized empires like Kanem-Borno and the Hausa States. The trans-Saharan trade and the early introduction of Islam also provided writing, bureaucracy, and a unified legal system (Sharia).
- **In the Forest Belts:** The thick forests made cavalry useless, so forest empires like Benin relied on well-organized infantry (foot soldiers) armed with iron weapons (and later European firearms). In the dense Eastern forests, the environment favored small, tight-knit, self-sustaining farming villages, which helped keep the Igbo and Ibibio acephalous.
- **In the Niger-Delta:** The swampy, riverine environment meant people lived on small islands as fishermen. Initially, they were acephalous. However, when the Atlantic trade (in slaves and later palm oil) began, these fishing villages reorganized themselves into wealthy **City-States** centered around powerful trading corporations known as the **House System**.

## Determining inter-state relations (Objective ii)

Before colonial rule, the states and peoples of the Nigeria area were never isolated; they interacted constantly through:
- **Trade:** The most common form of interaction. The forest peoples sent yams, palm oil, and kola nuts north; the savanna peoples sent cattle, salt, and leather goods south. The coastal people acted as middlemen between the European ships and the interior.
- **Religion:** Shared religious centers brought people together. For example, the Arochukwu oracle (Ibini Ukpabi) connected the Igbo with their neighbors, while Ile-Ife served as the spiritual headquarters for all Yoruba kingdoms. Islam united the northern states diplomatically.
- **War and Peace:** Wars were fought for territorial expansion, control of trade routes, and to capture slaves or extract tribute. Treaties, diplomatic marriages, and alliances were frequently used to make peace.

## Central Sudan – Kanuri and Hausa states

The Central Sudan refers to the savanna and sahel regions of northern Nigeria, extending towards Lake Chad. 

### 1. The Kanuri of Kanem-Borno
- **Origins and Migration:** The Kanuri people were the dominant group in the Borno Empire. Their rulers belonged to the **Saifawa dynasty**, which claimed descent from a mythical Arab hero named Saif ibn Dhi Yazan. Originally, the empire was located in **Kanem** (northeast of Lake Chad). In the late 14th century, continuous attacks by a rival group called the **Bulala** forced the Mai (king) and the Kanuri to migrate westward. They settled in Borno and built a new capital at **Ngazargamu** around 1470 under Mai Ali Gaji.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** Borno was highly centralized. The supreme ruler was the **Mai**. He ruled with a powerful council of twelve titled nobles. Key military titles included the **Galadima** (commander of the western frontier) and the **Kaigama** (commander-in-chief of the army). Royal women held enormous power, especially the **Magira** (Queen Mother), who had her own lands, courts, and army.
- **Inter-State Relations:** Borno frequently fought the Bulala to reclaim Kanem, clashed with the Hausa states (especially Kano), and defended against Jukun raids. Diplomatically, Borno maintained strong ties with North Africa and the Ottoman Empire based on their shared Islamic faith, trading slaves and salt for horses and firearms.

### 2. The Hausa States
- **Origins and Migration:** The origins of the Hausa states are explained by the **Bayajidda Legend**. According to the myth, a prince from Baghdad named Bayajidda arrived in Daura and killed a giant snake (Sarki) that terrorized the people. He married the Queen of Daura (Daurama). Their descendants founded the **Hausa Bakwai** (the seven "legitimate" states: Daura, Kano, Katsina, Zaria, Gobir, Rano, and Biram). Bayajidda’s illegitimate sons founded the **Banza Bakwai** (the seven "bastard" states, including Kebbi, Zamfara, and Nupe).
- **Socio-Political Organization:** The Hausa lived in centralized city-states centered around walled cities (*Birni*). The king, called the **Sarki**, was the political and religious head. He governed through a complex system of titled officials called the **Sarauta system**, with positions like the *Madawaki* (cavalry commander) and *Galadima* (senior counselor).
- **Inter-State Relations:** The states cooperated based on specializations: Gobir defended the north, Zaria captured slaves in the south, Kano and Katsina were trade and industrial hubs, and Daura was the spiritual center. Despite sharing a culture and language, the Hausa states fiercely competed and fought each other (especially Kano and Katsina) for dominance of trade routes. Islam, introduced around the 14th century by Wangara traders from Mali, shaped their laws and diplomacy.

## Niger-Benue Valley – Nupe, Jukun, Igala, Idoma, Tiv and Ebira

The Niger-Benue Valley, also known as the Middle Belt, was a diverse region that served as a cultural bridge between the northern savannas and the southern forests.

### 1. The Nupe
- **Origins:** Nupe origin is tied to the legend of **Tsoede** (or Edegi). Tsoede was a prince born to an Igala king (the Attah of Idah) and a Nupe mother. Following a succession dispute in Idah, he fled up the River Niger in a bronze canoe, taking royal regalia and bronze casting skills. He united the scattered Nupe villages into a kingdom around the 15th century.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** The Nupe formed a centralized kingdom ruled by the **Etsu Nupe**. The Etsu governed with a hierarchy of state officials. The capital eventually moved to Bida in the 19th century.
- **Inter-State Relations:** Controlling the middle Niger, the Nupe were major traders in slaves, glass, and brasswork. They frequently clashed with the Yoruba (Oyo) to their south and were eventually conquered by Fulani jihadists in the 19th century.

### 2. The Jukun (Kwararafa)
- **Origins:** The Jukun are believed to have migrated from the east into the Benue Valley, where they established the powerful state of **Kwararafa** (later moving their capital to **Wukari**).
- **Socio-Political Organization:** The Jukun had a centralized, theocratic government. The king, the **Aku Uka**, was a divine ruler restricted by religious taboos. If he fell ill or the state suffered natural disasters, tradition allowed for him to be ritually removed or killed by senior priests.
- **Inter-State Relations:** Between the 16th and 17th centuries, the Jukun were a terrifying military force. Their cavalry launched massive, deep raids into Hausaland (attacking Kano and Katsina) and Borno, looking for loot and slaves rather than permanent conquest.

### 3. The Igala
- **Origins:** Centered at **Idah** on the River Niger, the Igala have traditions linking them to the Yoruba, Edo, and Jukun. A key legend is that of **Ayegba oma Idoko**, a king who liberated the Igala from Jukun dominance by sacrificing his daughter, Princess Inikpi, to ensure victory in war.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** Igala was highly centralized. The divine king was the **Attah of Igala**. His power was counter-balanced by the **Achadu** (the Prime Minister).
- **Inter-State Relations:** Idah’s location near the Niger-Benue confluence gave the Igala immense control over riverine trade. They influenced the Nupe and Idoma, traded with Benin and the Igbo, and dominated the regional economy.

### 4. The Idoma and Ebira
- **Idoma:** Many Idoma groups trace their origins to Apa in the Jukun Kwararafa kingdom, migrating south to escape wars. They were a transitional society—living in independent communities governed by a council of elders (**Ojira**) and religious cults. The overarching title of *Och'Idoma* is a modern creation. They traded and sometimes fought with the Igala and Tiv.
- **Ebira:** Tracing their migrations from Wukari and Idah, the Ebira settled around Okene. They were largely decentralized clans. The title of **Ohinoyi** (chief) existed but did not carry absolute monarchical power until later.

### 5. The Tiv
- **Origins:** The Tiv claim descent from a single ancestor named Tiv. They migrated from the southeast (likely the Cameroon mountains) through a legendary hill called **Swem**. 
- **Socio-Political Organization:** The Tiv are a classic example of a **non-centralized (acephalous)** society. They were organized around the **Tar** (a territory occupied by a specific lineage). There was no central king; leadership was shared among elders, wealthy men, and those with spiritual power.
- **Inter-State Relations:** As their population grew, the Tiv were aggressive, expansionist farmers who pushed outwards, absorbing or fighting neighbors like the Jukun for farmland.

## Eastern Forest Belt – Igbo and Ibibio

### 1. The Igbo
- **Origins:** The Igbo do not have a single origin tradition. Many core communities (Awka, Orlu) claim **autochthony**—that they have always lived there. Archaeological discoveries at **Igbo-Ukwu** prove that a highly advanced bronze-casting civilization existed there by the 9th century. Communities on the edges claim migration from neighboring areas (e.g., Onitsha claims Benin origins; Nsukka claims Igala origins).
- **Socio-Political Organization:** The Igbo were famously **non-centralized (acephalous)**, operating a "Village Republic" system. The basic unit was the **Umunna** (extended family patrilineage). Decisions were made by the **Oha-na-eze** (general village assembly) where all adult males could speak, and consensus was required. Law and order were maintained by age grades, title societies (like the **Ozo** title), and women’s groups (**Umuada**). A few communities, like Onitsha, developed centralized kings (Obi) due to external influence.
- **Inter-State Relations:** The Igbo were master farmers and traders. Inter-state relations were heavily managed by powerful religious oracles, most notably the **Ibini Ukpabi (Long Juju) of Arochukwu**. The Aro people used this oracle to dominate regional trade, settling disputes and controlling the flow of slaves to the coastal middlemen.

### 2. The Ibibio
- **Origins:** Widely considered the oldest inhabitants of the southeastern region, the Ibibio oral traditions claim they migrated from the Benue/Cross River area (or Usak Edet in Cameroon) to settle in present-day Akwa Ibom State.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** Like the Igbo, the Ibibio were **non-centralized**. Society was organized into families (Ufok), lineages (Ekpuk), and villages (Idung), governed by elder councils. Lacking a central police force, they relied on powerful secret societies—the **Ekpo** (ancestral spirits) and **Ekpe** (leopard) societies—to execute criminals, collect debts, and enforce laws.
- **Inter-State Relations:** They engaged in extensive agricultural trade with the Igbo and provided goods to the coastal Efik (who had historically broken away from them) for the Atlantic trade.

## Western Forest Belt – Yoruba and Edo

### 1. The Yoruba
- **Origins:** All Yoruba groups trace their spiritual origins to **Ile-Ife** and the legendary founder **Oduduwa** (or Oodua). The *creation myth* states Oduduwa was sent from heaven by the Supreme Being to create the earth at Ile-Ife. The *migration myth* (popularized later) claims he was a prince who fled Mecca. Historians view Ile-Ife as an ancient indigenous center where a centralized political system (the Oduduwa revolution) emerged, after which princes dispersed to found other Yoruba kingdoms (Oyo, Egba, Ijebu, etc.).
- **Socio-Political Organization:** The Yoruba formed highly **centralized** kingdoms, with the **Oyo Empire** being the most powerful. The king was a sacred monarch (e.g., the **Alaafin** of Oyo). The king's power was not absolute; it was heavily checked. In Oyo, the **Oyomesi** (a council of seven kingmakers led by the Bashorun) could force a bad Alaafin to commit suicide. The **Ogboni** secret society mediated between the king and the Oyomesi. The army was led by the **Aare Ona Kakanfo**.
- **Inter-State Relations:** The Yoruba kingdoms shared a language, religion, and respect for the Ooni of Ife. Oyo used its powerful cavalry to conquer neighbors, extracting tribute from Dahomey and Nupe. In the 19th century, following Oyo's collapse, they engaged in devastating civil wars.

### 2. The Edo (Benin Empire)
- **Origins:** The earliest rulers of Benin were the **Ogiso** (Kings of the Sky). When that dynasty fell, the Edo people reportedly sent to Ife for a ruler. Oduduwa sent his son, **Oranmiyan**. Finding Benin difficult to rule, Oranmiyan left, but fathered a child with an Edo woman. This child, **Eweka I**, became the first Oba of the new dynasty. (Some historians argue this myth legitimizes an Ife conquest, while others claim Oduduwa was originally an exiled Edo prince).
- **Socio-Political Organization:** Benin was fiercely **centralized** and autocratic. The **Oba** was an absolute monarch and a living deity. The highest nobles were the **Uzama** (hereditary kingmakers). To balance their power, the Oba appointed town chiefs called the **Egharevba**, led by the **Iyase** (Prime Minister and army commander). Craftsmen were organized into strict royal guilds.
- **Inter-State Relations:** Benin was highly expansionist, conquering parts of Yorubaland (founding the Lagos dynasty) and western Igboland. It was also one of the first West African states to trade directly with Europeans, establishing relations with the Portuguese in the 1480s to trade pepper, ivory, and slaves for firearms.

## Coastal and Niger-Delta – Efik, Ijo, Itsekiri and Urhobo

The harsh, watery environment of the Niger Delta forced these groups to adapt, turning them from simple fishing communities into wealthy middlemen in the transatlantic trade.

### 1. The Efik
- **Origins:** The Efik trace their origins to the Ibibio/Igbo borderlands. Due to disputes, they migrated down the Cross River, eventually founding **Calabar**.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** Initially **acephalous** lineages, the Efik centralized to manage the booming Atlantic trade. They developed the **House System**, where political power belonged to wealthy trading families rather than just blood relatives. The titular king was the **Obong**. To maintain order without an absolute monarch, the **Ekpe (Leopard) Secret Society** became the supreme government, enforcing trade contracts and collecting debts.
- **Inter-State Relations:** The Efik were pure middlemen. They bought slaves and palm oil from the Igbo and Ibibio hinterland and sold them to European ships on the coast.

### 2. The Ijo (Ijaw)
- **Origins:** The Ijo are considered the indigenous (autochthonous) inhabitants of the Niger Delta, settling in the isolated creeks long before others arrived.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** They started as decentralized fishing villages. The Atlantic trade transformed the Eastern Delta Ijo into centralized **City-States** (like Bonny, Nembe, and Kalabari). Their society reorganized into the **Canoe House System (Wari)**. Leadership of a House was based on wealth and the number of trade canoes one could equip; even a slave (like Jaja of Opobo) could rise to lead a House. The city-state was ruled by a king called the **Amanyanabo**.
- **Inter-State Relations:** The Ijo city-states were fierce rivals, fighting each other to monopolize trade routes into the Igbo hinterland and violently preventing Europeans from going inland.

### 3. The Itsekiri
- **Origins:** The Itsekiri kingdom of Warri speaks a language related to Yoruba, but their political origins tie them to Benin. Tradition states that **Prince Ginuwa**, a son of a Benin Oba, fled in the 15th century with some chiefs and established the Itsekiri kingdom in the western delta.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** Due to the Benin influence, the Itsekiri were highly **centralized** from the beginning. The king was the **Olu of Warri**, who ruled with a council of chiefs known as the **Ojoye**.
- **Inter-State Relations:** Situated near the coast, the Itsekiri became powerful middlemen trading with the Portuguese and British. They traded fish, salt, and European imports to the Urhobo and Benin for agricultural produce.

### 4. The Urhobo
- **Origins:** The Urhobo live in the western delta hinterland. Their traditions point to a mix of indigenous populations and migrations from **Aka (Edo/Benin)**, with groups fleeing the oppressive reigns of various Obas.
- **Socio-Political Organization:** The Urhobo system was a blend of centralized and decentralized elements. They lived in independent polities headed by a priest-king called the **Ovie**, whose authority was largely spiritual. Real political power rested with the **Edion** (council of elders) and age grades.
- **Inter-State Relations:** The Urhobo were primarily farmers and hunters. They supplied the coastal Itsekiri and Ijo with yams and palm oil in exchange for fish and salt, occasionally clashing with the Itsekiri over trade terms.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Centralized state** | A society with a single supreme ruler (monarch), a central government, bureaucracy, and often a standing army. |
| **Acephalous society** | A "headless" or non-centralized society ruled by consensus among elders, age grades, or lineages, without a single king. |
| **Mai** | The title of the supreme ruler of the Kanem-Borno Empire. |
| **Sarki** | The title of the king in the Hausa city-states. |
| **Hausa Bakwai** | The seven original, "legitimate" Hausa states according to the Bayajidda legend (e.g., Daura, Kano, Katsina). |
| **Aku Uka** | The divine king of the Jukun (Kwararafa) people. |
| **Attah** | The title of the king of the Igala kingdom at Idah. |
| **Umunna** | The patrilineage or extended family unit, central to Igbo socio-political organization. |
| **Oyomesi** | The powerful council of seven kingmakers in the Oyo Empire, led by the Bashorun. |
| **Uzama** | The hereditary council of kingmakers in the Benin Empire. |
| **House System** | A socio-political and economic organization in the Niger Delta (Efik, Ijo) based on trading corporations rather than just blood lineage. |
| **Amanyanabo** | The title of the king in the Ijo (Ijaw) city-states. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse the origin myths:** Bayajidda = Hausa; Oduduwa = Yoruba; Tsoede = Nupe; Oranmiyan/Eweka = Benin; Ginuwa = Itsekiri. JAMB frequently tests these matches.
- **Centralized vs. Acephalous:** Always remember that the Igbo, Tiv, and Ibibio were traditionally acephalous (non-centralized), while the Hausa, Kanuri, Yoruba, Benin, and Igala were highly centralized.
- **The Delta Transformation:** Remember that the Efik and Ijo *started* as acephalous fishing villages but transformed into centralized city-states with Kings (Amanyanabo/Obong) and the House System due to the Atlantic trade.
- **Secret Societies:** Know their locations. Ekpe and Ekpo belong to the Ibibio/Efik. Ogboni belongs to the Yoruba.
- **Checks and Balances:** A common question is who checked the power of the king. In Oyo, it was the Oyomesi. In Igala, it was the Achadu. In Benin, the Uzama and Egharevba balanced each other.

## Quick summary

1. Traditions of origin generally fall into autochthony (creation on the spot) or migration, driven by land, security, trade, or dynastic disputes.
2. The Nigeria area was divided into highly centralized states (like Kanem-Borno, Hausa, Oyo, Benin, Igala) ruled by monarchs, and non-centralized/acephalous states (like Igbo, Tiv, Ibibio) ruled by elders and consensus.
3. The **Central Sudan** featured the Kanuri (Saifawa dynasty, Mai) and the Hausa (Bayajidda legend, Sarki), both heavily influenced by Islam, cavalry warfare, and trans-Saharan trade.
4. The **Niger-Benue Valley** was diverse, home to centralized states like the Nupe (Tsoede), Jukun (Aku Uka), and Igala (Attah), alongside the strictly acephalous Tiv.
5. The **Eastern Forest Belt** housed the acephalous Igbo (Village republics, Umunna) and Ibibio (Ekpo/Ekpe secret societies), who relied on farming and regional trade networks like the Arochukwu oracle.
6. The **Western Forest Belt** featured the centralized empires of the Yoruba (tracing to Oduduwa at Ife, with Oyo checked by the Oyomesi) and Edo/Benin (ruled by an absolute Oba, advised by the Uzama).
7. The **Coastal and Niger-Delta** groups (Efik, Ijo, Itsekiri) transformed into wealthy, centralized City-States utilizing the House System to dominate the middleman trade with Europeans.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following pre-colonial societies was strictly non-centralized (acephalous)?**

- A. The Nupe kingdom
- B. The Benin Empire
- C. The Tiv society
- D. The Igala kingdom

**Answer: C.** The Tiv society was acephalous (segmentary). They had no central king, and authority rested with elders and lineage heads. The Nupe, Benin, and Igala all had powerful centralized monarchs.

**2. According to Hausa tradition, the legendary founder of the Hausa states who killed a giant snake in Daura was:**

- A. Oduduwa
- B. Bayajidda
- C. Tsoede
- D. Oranmiyan

**Answer: B.** Bayajidda is the legendary Middle Eastern prince who killed the snake in Daura, married the queen, and became the ancestor of the Hausa states.

**3. In the pre-colonial political administration of the Oyo Empire, the power of the Alaafin was checked by the:**

- A. Ogboni cult
- B. Oyomesi
- C. Uzama
- D. Egharevba

**Answer: B.** The Oyomesi was the council of seven kingmakers led by the Bashorun, who had the power to reject and force a tyrannical Alaafin to commit suicide. The Uzama and Egharevba belong to the Benin Empire.

**4. The socio-political organization in the Niger Delta city-states during the era of legitimate trade was predominantly based on the:**

- A. Sarauta system
- B. Age grade system
- C. House system
- D. Ozo title system

**Answer: C.** The House system (or Canoe House) became the dominant socio-political and economic organization for the Ijo and Efik, replacing pure blood lineage with trading corporations led by wealthy merchants.

**5. The Jukun kingdom of Kwararafa is historically remembered in the Central Sudan primarily for its:**

- A. peaceful spread of Islam
- B. massive military cavalry raids on Borno and Hausaland
- C. introduction of bronze casting to the north
- D. monopoly over the transatlantic slave trade

**Answer: B.** Between the 16th and 17th centuries, the Jukun (Kwararafa) launched terrifying cavalry raids deep into Hausaland (Kano, Katsina) and Borno, raiding for slaves and loot.

**6. Which of the following groups trace the foundation of their centralized monarchy to Prince Ginuwa from Benin?**

- A. The Itsekiri
- B. The Urhobo
- C. The Ijo
- D. The Efik

**Answer: A.** The Itsekiri kingdom of Warri was founded by Prince Ginuwa, who migrated from Benin with a retinue of chiefs following a dynastic dispute.

**7. In the traditional Igbo socio-political system, the basic unit of administration was the:**

- A. Oha-na-eze
- B. Umunna
- C. Obi
- D. Umuada

**Answer: B.** The Umunna (patrilineage or extended family) was the basic and fundamental unit of political and social organization in traditional Igbo society. 

**8. The Ekpe secret society served as the supreme judicial and law enforcement body among the:**

- A. Yoruba and Edo
- B. Kanuri and Hausa
- C. Tiv and Idoma
- D. Efik and Ibibio

**Answer: D.** Because the Efik and Ibibio lacked a central monarch with a standing police force, they used the Ekpe (Leopard) secret society to enforce laws, punish criminals, and collect debts.

**9. The supreme ruler of the Kanem-Borno empire held the title of:**

- A. Sarki
- B. Mai
- C. Attah
- D. Obong

**Answer: B.** The supreme ruler of Kanem-Borno was the Mai. Sarki is Hausa, Attah is Igala, and Obong is Efik.

**10. Which legendary figure is credited with uniting the Nupe people and establishing a centralized kingdom after fleeing from Idah?**

- A. Ayegba oma Idoko
- B. Tsoede
- C. Eweka I
- D. Oranmiyan

**Answer: B.** Tsoede (or Edegi) is the legendary hero who fled the Igala court at Idah with royal regalia and united the Nupe people. Ayegba oma Idoko is an Igala hero.
