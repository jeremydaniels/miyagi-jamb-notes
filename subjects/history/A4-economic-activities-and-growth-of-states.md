## Identifying the various economic activities

Before the year 1800, the people of the Nigeria area were not just farmers; they engaged in a wide variety of economic activities to survive and build wealth. The economy was built on three main pillars: **agriculture** (producing food and raw materials), **industries** (manufacturing goods from raw materials), and **trade** (exchanging surplus goods). Because there were no modern factories or money as we know it today, everything was produced by human skill and traded using early currencies or by direct exchange.

## Agriculture

Agriculture was the mainstay of the traditional economy. Almost every family was involved in producing food.

### Hunting 
Hunting is one of the oldest economic activities. In both the forested south and the open northern savannah, professional hunters tracked and killed wild animals (such as antelopes, elephants, and bush pigs). 
- **Methods:** They used traps, snares, bows, poisoned arrows, and iron spears. Dogs were often trained to assist in tracking.
- **Products:** Hunting provided meat for food, hides for leather, ivory for carving and trade, and bones for tools. 
- **Social role:** Hunters were highly respected. They were often the first to explore new forests, and many towns in the Nigeria area were founded by hunters who discovered fertile land or water sources during their expeditions.

### Farming
Farming was the primary occupation of the majority of the people. It was labor-intensive, relying on simple iron tools like hoes and cutlasses.
- **Methods:** The most common farming system was **shifting cultivation** or **rotational bush fallow**. A farmer would clear a piece of land, burn the bush, and farm it until the soil lost its fertility. The land was then left to rest (fallow) for several years to regain its nutrients naturally, while the farmer moved to a new plot. In mountainous or hilly areas where land was scarce, such as the Jos Plateau and the Mandara Mountains, people used **terrace farming** (cutting flat step-like ridges into the hillsides to prevent soil from washing away).
- **Crops:** The environment decided what was grown. In the humid southern forests, farmers grew root crops like yams and cocoyams, alongside palm trees. In the drier northern savannah, they grew cereals like sorghum (guinea corn), millet, and later maize, as well as cotton and beans.

### Fishing
For communities living along the Atlantic coast, the Niger Delta creeks, and major rivers (like the Niger, Benue, and Cross River), fishing was the main occupation.
- **Methods:** Fishermen used woven nets, basket traps, hooks, harpoons, and sometimes specialized plant poisons that stunned the fish so they could be easily scooped from the water.
- **Importance:** Fishing communities (such as the Ijaw, Itsekiri, Ilaje, Efik, and Jukun) often caught more fish than they could eat. They **smoked or sun-dried** the surplus fish to preserve it, and traded it to inland farming communities in exchange for yams and vegetables.

### Animal husbandry
Animal husbandry is the rearing and keeping of livestock. It was practiced differently depending on the region.
- **Pastoralism in the North:** In the northern savannah and sahel, the absence of the tsetse fly (an insect that causes sleeping sickness in cattle) allowed people like the Fulani, Kanuri, and Shuwa Arabs to keep large herds of cattle, sheep, and goats. Many practiced **transhumance**, moving their herds seasonally to find fresh water and pasture.
- **Livestock in the South:** In the forest zones, the tsetse fly made keeping large cattle impossible. Instead, families kept small, disease-resistant livestock like the dwarf cattle (*muturu*), goats, sheep, and poultry within their household compounds.

### Horticulture
Horticulture is the intensive cultivation of fruits, vegetables, and medicinal herbs. In the traditional economy, this took the form of **compound farming**. Small gardens were maintained right behind the living quarters. These gardens were fertilized with household waste and ash, and produced essential daily items like peppers, leafy vegetables, pawpaw, and medicinal plants, providing a balanced diet alongside the main farm crops.

## Industries

The Nigeria area had a vibrant manufacturing sector. Craftsmen and women transformed agricultural produce and natural resources into useful goods.

### Pottery
Pottery was an essential industry, primarily dominated by women. Using locally sourced clay, potters built vessels using the **coiling method** (building the pot up with rings of clay) and fired them in open outdoor bonfires. 
- **Uses:** Pots were crucial for cooking food, storing grain, and keeping drinking water cool. They were also used in religious rituals. Famous traditional pottery centers included Afikpo, Ilorin, and the Abuja area.

### Salt-making
Salt was a highly prized commodity because it was necessary for seasoning food and preserving meat and fish.
- **Coastal and Riverine:** Communities in the Niger Delta (like Ke and Nembe) and the coast boiled seawater or burnt the roots of mangrove trees to extract salt.
- **Inland:** People boiled salty water (brine) drawn from natural salt springs and lakes. Famous inland salt-making centers included **Uburu** in present-day Ebonyi State and **Awe** in Nasarawa State.
- **Saharan Salt:** In the far north, thick blocks of salt were imported from desert oases like **Bilma** via the trans-Saharan trade.

### Iron-smelting and blacksmithing
These were two distinct but connected industries.
- **Iron-smelting:** This is the difficult process of extracting raw iron metal from iron ore rocks. Smelters built tall clay furnaces, filled them with ore and charcoal, and used bellows to pump in air, creating massive heat that melted the rock to release the iron. The ancient **Nok culture** and the site of **Taruga** are famous early centers of iron-smelting.
- **Blacksmithing:** Blacksmiths took the smelted iron and heated it in a forge. Using an anvil and hammer, they shaped the red-hot metal into hoes, cutlasses, axes, spears, swords, and arrowheads. Blacksmiths were highly respected and sometimes feared for their control over metal and fire; they often formed powerful, exclusive guilds (such as in Awka in Igboland).

### Leather-working
Leather-working flourished in the northern savannah, where cattle, sheep, and goats were abundant. Tanners treated animal hides with local plant pods (like *bagaruwa*) to stop them from rotting. Leather-workers then dyed the leather and crafted it into sandals, bags, cushions, saddles, shields, and sword sheaths. **Kano** was incredibly famous for its high-quality red and green dyed leather, which was traded across the Sahara to North Africa and became known in Europe as "Morocco leather".

### Wood-carving
Because of the abundance of timber, wood-carving was highly developed in the forest regions.
- **Products:** Carvers made everyday items like stools, doors, mortars, and pestles. They also carved deeply religious items like masks, shrine figures (like the Igbo *Ikenga* or Yoruba *ibeji* twins), and talking drums.
- **Canoe-building:** In the riverine areas, wood-carving was vital for transport. The Ijaw and other coastal peoples were expert canoe-builders, carving massive boats out of single, giant tree trunks. These canoes were used for fishing, trading, and warfare.

### Cloth-making and dyeing
The production of textiles was a major industry.
- **Cloth-making:** In the savannah, cotton was widely grown and spun into thread. Weavers used two main types of looms: the **broad vertical loom** (mostly used by women to weave wide pieces of cloth, like the Igbo *Akwete* cloth) and the **narrow horizontal loom** (mostly used by men to weave long, narrow strips of cloth that were sewn together, like the Yoruba *Aso-oke*).
- **Dyeing:** Once woven, cloth was often dyed to give it beautiful colors. The most famous dye was **indigo** (*elu* in Yoruba, *baba* in Hausa), which produced a rich, deep blue. Kano was famous for its ancient dye pits, such as the **Kofar Mata** dye pits, where cloth was soaked in deep holes filled with indigo and ash.

### Food processing
Raw crops often had to be processed before they could be eaten, stored, or traded. 
- **Examples:** Extracting palm oil from palm nuts (a labor-intensive process of boiling, pounding, and pressing); brewing alcoholic drinks like palm wine or sorghum beer (*burukutu*); smoking and drying fish and meat; and milling grains into flour using heavy grinding stones. *(Note: Processing cassava into garri became a major industry in later centuries after cassava was introduced from the Americas by the Portuguese).*

## Trade and trade routes

Trade was the glue that connected the different economic activities and regions. Trade happened at three main levels.

### Local trade
Local trade took place within a village or between neighbouring villages. Markets were held daily or every few days (e.g., every four days in many Igbo communities). This trade was mainly the exchange of everyday goods: a farmer trading surplus yams for a potter's clay pot or a hunter's meat. 
- **Exchange methods:** It originally relied on **barter** (direct exchange of goods). Later, local currencies were introduced, such as **cowrie shells**, iron bars, copper rods, and manillas.

### Regional trade
Regional trade covered longer distances, connecting different environmental zones. This was driven by the fact that different zones produced different goods.
- **The Exchange:** Coastal people traded their salt and smoked fish with forest people in exchange for yams, palm oil, and vegetables. Forest people traded kola nuts and ivory with the savannah people in exchange for cattle, leather goods, and iron tools.
- **Relay Trade:** This trade often operated on a relay system. A trader did not travel the whole distance; instead, goods were passed from one market to another, changing hands multiple times. Groups like the **Aro** in southeastern Nigeria created powerful trading networks, using the influence of the *Chukwu Abiama* (Arochukwu) oracle to control regional trade routes.

### Long distance trade, trans-Sahara trade and the trade in slaves
Long-distance trade required massive organization, capital, and months of travel. 

**The Trans-Sahara Trade:**
Before Europeans arrived on the coast, the major international trade route pointed north, across the Sahara Desert, connecting West Africa to North Africa and the Middle East.
- **The Journey:** Merchants traveled in large caravans using camels, which could survive the harsh desert.
- **The Goods:** The Nigeria area (especially states like Kano, Katsina, and Kanem-Borno) exported gold, slaves, ivory, animal hides, and kola nuts. In return, they imported **salt** (from desert lakes), **horses**, copper, silk, cowries, and later, firearms. 
- **Impact:** This trade brought immense wealth to the northern states. It also brought **Islam** and Arabic literacy, fundamentally changing the culture and politics of the savannah.

**The Trade in Slaves (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade):**
From the late 15th century, European explorers (starting with the Portuguese) arrived at the Atlantic coast. Initially, they traded in pepper, ivory, and gold. However, the trade soon shifted to human beings, needed to work on European plantations in the Americas.
- **The Mechanism:** Coastal states captured or bought slaves from the hinterland. Millions of able-bodied Africans were sold to European ships in exchange for firearms, gunpowder, spirits (alcohol), textiles, and brass.
- **Impact:** This trade caused massive depopulation, warfare, and suffering in the hinterland. However, it also enriched coastal middlemen (like the Niger Delta city-states) and shifted the economic centre of gravity from the northern trans-Saharan routes down to the southern coast.

### Trade routes
Trade routes were the physical paths that allowed the economy to function.
- **Overland routes:** In the north, well-established caravan trails stretched across the desert to cities like Tripoli and Tunis. Inland, narrow bush paths connected major market towns. Rulers often maintained and protected these paths, charging tolls to merchants who used them.
- **Waterways:** In the middle belt and the south, water transport was much faster and cheaper than carrying goods on heads. The Rivers Niger and Benue served as massive natural highways for large trade canoes. Along the coast, a complex, interconnected network of creeks and lagoons allowed traders to move safely without venturing into the rough Atlantic Ocean.

## Differentiating economic activities and specialties

Because the environment varied so drastically across the Nigeria area, people specialized in different economic activities. Geography dictated what a community could produce best.

| Geographical Zone | Main Occupations | Key Products and Specialties | Notable Peoples / Centers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Coastal and Riverine** (Swamps, Creeks, Coast) | Fishing, salt-making, canoe-building, trading (middlemen) | Smoked fish, sea salt, large wooden canoes, imported European goods | Ijaw, Itsekiri, Ilaje, Efik, Nembe, Kalabari |
| **Forest Zone** (Humid, heavy rainfall, tall trees) | Farming (root crops), hunting, wood-carving, palm produce extraction | Yams, cocoyams, palm oil, kola nuts, carved masks and doors | Yoruba, Edo (Benin), Igbo |
| **Savannah and Sahel** (Drier, open grasslands, tsetse-free) | Farming (cereals), animal husbandry, leather-working, cloth-dyeing | Sorghum, millet, cattle, sheep, dyed leather (Morocco leather), indigo cloth | Hausa, Fulani, Kanuri, Nupe, Tiv |

## Relating economic activities to the growth of states

The rise of large, powerful states and empires before 1800 (such as Oyo, Benin, Kanem-Borno, and the Hausa states) was not an accident. It was the direct result of successful economic activities. 

**How Agriculture Built States:**
A reliable food supply from successful farming (especially the introduction of new crops and better iron tools) led to population growth. As villages grew into towns and cities (**urbanization**), society changed. Because there was surplus food, not everyone had to farm. This allowed a **division of labour**: some people became full-time blacksmiths, weavers, priests, soldiers, or kings. A centralized government was formed to manage this complex society.

**How Industry Built States:**
Industries provided the tools of power. The mastery of **iron** was revolutionary. A state with highly skilled blacksmiths could equip its army with deadly iron spears and swords, allowing it to conquer neighbouring groups who only had wooden or stone weapons. Furthermore, industries like leather-working and cloth-making produced high-value luxury goods. The state could trade these goods to increase its wealth. 

**How Trade Built States:**
Trade was the engine of state expansion. Rulers taxed markets and trade routes (collecting **tolls**). This revenue made the kings wealthy. With this wealth, kings could hire mercenaries, buy superior weapons, and reward loyal generals, keeping the empire strong. Furthermore, states often went to war specifically to conquer and control valuable trade routes and resources (like salt lakes or slave markets). 

## Expansion of states

The expansion of major states in the Nigeria area provides clear historical examples of how economics drove political growth:

**Kanem-Borno Empire:**
Kanem-Borno expanded largely to control the lucrative trans-Saharan trade routes. By conquering territory, Borno took control of the crucial desert salt lakes at **Bilma**. The empire used the wealth generated from taxing the salt and slave trade to buy **horses** from North Africa. With these horses, Borno built a terrifying cavalry that easily conquered surrounding foot-soldiers, expanding the empire's borders massively.

**Oyo Empire:**
Oyo was located in the savannah margin, perfectly positioned between the northern grasslands and the southern forests. Oyo traded forest products (like kola nuts) to the north in exchange for horses. Like Borno, Oyo used these horses to build a highly mobile cavalry. This military advantage allowed Oyo to expand southwards through the forest, eventually reaching the coast at Porto-Novo, giving the empire total control over the lucrative trans-Atlantic trade with Europeans.

**Benin Empire:**
Benin's expansion was fueled by its highly organized industries and trade. The Oba of Benin controlled powerful guilds of craftsmen (wood-carvers, brass-casters, and blacksmiths) who produced luxury goods and weapons. Benin traded these goods, and later traded with Europeans at the coast, acquiring firearms. Using iron weapons and later guns, Benin expanded its influence across the forest region and down into the Niger Delta.

**Niger Delta City-States:**
States like Bonny, Nembe, and Kalabari expanded their power not by conquering huge tracts of land, but through economic dominance. They positioned themselves as **middlemen** in the trans-Atlantic trade. They prevented European merchants on the coast from travelling inland, and prevented inland producers from travelling to the coast. By controlling the waterway trade routes and holding a monopoly on the exchange of slaves and palm oil for European guns and goods, these small city-states amassed immense wealth and political power.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Shifting cultivation / Bush fallow** | A farming system where land is cleared, farmed until infertile, and then left to rest and recover its nutrients naturally. |
| **Terrace farming** | Cutting flat, step-like ridges into hillsides to farm on mountains without the soil washing away. |
| **Transhumance** | The seasonal movement of livestock (like cattle) by pastoralists in search of water and fresh pasture. |
| **Smelting** | The process of extracting metal (like iron) from its raw ore using high heat in a furnace. |
| **Barter** | The direct exchange of goods for other goods without using money. |
| **Cowries** | Small sea shells used as a standard currency in traditional West African markets. |
| **Trans-Saharan trade** | The ancient trade network across the Sahara Desert linking West Africa to North Africa. |
| **Middlemen** | Traders (like the Niger Delta states or the Aro) who buy goods from producers and sell them to final buyers, making a profit by controlling the route. |
| **Tsetse fly** | An insect common in the forest zone that transmits sleeping sickness (trypanosomiasis), preventing the keeping of large cattle in the south. |
| **Morocco leather** | High-quality dyed leather produced in Kano and exported across the Sahara, named after the North African region where Europeans first bought it. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Smelting vs. Blacksmithing:** Do not confuse the two. Smelting is extracting the iron from rocks (Nok, Taruga). Blacksmithing is shaping the already-smelted iron into tools and weapons.
- **The importance of Iron and Horses:** In JAMB questions, iron is usually the answer to what technological advancement led to state expansion (weapons and farm tools). Horses are the answer to what allowed northern empires (Oyo, Borno, Hausa states) to conquer wide territories (cavalry). 
- **The Tsetse Fly factor:** Remember that the tsetse fly is the main geographical reason why pastoralism (cattle rearing) was restricted to the northern savannah.
- **Direction of Trade:** The Trans-Saharan trade exported gold, slaves, and kola, and imported **salt** and **horses**. The Trans-Atlantic trade exported slaves (and later palm oil) and imported **firearms** and spirits.
- **Middlemen:** When JAMB asks about the economic role of the Niger Delta states or the Aro network, the keyword to look for is "middlemen".

## Quick summary

1. The traditional economy was built on agriculture, industries, and trade, varying heavily by geographical zone.
2. Farming (using shifting cultivation) was the primary occupation, while coastal people specialized in fishing and salt-making, and northern savannah people in animal husbandry.
3. Traditional industries included pottery, salt-making, iron-smelting, blacksmithing, leather-working (famous in Kano), wood-carving, and cloth-making.
4. Trade occurred locally (often by barter or cowries), regionally (exchanging different ecological products), and long-distance.
5. The Trans-Saharan trade linked the north to North Africa via camels, trading slaves and kola for salt and horses, and bringing Islam.
6. The Trans-Atlantic slave trade linked the coast to Europe, causing massive destruction inland but enriching coastal middleman states.
7. Agriculture created food surpluses, leading to population growth, urbanization, and a division of labour, which were the foundations of early states.
8. States expanded by using wealth from trade and superior iron weapons (or cavalry horses) to conquer neighbours and control lucrative trade routes.

## Practice questions

**1. The farming system where a piece of land is cultivated until its fertility drops, and then abandoned to recover naturally, is known as**
- A. terrace farming
- B. mixed farming
- C. shifting cultivation
- D. crop rotation

**Answer: C.** Shifting cultivation (or rotational bush fallow) involves farming a plot of land and then leaving it to rest (fallow) so the soil can naturally regain its nutrients. Crop rotation is a modern method of planting different crops on the same land to maintain fertility.

**2. Which of the following traditional industries was most crucial in providing the superior weapons needed for the expansion of early states?**
- A. Leather-working
- B. Wood-carving
- C. Iron-smelting and blacksmithing
- D. Pottery

**Answer: C.** The mastery of iron allowed states to produce superior, deadly weapons like spears and swords, which gave them a military advantage over groups using stone or wooden weapons.

**3. The rearing of large herds of cattle was mostly restricted to the northern savannah and sahel because**
- A. the northern soil was more fertile for growing yam
- B. the forest zone was infested with the tsetse fly
- C. only the Fulani knew how to domesticate animals
- D. the southern people preferred eating fish

**Answer: B.** The tsetse fly, which causes deadly sleeping sickness (trypanosomiasis) in large cattle, thrives in the humid forest zone, restricting large-scale pastoralism to the drier north.

**4. Kano became internationally famous in the traditional era for its production of**
- A. large wooden transport canoes
- B. sea salt
- C. brass and bronze statues
- D. high-quality dyed leather

**Answer: D.** Kano was renowned for its leather-working and dye pits, producing dyed leather that was traded across the Sahara and became famous in Europe as "Morocco leather".

**5. In traditional Nigerian economies, local trade in village markets was initially conducted primarily through**
- A. the use of paper money
- B. barter
- C. the exchange of gold coins
- D. writing cheques

**Answer: B.** Before the widespread use of currencies like cowries or manillas, local trade was conducted by barter, which is the direct exchange of one good for another.

**6. The Trans-Saharan trade contributed significantly to the expansion of Kanem-Borno and Oyo by providing them with**
- A. firearms and gunpowder
- B. large wooden canoes
- C. horses for their cavalry
- D. root crops for feeding the army

**Answer: C.** The wealth from the Trans-Saharan trade was used to import horses from North Africa, which allowed states like Borno and Oyo to build powerful cavalry units for rapid military expansion.

**7. Which of the following communities specialized in salt-making from seawater and fishing due to their geographical environment?**
- A. The Ijaw and Itsekiri
- B. The Hausa and Fulani
- C. The Nupe and Tiv
- D. The Kanuri and Shuwa Arabs

**Answer: A.** The Ijaw, Itsekiri, and other coastal/Niger Delta communities lived in the riverine and coastal swamps, making fishing and salt-boiling from seawater their natural specialties.

**8. The fundamental way in which successful agriculture led to the growth of centralized states was by**
- A. forcing everyone to become a farmer
- B. producing a food surplus that allowed for a division of labour and urbanization
- C. destroying all the forests to build large palaces
- D. making hunting unnecessary

**Answer: B.** A reliable food surplus meant that not everyone needed to farm. This allowed populations to grow, cities to form, and specialists (blacksmiths, soldiers, kings) to emerge, leading to state formation.

**9. During the Trans-Atlantic trade before 1800, the major role played by the Niger Delta city-states was that of**
- A. primary producers of firearms
- B. manufacturers of textiles for export
- C. middlemen between the European merchants and the hinterland
- D. missionaries spreading Christianity

**Answer: C.** The Niger Delta states monopolized the coastal trade routes, acting as wealthy middlemen who bought slaves (and later palm oil) from the interior to sell to Europeans.

**10. Which of the following is a major difference between iron-smelting and blacksmithing?**
- A. Smelting is the shaping of metal on an anvil, while blacksmithing is mining the rock.
- B. Smelting is extracting iron from raw ore using a furnace, while blacksmithing is shaping the extracted iron into tools.
- C. Smelting was only done by women, while blacksmithing was only done by men.
- D. Smelting uses only cold water, while blacksmithing uses fire.

**Answer: B.** Smelting is the primary industrial process of melting rock (ore) in a furnace to extract the raw iron metal. Blacksmithing is taking that metal and forging it into finished products using heat, a hammer, and an anvil.
