# JAMB History Notes: External Influences (North African and European Contacts)

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A, Topic 5 of the JAMB History syllabus: The Nigeria Area Up to 1800 – External Influences. It covers the early interactions between the peoples of the Nigeria area and the outside world before the 19th century.

It covers the following content items:
- **a. North Africans/Arabs/Tuaregs:**
  - i. introduction, spread and impact of Islam;
  - ii. trans-Saharan trade.
- **b. Europeans:**
  - i. early European trade with the coastal states.
  - ii. the trans-Atlantic slave trade (origin, organization and impact).
  - iii. impact of early missionary activities in the coastal areas.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
1. Assess the impact of the contact with North Africa on the people and states South of the Sahara.
2. Highlight the role of the Tuaregs in the trans-Saharan trade.
3. Examine the impact of early European contact with the coastal people.
4. Outline the impact of the early Christian missionary activities in the coastal areas.
5. Trace the origin, organization and impact of the trans-Atlantic slave trade.

**Note on the syllabus wording:** In objective iv, the syllabus has typing errors ("costal arears" and "Christians missionary activities"). These refer to "coastal areas" and "Christian missionary activities". Also, note that because this section covers history *up to 1800*, the "early Christian missionary activities" mentioned here refer mainly to the early attempts by the Portuguese in the 15th and 16th centuries, rather than the more famous 19th-century missionary wave (which is covered in later sections of the syllabus).

## Contact with North Africans, Arabs and Tuaregs

Before Europeans arrived on the coast, the major external influence on the peoples of the Nigeria area came from the north, across the Sahara Desert. This contact was maintained by North Africans, Arabs, and desert nomads like the Tuaregs.

### Trans-Saharan trade

**Content a(ii): trans-Saharan trade.**

The **trans-Saharan trade** was the vast network of commercial exchange across the Sahara Desert, linking West Africa (the regions south of the Sahara) with North Africa and the Mediterranean world. It began in ancient times but grew massively from the 8th century onwards following the introduction of the camel.

**How the trade was organized:**
- The trade was conducted using large convoys of camels called **caravans**. Camels were essential because they could go for days without water and carry heavy loads across the hot desert sands.
- **Trade routes:** Major routes connected Nigerian states to North Africa. One major route went from **Kano** (in Hausaland) through Agadez to Tripoli (in modern Libya). Another went from **Kanem-Borno** through the Fezzan to Tripoli and Egypt.
- **Articles of trade:** 
  - **From West Africa (South to North):** The primary exports were gold, slaves, ivory, kola nuts, ostrich feathers, and animal hides. Kano was famous for exporting dyed cotton cloth (Morocco leather and Kano cloth).
  - **From North Africa (North to South):** West Africans needed salt (mined in the desert oases like Bilma), horses, copper, cowrie shells (used as currency), weapons (swords and firearms), glassware, and books.

### The role of the Tuaregs in the trans-Saharan trade

**Objective ii: highlight the role of the Tuaregs in the trans-Saharan trade.**

The Tuaregs are a nomadic Berber people who live in the Sahara Desert. Often called the "lords of the desert" because of their mastery of the harsh environment, they were absolutely vital to the success and survival of the trans-Saharan trade. Their roles included:

1. **Guides (Takshif):** The Sahara is a vast, featureless desert with deadly sandstorms. The Tuaregs knew the locations of water wells (oases) and the safest routes. They served as expert guides, without whom caravans would easily get lost and die of thirst.
2. **Guards and Protectors:** Caravans carried highly valuable goods (gold, slaves, salt) and were frequent targets for desert robbers. Tuareg warriors were hired to guard the caravans for a fee.
3. **Suppliers of Camels:** The Tuaregs bred and maintained the thousands of camels needed for the trade. They rented or sold these beasts of burden to merchants.
4. **Maintenance of Oases:** They controlled the desert oases, keeping the water wells clean and accessible, which was crucial for the survival of the caravans.
5. **Middlemen and Traders:** Some Tuaregs were merchants themselves, trading salt from desert mines (like Bilma and Taghaza) for food crops from the savanna.
6. **Threats and Raiders:** It is important to note that if they were not paid protection money, Tuaregs could also be dangerous raiders who attacked and looted trade caravans.

### Introduction, spread and impact of Islam

**Content a(i): introduction, spread and impact of Islam.**

Islam was introduced to the Nigeria area not by military conquest, but peacefully through the **trans-Saharan trade**. North African and Arab merchants, as well as Muslim scholars, brought the religion with them.

- **In Kanem-Borno:** Islam was established very early. According to historical records, the first Mai (king) to convert to Islam was **Mai Umme Jilmi** in the 11th century (around 1085). By the 13th century, under Mai Dunama Dibbalemi, Islam had become the state religion, and Borno established diplomatic ties with Muslim states in North Africa and the Middle East, even building a hostel in Cairo for Borno pilgrims.
- **In Hausaland:** Islam arrived a bit later. According to the Kano Chronicle, Islam was introduced to Kano in the 14th century during the reign of **King Yaji**, brought by Wangara traders (Wangarawa) from the Mali Empire. In the 15th century, during the reign of **Muhammad Rumfa**, Islam became firmly established as the state religion in Kano, and the famous scholar Al-Maghili visited to write a guide on Islamic government. Katsina also became a major center of Islamic learning.

### Impact of contact with North Africa on the people and states South of the Sahara

**Objective i: assess the impact of the contact with North Africa on the people and states South of the Sahara.**

The prolonged contact with North Africa via trade and religion deeply changed the political, economic, and social landscape of the savanna states (like Kanem-Borno and the Hausa states). 

1. **Introduction of Literacy and Education:** The contact brought the Arabic language and writing script. Arabic became the language of diplomacy, record-keeping, and learning. Qur'anic schools were established, making towns like Kano and Katsina centers of scholarship.
2. **Political and Administrative Changes:** Islam introduced new concepts of government. Traditional rulers adopted Islamic titles (like Sultan or Emir) and used Sharia (Islamic law) to govern their people and settle disputes. Rulers employed literate Muslim scholars as advisers and judges (Alkalis).
3. **Economic Growth:** The trans-Saharan trade brought immense wealth to the states of the savanna. Kings grew rich from taxing the trade, which allowed them to maintain large armies and expand their territories.
4. **Military Strength:** The importation of North African horses (essential for savanna cavalry) and later firearms gave states like Kanem-Borno a massive military advantage over their neighbors.
5. **Architectural Developments:** Contact introduced new building styles, such as the use of rectangular mud-brick houses, flat roofs, and the construction of elaborate mosques and palaces.
6. **Cultural Influence:** The contact influenced local dress (the wearing of turbans and flowing gowns/riga), dietary habits, and social customs, heavily integrating them with Islamic practices.
7. **Diplomatic Relations:** It connected West African states to the wider Islamic world. Rulers went on pilgrimage (Hajj) to Mecca, creating alliances with foreign powers.

## Early European Contact and Trade

While the northern parts of the Nigeria area were looking across the Sahara, the coastal areas in the south eventually began to interact with Europeans arriving by sea.

### Early European trade with the coastal states

**Content b(i): early European trade with the coastal states.**

In the late 15th century, European explorers and merchants began arriving on the coast of West Africa. They were looking for a sea route to India and new sources of wealth.

- **The Portuguese:** The Portuguese were the first Europeans to reach the Nigerian coast. In **1472**, the Portuguese explorer **Ruy de Sequeira** reached the Bight of Benin. Shortly after, the Portuguese visited the Oba of Benin.
- **Legitimate Trade:** At first, the trade was "legitimate" (not involving human beings). The Portuguese were primarily interested in **pepper, ivory, gold, and coral beads**. 
- **The Exchange:** In return for African goods, the Europeans brought manufactured items such as **copper, brass, textiles (cloth), cowrie shells, beads, umbrellas, mirrors, and later, firearms and alcohol**.
- The trade soon expanded to other coastal communities, including Warri, Calabar, Bonny, and Brass. By the 17th century, other European nations—such as the Dutch, British, and French—had joined the trade, breaking the Portuguese monopoly.

### Impact of early European contact with the coastal people

**Objective iii: examine the impact of early European contact with the coastal people.**

The arrival of Europeans on the coast had a profound impact on the societies of southern Nigeria:

1. **Shift of Economic Power:** Before European contact, the economic center of gravity in West Africa was the savanna (due to trans-Saharan trade). The arrival of Europeans shifted wealth and power to the coastal region (the Atlantic trade).
2. **Rise of Coastal Middlemen:** Coastal peoples, such as the **Itsekiri, Ijo (Ijaw), Efik, and Nembe**, became extremely wealthy and powerful. They acted as **middlemen**, preventing Europeans from going inland and preventing hinterland people from coming to the coast. They bought goods from the interior and sold them to the Europeans at a high profit.
3. **Introduction of New Crops:** This is a very important impact. The Portuguese brought new food crops from the Americas (the New World) to West Africa. These included **cassava, maize (corn), tomatoes, pawpaw, and sweet potatoes**. These hardy crops improved food security and led to population growth.
4. **Change in Warfare:** The introduction of European firearms (guns and gunpowder) revolutionized warfare. States that acquired guns could easily defeat their neighbors, leading to increased conflicts and empire-building.
5. **Rise of City-States:** Small fishing villages in the Niger Delta grew into powerful, wealthy trading city-states (like Bonny and Kalabari) based on the wealth generated from European trade.
6. **Transition to the Slave Trade:** Sadly, the early trade in goods gradually shifted into the devastating trans-Atlantic slave trade as European demand for labor in the Americas grew.

## The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade

**Content b(ii): the trans-Atlantic slave trade (origin, organization and impact).**
**Objective v: trace the origin, organization and impact of the trans-Atlantic slave trade.**

The trans-Atlantic slave trade was the capture, sale, and forced transportation of millions of Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas. It lasted from the 16th to the 19th centuries.

### Origin of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade

1. **Discovery of the "New World":** In 1492, Christopher Columbus reached the Americas. European powers (Spain, Portugal, Britain, France) soon established vast colonies there.
2. **The Need for Labor:** The Europeans set up huge plantations to grow cash crops like sugar, tobacco, and cotton, as well as lucrative silver and gold mines. These required a massive, cheap labor force.
3. **Failure of Indigenous Labor:** At first, Europeans enslaved the indigenous Native Americans. However, millions of Native Americans died from brutal treatment and European diseases (like smallpox). Attempts to use poor European servants also failed because they could not handle the tropical climate and diseases like malaria.
4. **The Turn to Africa:** Africans were seen as physically strong, skilled in tropical agriculture, and accustomed to the hot climate. Thus, the Europeans turned to West Africa as a seemingly endless source of forced labor, beginning the trans-Atlantic slave trade.

### Organization of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade

The trade was organized in a complex, three-part system known as the **Triangular Trade**:

- **Leg 1 (Europe to Africa):** European ships left ports (like Liverpool or Nantes) loaded with manufactured goods such as firearms, gunpowder, cheap textiles, alcohol (rum), and iron bars. They sailed to the West African coast and exchanged these goods for enslaved Africans.
- **Leg 2 (The Middle Passage - Africa to the Americas):** The enslaved Africans were packed tightly into the dark, filthy holds of ships and transported across the Atlantic Ocean. This journey, known as the Middle Passage, was horrific. Captives were chained, fed poorly, and suffered from diseases like dysentery. Many died and were thrown overboard.
- **Leg 3 (The Americas to Europe):** In the Americas, the surviving Africans were sold to plantation owners. The ships were then cleaned and loaded with plantation products grown by slave labor (sugar, tobacco, cotton, molasses) and sailed back to Europe to be sold at a massive profit.

**How slaves were acquired in Nigeria:**
Europeans rarely went inland because of the risk of malaria and strong coastal armies. Instead, they stayed in their ships or in coastal forts (barracoons). Slaves were supplied by **African coastal middlemen** and inland states. Captives were acquired through:
- **Warfare:** The most common method. States went to war specifically to capture prisoners to sell.
- **Raiding:** Surprise attacks on weaker villages.
- **Kidnapping:** Individuals were frequently kidnapped while traveling or farming.
- **Judicial punishment:** Criminals or debtors were sometimes sold into slavery as punishment.
- **Oracles:** In Igboland, the Aro people used the powerful **Aro Chukwu oracle (Ibin Ukpabi)** to manipulate people and acquire slaves.

### Impact of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade

The impact of this trade on the Nigeria area was devastating and long-lasting:

1. **Demographic Drain (Loss of Population):** Millions of the youngest, strongest, and most productive men and women were taken away. This severely crippled population growth and deprived societies of their workforce.
2. **Economic Stagnation:** Legitimate agriculture, local industries (like metalworking and cloth weaving), and normal trade declined rapidly. It was easier and more profitable for leaders to hunt and sell people than to engage in productive economic activities.
3. **Increased Warfare and Instability:** The trade created a vicious "gun-slave cycle." To survive, a state needed guns. To get guns from Europeans, they had to sell slaves. To get slaves, they had to wage war. This led to centuries of chaos, insecurity, and fear across the region.
4. **Rise of New Military States:** Some states became very powerful and wealthy by participating actively in the trade. For example, the Oyo Empire and coastal city-states like Bonny grew rich by supplying slaves. However, this wealth was built on the destruction of neighboring communities.
5. **Decay of Traditional Values:** Human life lost its value. Greed replaced traditional systems of justice, as leaders began manipulating laws to condemn minor offenders to slavery.

## Early Christian Missionary Activities

**Content b(iii): impact of early missionary activities in the coastal areas.**
**Objective iv: outline the impact of the early Christian missionary activities in the coastal areas.**

*Exam Tip: The syllabus covers the history "Up to 1800". Therefore, "early Christian missionary activities" here strictly refers to the pioneering efforts by the Portuguese in the 15th, 16th, and 17th centuries, mainly in Benin and Warri. Most textbooks focus heavily on the later 19th-century wave (starting in Badagry in 1842 with Thomas Birch Freeman and Ajayi Crowther). We will outline the early Portuguese impact, but also summarize the general impact of missionaries in case JAMB tests the wider effects of Christianity under this objective.*

### Early Missionary Activities (Up to 1800)

When the Portuguese arrived in the 15th century, they brought Catholic priests with them.
- **In Benin:** Catholic missionaries visited the court of the Oba of Benin in the 15th and 16th centuries (particularly during the reign of Oba Esigie). Churches were briefly built, and some chiefs learned to read Portuguese. However, the Oba was more interested in getting Portuguese guns than their religion, and traditional Benin religion remained dominant.
- **In Warri:** The Portuguese had more success in the Itsekiri kingdom of Warri. In the late 16th/early 17th century, the Olu of Warri allowed missionaries to preach. One Olu even sent his son, **Prince Domingos**, to Portugal to be educated. Domingos returned with a Portuguese wife, and several successive Olus were professing Catholics.

**Why the early (pre-1800) missions failed:**
By the 18th century, these early Catholic missions had virtually died out. The reasons included:
1. The deadly climate and diseases (malaria) which killed many European priests.
2. The lack of sustained interest from the Portuguese, who became fully consumed by the profitable slave trade.
3. The strong attachment of the local people to their traditional religions.

### Impact of Early Christian Missionary Activities

Whether looking at the early Portuguese attempts or the very beginning of the 19th-century wave, the impact of missionaries in the coastal areas included:

1. **Introduction of Christianity:** They planted the first seeds of the Christian faith in the coastal areas, challenging traditional religious practices.
2. **Introduction of Western Education:** The missionaries were the first to introduce formal Western education and literacy. In Warri, the Portuguese established early schools for the royal family. (Later, in the 19th century, missionaries built hundreds of schools).
3. **Cultural Influence:** The early missions introduced elements of European culture, dress, and language. Some coastal chiefs learned to speak and write in Portuguese (and later English) to facilitate trade and diplomacy.
4. **Social Reforms:** Missionaries actively campaigned against certain traditional practices they considered barbaric, such as human sacrifice and the killing of twins. 
5. **Strengthening of Trade Ties:** The presence of missionaries often made European merchants more comfortable trading with specific coastal kingdoms, increasing the wealth and influence of those areas.
6. **Linguistic Work:** As missionary work developed, they began studying local languages to preach effectively, which eventually led to the translation of the Bible and the writing down of Nigerian languages (though this was fully realized later by figures like Bishop Ajayi Crowther).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Trans-Saharan Trade** | Trade across the Sahara Desert linking West Africa to North Africa. |
| **Tuaregs** | Nomadic Berber people of the Sahara who served as guides, guards, and merchants in the desert trade. |
| **Caravan** | A large group of merchants and camels traveling together for safety across the desert. |
| **Triangular Trade** | The three-legged trade route linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas during the era of the slave trade. |
| **Middle Passage** | The horrific journey across the Atlantic Ocean on slave ships, carrying enslaved Africans to the Americas. |
| **Middlemen** | Coastal merchants (like the Efik or Ijo) who bought goods/slaves from the interior and sold them to Europeans on the coast. |
| **Legitimate Trade** | Trade in commodities (like pepper, palm oil, ivory) as opposed to trade in human beings. |
| **Sharia** | Islamic law, introduced to states in northern Nigeria through contact with North Africa. |
| **Barracoon** | An enclosure or fort on the coast where enslaved people were held before being put on ships. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Common mistake:** Confusing the trans-Saharan trade (North) with the trans-Atlantic trade (South). Trans-Saharan used camels, went north to Arabs, and traded gold/salt. Trans-Atlantic used ships, went south/west to Europeans, and heavily involved slaves.
- **Tuareg roles:** Questions frequently ask about the Tuaregs. Remember they were the **guides and guards** of the desert. They were not the main kings of West Africa, nor were they European.
- **New Crops:** Always remember that **cassava and maize** were introduced by early European (Portuguese) contact from the Americas. They are not indigenous to Nigeria.
- **Missionaries up to 1800:** If a question asks about the very first Europeans to introduce Christianity or trade in the 15th/16th centuries, the answer is always the **Portuguese**, and the main areas were **Benin and Warri**.
- **Shift of Power:** A very common JAMB question asks about the economic effect of European contact. The key answer is that it shifted the economic center of gravity from the **savanna to the coast**.

## Quick summary

1. Contact with North Africa via the trans-Saharan trade brought Islam, Arabic literacy, Sharia law, and immense wealth to the savanna states (like Kanem-Borno and Kano).
2. The Tuaregs were essential to the trans-Saharan trade, acting as expert guides, protectors, and providers of camels.
3. The Portuguese were the first Europeans to trade on the coast in the 15th century, buying pepper and ivory, and introducing new crops like cassava and maize.
4. Early European trade shifted economic power from the northern savanna to the coastal states, enriching coastal middlemen who controlled access to the interior.
5. The trans-Atlantic slave trade began due to the European need for cheap labor on American plantations after indigenous populations declined.
6. Organized as a Triangular Trade, the slave trade caused massive depopulation, increased warfare (the gun-slave cycle), and economic stagnation in West Africa.
7. Early Christian missionary efforts by the Portuguese up to 1800 had limited religious success but introduced early Western education and European cultural influence in places like Warri.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary role of the Tuaregs in the trans-Saharan trade was to**
- A. manufacture the goods sold in North Africa
- B. act as guides and protectors for the caravans
- C. build ships for coastal merchants
- D. introduce Christianity to the savanna

**Answer: B.** The Tuaregs were masters of the desert. They knew the routes and oases, serving as vital guides (takshif) and guards for the trade caravans.

**2. Which of the following crops were introduced to the West African coast by early European traders?**
- A. Yam and palm produce
- B. Millet and sorghum
- C. Cassava and maize
- D. Kola nut and cocoa

**Answer: C.** Early Portuguese traders brought crops from the Americas (the New World), including cassava, maize, and sweet potatoes, which greatly impacted local agriculture.

**3. The trans-Atlantic slave trade was organized primarily around a system known as the**
- A. Saharan route
- B. Legitimate trade
- C. Triangular trade
- D. Middlemen system

**Answer: C.** The trade formed a triangle: manufactured goods from Europe to Africa, enslaved people from Africa to the Americas (the Middle Passage), and raw materials from the Americas to Europe.

**4. A major political impact of the contact with North Africa on the states of the Nigerian savanna was the**
- A. adoption of Western education
- B. introduction of Sharia law and Islamic administration
- C. introduction of the gun-slave cycle
- D. shift of power to the coastal middlemen

**Answer: B.** Contact with North Africa brought Islam, which led to the adoption of Islamic administrative practices, titles, and Sharia law by rulers in Kanem-Borno and Hausaland.

**5. The journey of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic Ocean in overcrowded ships was known as the**
- A. Middle Passage
- B. Trans-Saharan route
- C. Royal Niger crossing
- D. Legitimate voyage

**Answer: A.** The Middle Passage was the brutal, deadly second leg of the triangular trade where captive Africans were transported to the Americas.

**6. The very first European nation to establish trade contact and attempt missionary work on the Nigerian coast was**
- A. Britain
- B. France
- C. Portugal
- D. Spain

**Answer: C.** The Portuguese, arriving in the 1470s under explorers like Ruy de Sequeira, were the first to reach the coast, trading with Benin and attempting early missionary work in Warri.

**7. How did the arrival of Europeans on the coast affect the economic geography of the Nigeria area?**
- A. It made the Sahara Desert the only route for international trade
- B. It shifted the economic center of gravity from the savanna to the coast
- C. It caused the coastal people to abandon trade for farming
- D. It destroyed the wealth of the Niger Delta city-states

**Answer: B.** Before European arrival, wealth was concentrated in the north due to the trans-Saharan trade. European sea trade shifted wealth and power southwards to the coastal middlemen.

**8. Which of the following was a major negative consequence of the trans-Atlantic slave trade on West Africa?**
- A. It led to the immediate colonization of the continent
- B. It caused a massive demographic drain and increased warfare
- C. It prevented the spread of Islam in the north
- D. It stopped the trans-Saharan trade completely

**Answer: B.** The trade removed millions of young, productive people (demographic drain) and fueled constant warfare as states fought to capture slaves in exchange for European guns.

**9. In the 15th and 16th centuries, which Nigerian ruler sent his son to Portugal for education during early missionary contacts?**
- A. The Oba of Benin
- B. The Sultan of Sokoto
- C. The Olu of Warri
- D. The Amanyanabo of Bonny

**Answer: C.** The Olu of Warri established strong ties with the Portuguese, and Prince Domingos was sent to Portugal to be educated, returning with a Portuguese wife.

**10. Before the large-scale shift to the slave trade, early European legitimate trade with the coastal states heavily involved the export of**
- A. palm oil and groundnuts
- B. pepper and ivory
- C. crude oil and coal
- D. cotton and indigo

**Answer: B.** The initial Portuguese trade in the 15th century focused on legitimate items like pepper (from Benin), ivory, and gold, before the demand for slave labor in the Americas grew.
