# JAMB History Notes: Yorubaland in the 19th Century

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B, Topic 3 of the JAMB History syllabus: Yorubaland. It covers the political changes, civil wars and peace treaties that shaped the Yoruba-speaking region in the 19th century. 

This file covers the following content items:
- **a. The fall of the Old Oyo Empire;**
- **b. The Yoruba wars and their impact;**
- **c. The peace treaty of 1886 and its aftermath.**

## The Fall of the Old Oyo Empire

**Objective i: examine the causes and the implications of the fall of the Old Oyo Empire.**
**Content a: The fall of the Old Oyo Empire.**

Up to the late 18th century, the **Old Oyo Empire** was the most powerful state in the Nigeria area, controlling much of Yorubaland, as well as Borgu, Nupe and the Kingdom of Dahomey (in present-day Republic of Benin). Its capital was **Oyo-Ile** (also known as Katunga), located far to the north in the savanna. 

However, by the late 18th century, the empire began to decline, and by the 1830s, it collapsed entirely. The fall of this great empire was brought about by a mixture of internal weaknesses and external pressures.

### Internal causes of the fall

**1. Constitutional crisis and power struggles**
In the Old Oyo political system, the king, the **Alaafin**, was theoretically an absolute monarch (*Alase ekeji Orisa*). However, his power was checked by the **Oyomesi**, a powerful council of seven non-royal chiefs led by the **Bashorun** (prime minister). If the Alaafin became tyrannical, the Oyomesi could force him to commit suicide by presenting him with an empty calabash (or parrot's eggs). The military was led by the **Are Ona Kakanfo** (Generalissimo), who lived outside the capital and was expected to win his battles or die fighting.
In the mid-18th century, this balance of power broke down. A powerful prime minister, **Bashorun Gaa**, usurped power and cruelly executed several Alaafins. Although Alaafin Abiodun eventually defeated and killed Gaa in 1774, the long power struggle severely weakened the central authority of the empire. 

**2. The curse of Alaafin Awole and Afonja's rebellion**
When Alaafin Abiodun died around 1789, he was succeeded by **Alaafin Awole**. Awole soon clashed with his military commander, **Afonja**, the Are Ona Kakanfo, who was stationed in the northern town of Ilorin. In 1795, Awole ordered Afonja and the army to attack Iwere-Ile (a town protected by a sacred curse) and Apomu (a market town belonging to Ife). This was a suicide mission for the army. Instead of obeying, Afonja and the army mutinied. In 1796, they sent the empty calabash to Awole, forcing him to commit suicide.
According to Yoruba oral tradition, before Awole died, he stepped outside, shot three arrows to the north, south and west, and placed a curse on the Yoruba nation, declaring that they would be perpetually torn apart by internal wars and sold into slavery.

**3. Oppressive rule by provincial governors**
Oyo ruled its vast territories through resident supervisors called **Ajeles** (or *Ilari*). Over time, many Ajeles became corrupt and oppressive, demanding excessive tributes and treating the subject towns cruelly. This caused deep hatred for Oyo rule and made vassal states eager to rebel.

**4. The large size of the empire**
The empire had simply grown too large to manage effectively. With a weakened central government in Oyo-Ile and a divided army, it became impossible to police the distant provinces or stop rebellions.

### External causes of the fall

**1. Rebellions by vassal states**
Seeing the political chaos in Oyo-Ile, conquered states began to declare independence. The **Egba** broke away successfully under their leader Lisabi in the late 18th century. The Bariba (Borgu) and Nupe also freed themselves. In 1818, the Kingdom of **Dahomey**, which had paid tribute to Oyo for decades, declared its independence under King Gezo, cutting off Oyo's wealth from tribute.

**2. The Sokoto Jihad and the loss of Ilorin**
After Alaafin Awole's death, Afonja declared Ilorin independent from Oyo. To build a strong army, Afonja recruited runaway Hausa slaves and Oyo Muslims, forming a ruthless fighting force called the **Jama'a**. He also invited a wandering Fulani Islamic scholar, **Malam Alimi**, to be his spiritual adviser. 
However, Afonja's new allies soon became uncontrollable. When Afonja tried to disband the Jama'a after Alimi's death, they turned against him. Led by Alimi's son, **Abdulsalami**, the Jama'a and Fulani jihadists killed Afonja (around 1823 or 1824). Ilorin became a Fulani Emirate and part of the **Sokoto Caliphate**. Ilorin then became a permanent military base from which jihadist cavalry launched devastating attacks deeper into Oyo territory.

**3. Shift in trade routes**
Oyo-Ile was built on wealth from the trans-Saharan trade routes. By the 19th century, the Atlantic slave trade and legitimate commerce at the coast had become much more important. Economic power shifted to the coastal and forest states, leaving the northern capital impoverished and lacking access to modern European firearms.

### The final collapse (The Eleduwe War)
The Ilorin cavalry, eager to push Islam to the sea, relentlessly attacked Oyo towns. The final Alaafin of Old Oyo, **Alaafin Oluewu**, made a desperate alliance with the King of Borgu (Eleduwe) to drive the Fulani out of Ilorin. The allied forces were defeated by Ilorin in the **Eleduwe War**. Oyo-Ile was sacked, burnt and entirely abandoned. 

**Note on dates:** Textbooks and historians differ on the exact date Oyo-Ile was destroyed. Most commonly, it is cited as **1835** or **1837**. Because of this, JAMB questions generally focus on the causes and the people involved rather than the exact year of the city's fall.

### Implications of the fall of the Old Oyo Empire

The collapse of the greatest Yoruba power had massive and long-lasting effects:

1. **Massive population displacement:** The destruction of Oyo-Ile and northern Yoruba towns caused a huge wave of refugees fleeing southwards towards the safety of the forests.
2. **Rise of new successor states:** To survive, the refugees and warriors built new, heavily fortified towns that grew into powerful city-states. The most important were:
   - **Ibadan:** Founded originally as a war camp, it grew into a powerful military republic.
   - **Abeokuta:** Founded around 1830 by Egba refugees led by **Sodeke**, who settled under the protection of the Olumo Rock.
   - **Ijaye:** Founded by Oyo refugees led by the powerful warlord **Kurunmi**.
   - **New Oyo (Oyo-Atiba):** A surviving prince, Atiba, moved the capital south to Ago-Oja and declared it the new Oyo capital, relying on Ibadan and Ijaye for military protection.
3. **A century of civil wars:** The fall of Oyo left a power vacuum (no single ruler in charge). The new states spent the rest of the 19th century fighting each other to see who would replace Oyo as the dominant power.
4. **Spread of Islam:** Ilorin's position as a jihadist outpost facilitated the rapid spread of Islam into northern and central Yorubaland.
5. **Rise of a new military class:** Power shifted away from traditional kings based on royal blood (like the Alaafin) to a new class of professional warriors and warlords called the **Ologun**, whose authority was based on their military skill.

## The Yoruba Civil Wars and Their Impact

**Objective ii: examine the causes and effects of the Yoruba wars.**
**Content b: The Yoruba wars and their impact.**

Following the collapse of Old Oyo, Yorubaland was plunged into a series of devastating civil wars that lasted almost continuously from the 1820s to 1886. 

### General causes of the Yoruba Wars

1. **The power vacuum left by Old Oyo:** With Oyo gone, states like Ibadan, Ijaye and Abeokuta fought for supremacy. Ibadan, in particular, wanted to build a new empire over the rest of Yorubaland.
2. **Control of the trade routes:** The new states desperately needed European firearms (Dane guns and rifles) to survive, which meant they needed direct access to the coastal ports (especially Lagos). Coastal states like Ijebu and Egba (Abeokuta) often blocked these routes, leading to wars with inland states like Ibadan.
3. **The threat of the Ilorin Jihad:** The need to stop the Fulani cavalry from conquering the rest of Yorubaland led to major defensive wars.
4. **Boundary disputes and land hunger:** With thousands of refugees needing land to farm and settle, conflicts over territory were common.
5. **Personal ambitions of the Ologun:** The new military leaders gained immense wealth, slaves and prestige from warfare, giving them a personal interest in continuing the fighting.

### Major phases and battles of the Yoruba Wars

The conflicts can be divided into several major wars.

#### 1. The Owu War (c. 1820–1825)
This was the first major inter-state war of the 19th century. It began over a dispute at the **Apomu market**, where an Owu man and an Ijebu trader clashed over the sale of alligator pepper (and the capture of slaves). 
The Ijebu formed an alliance with the Ife army, and they were later joined by Oyo refugees. They laid a brutal siege to the walled city of Owu for several years. Eventually, Owu was starved into submission and completely destroyed. The destruction of Owu sent more refugees fleeing and set the pattern of total warfare that would dominate the century.

#### 2. The Osogbo War (1840)
By 1840, the Ilorin Fulani cavalry had swept deep into Yorubaland, threatening the town of Osogbo. If Osogbo fell, the whole of Yorubaland might have been absorbed into the Sokoto Caliphate. 
The Ibadan army, using disciplined infantry tactics and relying on firearms rather than horses, marched to Osogbo and ambushed the Ilorin cavalry. Ibadan won a decisive victory. 
**Significance:** The Osogbo War halted the Fulani jihadist advance into Yorubaland permanently and cemented Ibadan's position as the supreme military power in the region.

#### 3. The Batedo War (1844)
This was a brief supremacy clash between Ibadan and Ijaye. Both towns were growing rapidly and testing each other's strength, though it ended without a clear victor.

#### 4. The Ijaye War (1860–1862)
This war was sparked by a mix of political tradition and the rivalry between Ibadan and Ijaye.
- **The cause:** In 1859, Alaafin Atiba of New Oyo died. He had arranged for his son, **Adelu**, to succeed him. However, ancient Oyo tradition dictated that the crown prince (*Aremo*) must commit suicide when the Alaafin dies. **Kurunmi**, the powerful ruler of Ijaye, refused to recognise Adelu, arguing that tradition must be respected. Ibadan, however, supported Adelu.
- **The course:** The dispute turned into a proxy war for supremacy. Ibadan laid siege to Ijaye. The Egba of Abeokuta allied with Ijaye, fearing that if Ibadan won, they would be too powerful. Despite Egba's help, the Ibadan army was too strong. They cut off Ijaye's food supplies, and in 1862, Ijaye was completely destroyed. Kurunmi died during the war.

#### 5. The Kiriji / Ekitiparapo War (1877–1886)
This was the longest, bloodiest and most technologically advanced of all the Yoruba wars.
- **The cause:** After defeating Ijaye, Ibadan built a vast empire, taking over the Ijesha and Ekiti lands in the east. Ibadan ruled these areas through resident administrators called **Ajeles**. The Ajeles were brutal, seizing property and women. The spark came when an Ajele in Okemesi misbehaved, and a local warrior named **Fabunmi** beheaded him. This sparked a mass revolt against Ibadan rule.
- **The course:** The Ijesha and Ekiti people formed a powerful military alliance called the **Ekitiparapo**, led by the famous Ilesha warlord **Ogedengbe**. They were supported by Ife, Egba, and Ijebu, meaning Ibadan was fighting almost the rest of Yorubaland.
- **The weapons:** Educated Ekiti and Ijesha elites living in Lagos smuggled modern breech-loading rifles (Snider rifles) to the Ekitiparapo forces. When fired, these rifles made a loud *"Ki-ri-ji"* sound, which gave the war its name. Ibadan was led by **Aare Latosa**. The two massive armies met in the hills of Igbajo and Imesi-Ile (the Kiriji battlefield) and fought to a bloody, exhausting stalemate that lasted for years.

### Effects of the Yoruba wars

The wars drastically transformed Yoruba society:

1. **Massive loss of life and destruction of property:** Hundreds of ancient towns and villages were burnt to the ground and never rebuilt. Farming was heavily disrupted, leading to periods of severe famine.
2. **Enslavement and demographic changes:** Captives of war were sold as slaves. This period fed the last great wave of the Atlantic slave trade, creating large Yoruba diaspora populations in Brazil, Cuba and Sierra Leone. Internally, slavery grew massively as captives were used for farming and carrying goods.
3. **Political fragmentation:** The dream of uniting Yorubaland under one ruler failed. Instead, Yorubaland remained divided into independent, heavily armed city-states.
4. **Rise of the Ologun:** Traditional Obas (kings) and councils lost power to the military commanders (the Ologun) who dictated state policy based on the needs of the army.
5. **Urbanisation:** To protect themselves, people abandoned small villages and clustered together in massive walled cities. Ibadan and Abeokuta grew into some of the largest cities in pre-colonial Africa as a direct result of the wars.
6. **Opening the door for British colonialism:** Decades of fighting exhausted the Yoruba states. The disruption of trade eventually forced the British in Lagos to intervene, setting the stage for the complete British takeover of Yorubaland.

## The Peace Treaty of 1886 and Its Aftermath

**Objective iii: assess the impact of the 1886 peace treaty.**
**Content c: The peace treaty of 1886 and its aftermath.**

By the 1880s, the Kiriji War was at a stalemate. Neither Ibadan nor the Ekitiparapo could win. The war had effectively closed the trade routes from the interior to the coast.

### British intervention

The British had annexed Lagos as a colony in 1861. The prosperity of Lagos depended entirely on trade (especially palm oil and kernels) from the Yoruba interior. The Kiriji War stopped this trade, hurting British merchants and reducing the Lagos government's tax revenues.
The British Governor of Lagos, **Alfred Moloney**, decided that peace had to be imposed. He used educated Yoruba Christian missionaries, notably **Rev. Samuel Johnson** (who later wrote the famous book *The History of the Yorubas*) and **Rev. Charles Phillips**, as messengers to negotiate with the warring leaders at the Kiriji camps.

### Terms of the 1886 Peace Treaty

After intense negotiations, representatives of the warring factions travelled to Lagos, where the **1886 Peace Treaty** was signed on **23 September 1886**. 

The main provisions of the treaty were:
1. **Declaration of peace:** A general peace was proclaimed across Yorubaland.
2. **Independence of the Ekitiparapo:** Ibadan was forced to recognise the independence of the Ekiti, Ijesha and other towns. The oppressive Ajele system was abolished in these areas.
3. **Disbandment of armies:** Both the Ibadan and Ekitiparapo armies were ordered to break down their camps at Kiriji and return to their hometowns.
4. **Recognition of traditional rulers:** The Alaafin of Oyo and the Awujale of Ijebu were recognised and respected in their traditional roles.
5. **The Modakeke resettlement:** Modakeke was a settlement of Oyo refugees living on land belonging to Ife. During the wars, Modakeke had fought against Ife. The treaty ordered that the people of Modakeke must leave Ife territory and be resettled elsewhere, while Ife would be rebuilt.

### Impact and aftermath of the Treaty

The treaty was a turning point, but it did not solve all the problems immediately. Its impacts include:

1. **End of the main Kiriji conflict:** The treaty successfully broke up the massive armies camped at Kiriji. The men returned home, bringing the bloodiest phase of the Yoruba wars to an end.
2. **Failure of the Modakeke clause:** The people of Modakeke flatly refused to leave their homes and rebuild elsewhere. This failure left a bitter legacy, resulting in the violent Ife-Modakeke conflicts that continued well into the 20th and 21st centuries.
3. **Continued fighting in the North:** The treaty did not include Ilorin. Because of this, Ibadan forces refused to fully disband their northern camps and continued fighting against the Ilorin cavalry around Offa and Ogbomoso for several more years.
4. **Increased British prestige and influence:** The warring states had accepted the British Governor as a higher authority and peacemaker. This drastically increased British political influence in the hinterland.
5. **Economic recovery:** The trade routes to Lagos were gradually reopened, allowing commerce and agriculture to recover.
6. **The final pacification (1893):** Because sporadic fighting continued, the next British Governor, **Gilbert Carter**, undertook an expedition into the interior in 1893. He signed new, binding treaties with Abeokuta and New Oyo, and forced the Ibadan army to finally break up its last camps. By 1893, Yorubaland was completely pacified and effectively became a British Protectorate.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mixing up the clashes in Old Oyo:** Remember that Alaafin Abiodun clashed with **Bashorun Gaa**, while Alaafin Awole clashed with **Afonja**. It was Afonja's rebellion that brought the Fulani jihadists to Ilorin.
- **The Battle of Osogbo (1840):** A very common JAMB question asks which battle stopped the Fulani Jihad from conquering Yorubaland. The answer is the **Osogbo War**, and it was won by **Ibadan**, not Oyo.
- **Causes of the wars:** Do not confuse the cause of the Owu War (Apomu market dispute) with the Ijaye War (refusal of Kurunmi to accept Alaafin Adelu's succession) or the Kiriji War (revolt against Ibadan's Ajele system).
- **The date of the peace treaty vs. final pacification:** The major peace treaty that ended the Kiriji War was signed in **1886**. However, full pacification and the final disbanding of camps was only achieved by Governor Carter in **1893**.
- **Afonja's title:** Afonja was the **Are Ona Kakanfo** (military commander), never an Alaafin or a king. 
- **The peacemakers:** Remember the role of educated missionaries, especially Rev. Samuel Johnson, in negotiating the 1886 treaty.

## Quick summary

1. The Old Oyo Empire collapsed in the early 19th century due to internal power struggles (the clash between Alaafin Awole and Afonja), oppressive rule, the rebellion of vassal states, and the loss of Ilorin to the Sokoto Jihad.
2. Ilorin jihadist cavalry repeatedly attacked Oyo, ultimately destroying the capital, Oyo-Ile, in the Eleduwe War (mid-1830s), forcing massive southward migrations.
3. The collapse led to the rise of powerful new military city-states, including Ibadan, Abeokuta, Ijaye and New Oyo, led by a new class of warlords (the Ologun).
4. A power vacuum and the struggle to control trade routes led to a century of Yoruba civil wars, including the Owu, Batedo, Ijaye and Kiriji wars.
5. In 1840, the Ibadan army decisively defeated the Ilorin cavalry at the Osogbo War, halting the Fulani jihadist advance into Yorubaland.
6. The longest conflict was the Kiriji War (1877–1886), where the Ekitiparapo alliance used modern rifles to fight Ibadan to a standstill over its oppressive Ajele system.
7. To protect trade, the British in Lagos intervened. The 1886 Peace Treaty officially ended the Kiriji War, granted independence to the Ekitiparapo, and increased British influence, paving the way for full colonial takeover in 1893.

## Practice questions

**1. The collapse of the Old Oyo Empire was triggered internally by the rebellion of which military commander?**
- A. Bashorun Gaa
- B. Kurunmi
- C. Afonja
- D. Ogedengbe

**Answer: C.** Afonja, the Are Ona Kakanfo stationed at Ilorin, refused Alaafin Awole's orders and led a mutiny, which started the chain of events that destroyed the empire. A common mistake is choosing Bashorun Gaa, who caused a crisis earlier during the reign of Alaafin Abiodun.

**2. Which of the following wars halted the advance of the Fulani jihadists into Yorubaland?**
- A. The Owu War
- B. The Kiriji War
- C. The Eleduwe War
- D. The Osogbo War

**Answer: D.** The Osogbo War (1840) saw the Ibadan infantry defeat the Ilorin cavalry, permanently stopping the jihadist push into the forest region.

**3. The Yoruba civil wars of the 19th century began with the destruction of which town over a dispute at the Apomu market?**
- A. Ijaye
- B. Owu
- C. Ife
- D. Abeokuta

**Answer: B.** The Owu War (c. 1820–1825) began after a dispute at the Apomu market, leading Ife and Ijebu to lay siege to and destroy Owu.

**4. The immediate cause of the Ijaye War (1860-1862) was a dispute over:**
- A. the oppressive Ajele system of Ibadan
- B. control of the trade routes to Lagos
- C. the succession of Alaafin Adelu contrary to tradition
- D. the establishment of a British consulate in Abeokuta

**Answer: C.** Kurunmi of Ijaye refused to recognize Adelu because tradition stated the crown prince (Aremo) must die with his father, Alaafin Atiba. Option A caused the Kiriji War.

**5. The alliance formed by the Ekiti and Ijesha to fight against Ibadan's imperialism was called the:**
- A. Oyomesi
- B. Ekitiparapo
- C. Jama'a
- D. Ologun

**Answer: B.** The Ekitiparapo was the military alliance formed to throw off Ibadan's harsh rule during the Kiriji War. The Jama'a was Afonja's army in Ilorin.

**6. What was the main reason the British Governor of Lagos intervened to negotiate the 1886 Peace Treaty?**
- A. He wanted to spread Christianity to the interior
- B. He wanted to help Ibadan defeat the Ekitiparapo
- C. The wars had disrupted the trade routes, hurting British economic interests
- D. He was invited by the Alaafin of Oyo to restore order

**Answer: C.** The Kiriji War blocked trade to the coast, reducing the profits of Lagos merchants and the tax revenue of the British colonial government.

**7. Which town was established as a military camp by allied forces and emerged as the most powerful Yoruba state in the 19th century?**
- A. Ibadan
- B. Abeokuta
- C. Ilorin
- D. Modakeke

**Answer: A.** Ibadan was founded as a war camp by Oyo, Ife and Ijebu soldiers and grew into a powerful military republic that dominated Yorubaland.

**8. The resident administrators used by Old Oyo and later by Ibadan to supervise conquered towns were known as:**
- A. Ologun
- B. Ajeles
- C. Aremo
- D. Bashorun

**Answer: B.** The Ajeles (or Ilari) were provincial supervisors. Their oppressive behaviour in Ekiti and Ijesha lands sparked the Kiriji War.

**9. One of the provisions of the 1886 Peace Treaty that failed and led to future conflict was the order to resettle which community?**
- A. Egba
- B. Ijesha
- C. Modakeke
- D. Ijebu

**Answer: C.** The treaty ordered the Oyo refugee settlement of Modakeke to move away from Ife territory, but they refused, leading to the ongoing Ife-Modakeke conflict.

**10. The Kiriji War got its name from the:**
- A. town where the peace treaty was signed
- B. sound of the modern breech-loading rifles used in the battle
- C. title of the Ibadan military commander
- D. traditional drums beaten before the battle began

**Answer: B.** The breech-loading Snider rifles, smuggled from Lagos by the Ekitiparapo, made a loud "Ki-ri-ji" sound, which gave the war its popular name.
