# JAMB History Notes: Benin in the 19th Century

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B, Topic 4 of the JAMB History syllabus: Benin. It covers the history of the Benin Kingdom (in present-day Edo State, Nigeria) during the 1800s. It has three content items:

- **a. Internal political and cultural development**
- **b. Relations with neighbours**
- **c. Relations with the Europeans**

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. examine the internal political and cultural development;
2. examine her relations with her neighbours;
3. assess her relationship with the Europeans.

## Internal political and cultural development

**Objective i: examine the internal political and cultural development.**

By the 19th century, the Kingdom of Benin was no longer the vast, expanding empire it had been during its "Golden Age" in the 15th and 16th centuries. However, it remained a highly centralized, powerful, and culturally rich state. The 19th century in Benin was marked by efforts to maintain internal stability, defend its borders against new threats, and preserve its ancient traditions.

### The Political Structure of Benin

To understand the internal politics of 19th-century Benin, you must understand its complex system of government, which relied on a balance of power between the king and various groups of chiefs.

1. **The Oba:** The Oba of Benin was a divine monarch. He was believed to be a representative of the gods and ancestors on earth. He was the supreme political, judicial, and spiritual leader. However, he did not rule as an absolute dictator; his power was checked by powerful councils of chiefs.
2. **The Uzama (The Kingmakers):** This was the oldest and highest-ranking order of chiefs. There were seven Uzama chiefs, led by the **Oliha**. Their titles were hereditary (passed from father to son). Their most important duty was to crown the new Oba, but they lived outside the inner city walls and had less day-to-day political power in the 19th century than the town and palace chiefs.
3. **The Eghaevbo n'Ogbe (Palace Chiefs):** These chiefs lived in the Oba's palace area (Ogbe). They managed the royal household, the Oba's wives, and the royal treasury. They were the Oba's closest advisers. Their leader was the **Uwangue**.
4. **The Eghaevbo n'Ore (Town Chiefs):** These chiefs represented the interests of the ordinary citizens and governed the town (Ore) outside the palace. They were often wealthy men who earned their titles through merit and wealth rather than inheritance. Their leader was the **Iyase** (the Prime Minister and commander-in-chief of the army).

A recurring theme in 19th-century Benin politics was the struggle for power between the Oba and the **Iyase**. Because the Iyase represented the townspeople and commanded the army, a powerful Iyase could strongly challenge the Oba's authority.

### 19th Century Political Developments

The 19th century in Benin saw a series of Obas who had to navigate civil wars, succession disputes, and rebellious chiefs. 

- **Oba Obanosa (c. 1804–1816):** His reign was troubled by a bitter power struggle with his powerful chiefs, particularly the Osodin. This led to internal conflict and weakened the kingdom early in the century.
- **Oba Osemwende (c. 1816–1848):** He restored much of the kingdom's prestige. After surviving a succession dispute with his brother, he successfully crushed a rebellion in Akure (in eastern Yorubaland) in 1818, re-establishing Benin's authority over its western borders. He expanded the empire slightly and brought stability.
- **Oba Adolo (c. 1848–1888):** His reign was relatively peaceful internally, though he faced external threats from the Nupe jihadists invading from the north. He was known for encouraging trade, establishing markets, and being a generous ruler, though he struggled to control his ambitious Iyase.
- **Oba Ovonramwen Nogbaisi (1888–1897):** To secure his throne, Ovonramwen had to execute several powerful chiefs who opposed him. While this centralized his power, it created internal fears and divisions. He was the last independent Oba of the 19th century and had to face the British invasion.

### Cultural Development in the 19th Century

Benin was famous worldwide for its rich culture, highly organized society, and magnificent art. 

**1. The Guild System:**
Crafts and industries in Benin were organized into specialized family guilds (associations of craftsmen). Each guild lived in a specific part of the city and worked exclusively for the Oba. 
- **Igun Eronmwon:** The royal brass and bronze casters.
- **Igbesanmwan:** The royal ivory and wood carvers. 
- **Owina:** The weavers and carpenters.

**2. Art and Architecture:**
Benin's art was a historical record. When an important event occurred, the Oba commissioned the bronze casters to create a plaque or a sculpture to record it. 
- **The Lost-Wax Method (Cire Perdue):** The bronze casters used this highly advanced technique. A model was carved in beeswax, covered in clay, and baked. The wax melted and ran out (was "lost"), leaving a hollow clay mould. Molten bronze or brass was then poured into the mould.
- **Architecture:** Benin City was an architectural marvel. It was surrounded by massive earthworks (walls and deep moats) to protect it from invaders. The Oba's palace was a massive complex of courtyards, altars, and galleries, with wooden pillars covered in bronze plaques.

**3. Religion and State Rituals:**
Religion and politics were inseparable. The Oba was divine. 
- **The Igue Festival:** This was the most important annual festival. It was a time of thanksgiving and spiritual renewal, where the Oba's magical powers were renewed for the benefit of the nation. During this sacred period, the Oba was secluded and forbidden from seeing non-indigenes or foreigners.
- **Sacrifices:** Like many ancient kingdoms, Benin practiced animal and human sacrifice to appease the gods and ancestors, especially during times of national crisis, disease, or the death of an Oba. In the late 19th century, as Benin faced severe internal stress and the looming threat of British invasion, historians note that the frequency of these sacrifices increased. British officials later exaggerated reports of these sacrifices to justify their conquest of the kingdom.

## Relations with neighbours

**Objective ii: examine her relations with her neighbours.**

In the 19th century, the borders of the Benin Empire had shrunk from their peak, but Benin still claimed authority or significant influence over a wide area. Relations with neighbours involved a mix of trade, tribute collection, cultural exchange, and military conflict.

### 1. Relations with Yorubaland (The West)
Benin shared its western border with eastern Yoruba states like Ekiti, Ondo, and Akure. 
- **Tribute and Rebellions:** Many of these towns were historically tributary to Benin (they paid taxes/tribute in slaves, food, or goods). In 1818, Akure rebelled, but Oba Osemwende sent his army to crush the rebellion, reaffirming Benin's control over the eastern Yoruba borderlands.
- **The Threat of Ibadan:** Later in the 19th century, the new military state of Ibadan expanded aggressively into Ekiti and Ijesha lands. Benin armies had to carefully guard their western frontiers to prevent Ibadan from encroaching on Benin's tributary towns.

### 2. Relations with Esan (Ishan) and Afenmai (The North and Northeast)
- **Esan (Ishan):** The Esan people share close cultural and linguistic ties with Benin. Many Esan chiefdoms were founded by people who migrated from Benin in earlier centuries. In the 19th century, Benin maintained control over Esanland. The local rulers (called *Enigie*, singular *Onogie*) had to travel to Benin City to have their titles confirmed by the Oba, and they paid regular tribute.
- **Afenmai (Etsako) and the Nupe Threat:** Further north, Benin historically influenced the Afenmai region. However, during the reign of Oba Adolo (mid-to-late 19th century), Fulani jihadists from the Nupe Kingdom in Bida invaded Afenmai and parts of northern Esan. Benin armies were dispatched to fight the Nupe, but Benin ultimately lost effective control of much of its northern territories to the Nupe cavalry.

### 3. Relations with Western Igbo / Anioma (The East)
Across the River Niger to the east were the Western Igbo communities (such as Agbor, Asaba, and Issele-Uku). 
- Benin claimed suzerainty over several of these communities, who paid tribute to the Oba. 
- Cultural influence was strong; many Western Igbo rulers adopted Benin royal regalia, court titles, and the institution of the *Eze* (king) modelled after the Oba. When communities like Agbor refused to pay tribute, Benin occasionally sent military expeditions to enforce compliance.

### 4. Relations with the Niger Delta (The South)
Relations with the peoples of the Niger Delta—particularly the Itsekiri, Urhobo, and Ijaw—were vital for Benin's survival in the 19th century because they controlled access to the sea and, by extension, the European trade.
- **The Itsekiri:** The Olu of Warri and the Itsekiri people acted as the primary middlemen between Benin and the European merchants on the coast. Benin merchants would take their goods (palm oil, ivory) to waterside markets to trade with the Itsekiri.
- **Nana Olomu:** By the late 19th century, powerful Itsekiri merchant princes, notably **Nana Olomu**, monopolized the trade on the Benin River. This frustrated the Oba of Benin, as Nana controlled the prices and restricted Benin's direct access to the wealth of the European trade. 
- **Urhobo and Isoko:** Benin also traded extensively with the Urhobo and Isoko people, exchanging manufactured goods and coral beads for agricultural produce and palm oil.

## Relations with the Europeans

**Objective iii: assess her relationship with the Europeans.**

The 19th century brought a dramatic shift in Benin's relations with European powers (primarily the British), changing from a relationship of equal trading partners to one of colonial subjugation.

### The Shift from Slaves to "Legitimate Trade"
In the early 1800s, Britain abolished the transatlantic slave trade. The European demand shifted to "legitimate trade"—primarily palm oil and palm kernels, which were needed to lubricate machinery and make soap during the European Industrial Revolution. Benin also exported ivory and timber.
- **The Oba's Monopoly:** The Oba of Benin maintained strict control over trade. All major trade required his permission, and he fixed the prices. He could place an embargo (a ban) on trade to force the Europeans or the Delta middlemen to accept his terms.
- **British Frustration:** The British merchants believed in "free trade." They hated the Oba's monopolies and wanted to bypass the Itsekiri middlemen to trade directly with the resource-rich Benin hinterland.

### Consular Authority and the 1892 Treaty
In the 1880s and 1890s, the British established the Niger Coast Protectorate. They began sending Consuls to sign treaties with local rulers, effectively tricking or forcing them to surrender their sovereignty.
- **The Gallwey Treaty (1892):** Captain H.L. Gallwey, a British Vice-Consul, visited Benin City and convinced Oba Ovonramwen to sign a "Treaty of Protection." 
- **The Terms:** The treaty stated that Benin would abolish human sacrifice, allow Christian missionaries, and open its borders to free trade with British merchants. 
- **The Dispute:** According to Benin oral tradition, Oba Ovonramwen was suspicious of the treaty. He allegedly refused to touch the pen to sign it, though Gallwey claimed the treaty was valid. Regardless, the Oba had no intention of handing over his kingdom's independence or destroying his royal trade monopoly, and he continued to regulate trade as he saw fit.

### The "Benin Massacre" (January 1897)
By late 1896, British merchants and officials were angry that Oba Ovonramwen was ignoring the 1892 treaty and blocking their trade.
1. **Phillips' Expedition:** The Acting British Consul-General, **James Robert Phillips**, decided to visit Benin City to force the Oba to open the trade routes. He even sent a secret request to London asking for permission to depose (remove) the Oba.
2. **The Ague (Igue) Festival:** Phillips sent a message to the Oba announcing his visit. The Oba sent word back pleading with Phillips to delay the visit by two months. The Oba explained that he was celebrating the sacred Ague (Igue) festival, during which tradition strictly forbade him from seeing any foreigners.
3. **The Defiance:** Ignoring the warnings, Phillips and a small, lightly armed party of white officials and over 200 African porters set out for Benin City in January 1897.
4. **The Ambush:** The Benin chiefs, fearing that the British were coming to attack the city and disrupt the sacred rituals, organized an ambush. According to most historical accounts, this was done without the Oba's permission, led by the **Ologbosere** (a senior military chief). The Benin soldiers attacked Phillips's party on the road. Phillips and almost all the European officers (except two who escaped into the bush) and many African porters were killed.
5. **The Aftermath:** The British press called it the "Benin Massacre" and used it as the perfect excuse to invade and conquer the kingdom.

### The British Punitive Expedition (February 1897)
Within weeks, the British government organized a massive military retaliation called the **Punitive Expedition**, commanded by **Admiral Harry Rawson**.

1. **The Invasion:** In February 1897, a heavily armed British naval and land force attacked Benin from three directions. Despite fierce resistance from the Benin army using Dane guns, bows, and swords, the British Maxim machine guns easily overwhelmed them.
2. **The Fall of Benin City:** The British captured Benin City. A massive fire—whether started accidentally by the British or by the defenders—burned down much of the city, including the Oba's ancient palace.
3. **The Looting of the Bronzes:** The British forces systematically looted the royal palaces and shrines. They stole thousands of priceless brass, bronze, and ivory artworks (the famous **Benin Bronzes**). These were taken to Britain and sold to museums and collectors to pay for the cost of the expedition. (At the time of writing, Nigeria is still actively campaigning for the return of these stolen artifacts).
4. **The Fate of the Oba and Chiefs:** Oba Ovonramwen fled the city but surrendered a few months later. He was subjected to a humiliating trial by the British. The British exiled him to **Calabar**, where he lived until his death in 1914. Chief Ologbosere, who led the ambush, waged a guerrilla war for two years but was eventually captured and executed by the British. 

The Punitive Expedition of 1897 marked the violent end of the independent Benin Empire. The territory was subsequently absorbed into the British Niger Coast Protectorate, paving the way for the eventual creation of modern Nigeria.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not mix up the chiefs:** Remember that the **Uzama** are the kingmakers, while the **Iyase** is the Prime Minister (town chief) who often challenged the Oba.
- **Understand the cause of the 1897 invasion:** The immediate cause was the killing of Acting Consul Phillips (the "Benin Massacre"), but the underlying, root cause was British economic desire to break the Oba's monopoly and enforce free trade.
- **The Ague/Igue Festival is heavily tested:** Remember that Phillips was asked not to visit because the Oba was undergoing the sacred rituals of this festival and could not see foreigners.
- **The fate of the Oba:** Oba Ovonramwen was **not** executed by the British. He was exiled to Calabar. Chief Ologbosere was the one executed for the ambush.
- **Nupe invasions:** Remember that the main threat to Benin's northern territories (Afenmai) in the 19th century came from the Nupe jihadists, not from the British.

## Quick summary

1. In the 19th century, Benin's political structure balanced the power of the divine Oba with powerful councils of chiefs, particularly the Uzama (kingmakers) and the Iyase (Prime Minister).
2. Despite a decline from its 16th-century peak, Obas like Osemwende and Adolo managed to maintain stability, crush rebellions, and encourage economic growth.
3. Benin possessed a highly advanced culture, globally famous for its bronze and brass casting using the lost-wax method, managed by strict royal guilds.
4. Benin maintained complex relations with its neighbours: fighting off Nupe invaders in the north, enforcing tribute from Western Igbo and Yoruba towns, and trading through Itsekiri middlemen in the Delta.
5. Relations with Europeans shifted from the slave trade to "legitimate trade" in palm oil, over which the Oba maintained a strict monopoly.
6. British efforts to enforce "free trade" led to the 1892 Gallwey Treaty, which the Oba largely ignored.
7. In January 1897, Acting Consul Phillips ignored warnings not to visit during the sacred Igue festival and was ambushed and killed by Benin chiefs.
8. In February 1897, the British launched a Punitive Expedition, burning Benin City, looting its priceless bronzes, and exiling Oba Ovonramwen to Calabar, ending the independent Benin Empire.

## Practice questions

**1. The highest order of chiefs in the pre-colonial Benin political system, responsible for crowning a new Oba, was the:**

- A. Eghaevbo n'Ogbe
- B. Eghaevbo n'Ore
- C. Uzama
- D. Igun Eronmwon

**Answer: C.** The Uzama were the seven traditional kingmakers of the Benin Kingdom. Option A (Eghaevbo n'Ogbe) is a common confusion, but they were the palace chiefs and royal advisers, not the kingmakers.

**2. Which of the following titles represented the Prime Minister and commander-in-chief of the Benin army who frequently challenged the Oba's authority?**

- A. The Oliha
- B. The Iyase
- C. The Uwangue
- D. The Ologbosere

**Answer: B.** The Iyase was the leader of the town chiefs (Eghaevbo n'Ore) and the most powerful chief outside the palace, often serving as a check on the Oba's power. Option D (Ologbosere) is tempting because he was a senior military chief who famously led the 1897 ambush, but the Iyase held the supreme Prime Minister role.

**3. The famous Benin Bronze artworks were created using an advanced metallurgical technique known as:**

- A. Iron smelting
- B. The lost-wax method (Cire Perdue)
- C. Electroplating
- D. Cold forging

**Answer: B.** Benin casters used the lost-wax (cire perdue) method to create highly detailed brass and bronze plaques and sculptures. Option A (Iron smelting) is a typical confusion since blacksmithing was widespread in the region, but the intricate royal brass items were cast using the lost-wax process.

**4. In the 19th century, Benin lost effective control of its northern tributary areas in Afenmai primarily due to invasions by the:**

- A. British forces
- B. Ibadan warriors
- C. Nupe jihadists
- D. Itsekiri middlemen

**Answer: C.** The Nupe cavalry, expanding southward from Bida, invaded and seized control of parts of Afenmai and northern Esan from Benin's sphere of influence during Oba Adolo's reign. Option B (Ibadan warriors) is a common mistake because Ibadan was aggressively expanding at the time, but they pushed into eastern Yorubaland on Benin's western border, not the north.

**5. Which powerful group in the Niger Delta acted as middlemen, controlling the trade between Benin and the European merchants on the coast?**

- A. The Western Igbo
- B. The Itsekiri
- C. The Esan
- D. The Ekiti

**Answer: B.** The Itsekiri, led by powerful figures like the Olu and later Nana Olomu, controlled the waterways and acted as middlemen, which frustrated Benin's desire for direct access to European markets. Option D (Ekiti) is incorrect because they were a tributary neighbor located in the inland west, far from the coastal trade routes.

**6. The primary economic reason the British wanted to depose the Oba of Benin in the late 19th century was to:**

- A. Stop the transatlantic slave trade
- B. Break the Oba's trade monopoly and enforce free trade
- C. Seize the gold mines in Benin City
- D. Convert the Oba to Christianity

**Answer: B.** British merchants and officials were frustrated by the Oba's strict control over the trade in palm oil and ivory, wanting to open the hinterland to free trade. Option A (Stop the transatlantic slave trade) is a typical confusion because the British used abolition as an excuse to intervene elsewhere earlier in the century, but by the 1890s in Benin, the struggle was entirely about "legitimate trade."

**7. Oba Ovonramwen pleaded with Acting Consul Phillips not to visit Benin City in January 1897 because:**

- A. The British army was threatening to attack
- B. He was observing the sacred Igue (Ague) festival and could not see foreigners
- C. There was a severe smallpox outbreak in the city
- D. He was away fighting a war against Ibadan

**Answer: B.** The Oba requested a two-month delay because the sacred rituals of the Igue festival strictly prohibited him from interacting with non-indigenes or foreigners. Option C (smallpox outbreak) might seem like a plausible practical reason to isolate, but historical records explicitly point to the spiritual requirements of the Igue festival.

**8. Who led the ambush against the British party commanded by Consul Phillips in 1897?**

- A. Oba Ovonramwen
- B. Chief Ologbosere
- C. The Iyase
- D. The Oliha

**Answer: B.** Chief Ologbosere, a senior military leader, organized the ambush. Option A (Oba Ovonramwen) is the most common mistake among students; however, most historical accounts agree the Oba explicitly ordered his chiefs not to attack, but Ologbosere acted independently out of fear for the kingdom's safety.

**9. Following the successful Punitive Expedition of 1897, the British exiled Oba Ovonramwen to:**

- A. Lokoja
- B. Calabar
- C. London
- D. Lagos

**Answer: B.** Oba Ovonramwen was captured, tried, and exiled to Calabar, where he lived the rest of his life until he died in 1914. Option C (London) is tempting because thousands of stolen bronzes were taken there, but the Oba himself was sent to Calabar.

**10. The 1892 treaty that the British used to claim legal authority over Benin's trade and foreign policy was signed by:**

- A. Admiral Rawson
- B. Consul Phillips
- C. Captain Gallwey
- D. George Goldie

**Answer: C.** Captain H.L. Gallwey negotiated the 1892 "Treaty of Protection" with Oba Ovonramwen. Option B (Consul Phillips) is a common mix-up because his later death sparked the 1897 invasion, but it was Vice-Consul Gallwey who pushed the initial treaty five years earlier.
