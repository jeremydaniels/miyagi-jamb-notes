## European exploration of the interior

For nearly three hundred years, European contact with the Nigerian area was strictly limited to the coast. European merchants bought slaves from African middlemen and knew almost nothing about the inland areas. In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, this changed as Europeans began sending explorers deep into the interior. 

### Motives for the exploration of the interior

**Objective i: examine the motive for the exploration of the interior.**

The push to explore the African interior was driven by several overlapping motives:

1. **Geographical curiosity and scientific inquiry:** During the Enlightenment in Europe, there was a strong desire to map unknown parts of the world. In West Africa, the greatest geographical mystery was the **River Niger**. Europeans did not know where it started, which direction it flowed, or where it entered the sea. In 1788, a group of wealthy Englishmen formed the **African Association** specifically to sponsor explorers to solve this puzzle.
2. **Economic motives (The Industrial Revolution):** As Britain industrialised, factory owners needed new sources of cheap raw materials (such as palm oil and cotton) and new markets to sell their mass-produced manufactured goods. Exploring the interior and finding navigable rivers was the first step to opening up direct trade with inland African societies, bypassing the expensive coastal middlemen.
3. **Missionary and humanitarian zeal:** The evangelical revival in Europe convinced many Christians that they had a duty to spread the gospel to Africa. Furthermore, humanitarian groups believed that the only way to stop the internal slave trade was to open up the interior to "legitimate commerce" and Christianity—a policy popularised as **"the Bible and the Plough"**. 

**Note on terminology:** When history textbooks say Europeans "discovered" the River Niger or its mouth, they mean it was discovered *for European geographical knowledge*. African communities had been living along these rivers, fishing in them, and navigating them for thousands of years.

### Key explorers and their achievements

The British government and the African Association sponsored several famous expeditions.

| Explorer(s) | Date(s) | Key Achievement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mungo Park** | 1795, 1805 | A Scottish doctor who proved that the River Niger **flowed eastwards**. During his second trip, he sailed down the river but was killed in an ambush at Bussa (in present-day Niger State) in 1806 without finding its mouth. |
| **Hugh Clapperton**, Dixon Denham, Walter Oudney | 1822–1824 | Crossed the Sahara Desert from North Africa to reach **Kanem-Borno**, Kano, and the **Sokoto Caliphate**. Clapperton returned on a second expedition starting from Badagry in 1825 but died in Sokoto in 1827. They provided Europe with detailed written accounts of Northern Nigeria. |
| **Richard and John Lander** | 1830 | Richard Lander (who had been Clapperton's servant) and his brother John finally solved the Niger mystery. They travelled overland to Bussa, entered the River Niger, and sailed downstream until they reached the Atlantic Ocean in the **Niger Delta**. This proved the Delta was the mouth of the Niger. |
| **Heinrich Barth** | 1850s | A German scholar working for the British government. He travelled extensively throughout Northern Nigeria (including Borno, Kano, and Katsina) and produced massive, detailed records of the region's history, languages, and geography. |
| **William Baikie** | 1854 | A Scottish doctor who led an expedition up the River Niger and River Benue. His most important achievement was successfully using **quinine** to prevent and treat malaria. Not a single European on his crew died. This proved that Europeans could survive the West African interior, paving the way for merchants and missionaries. |

## The suppression of the trans-Atlantic slave trade

For centuries, the trans-Atlantic slave trade was the main form of commerce between Europeans and the Nigerian coast. However, in **1807**, Britain passed the Abolition Act, making it illegal for British subjects to trade in slaves, and eventually pushed to stop the trade entirely.

### Reasons for the suppression of the trans-Atlantic slave trade

**Objective ii: give reasons for the suppression of the trans-Atlantic slave trade.**

Historians usually group the reasons for abolition into three main categories:

1. **Economic reasons (The Industrial Revolution):** Many historians argue that economics was the main driver. The invention of machines meant that European factory owners now needed raw materials (like palm oil to lubricate machines and make soap) much more than they needed slave labour on American plantations. Furthermore, factories produced massive amounts of goods that needed buyers. Slaves do not earn wages and cannot buy goods. It became more profitable to keep Africans in Africa to produce agricultural goods and buy British products.
2. **Humanitarian and religious reasons:** A powerful anti-slavery movement arose in Britain, led by Evangelical Christians and Quakers such as **William Wilberforce**, **Thomas Clarkson**, and **Granville Sharp**. They argued that the slave trade was a brutal, un-Christian sin against human dignity. They organised boycotts, wrote pamphlets, and pressured the British parliament to abolish the trade.
3. **Intellectual and political reasons:** The intellectual movement known as the Enlightenment promoted the ideals of liberty, equality, and natural human rights. At the same time, fierce and costly resistance by enslaved people—most notably the successful **Haitian Revolution**—made the institution of slavery increasingly dangerous and expensive to maintain.

### How the trade was suppressed

Passing a law in London did not immediately stop the trade, as other European nations (like Portugal and Spain) and African middlemen continued it. Britain used several methods to enforce suppression on the Nigerian coast:

- **The Preventive Squadron:** Britain stationed a naval force (the West Africa Squadron) off the coast of West Africa to patrol the waters, stop slave ships, and free the captives (who were mostly taken to Freetown in Sierra Leone).
- **Anti-slave trade treaties:** British officials pressured, bribed, or forced African coastal rulers to sign treaties promising to stop selling humans and switch to natural products.
- **The use of force (Gunboat Diplomacy):** When rulers refused to stop the trade, the British navy attacked them. A famous example is the **bombardment of Lagos in 1851**. Oba Kosoko of Lagos was a major slave trader who refused to sign a treaty. The British navy attacked Lagos, forced Kosoko into exile, and installed his uncle **Akitoye**, who immediately signed a treaty banning the slave trade.

## The development of commodity trade and rise of consular authority

As the slave trade was gradually stamped out, it was replaced by a new economic system, which in turn required a new political arrangement on the coast.

### Tracing the development of commodity trade

**Objective iii: trace the development of commodity trade.**

The transition from trading human beings to trading natural agricultural products is known in history as the shift to **legitimate commerce** (or legitimate trade).

#### The main commodities
The most important commodity in the 19th-century Nigerian area was **palm oil**, heavily concentrated in the Niger Delta, which became known as the **Oil Rivers**. Palm oil was in huge demand in Europe for making soap and lubricating industrial machinery. Later, **palm kernels**, **timber**, **groundnuts** (from the north), and **cotton** became major exports. In return, Africans imported manufactured goods such as cloth, iron pots, firearms, alcohol, and cowrie shells (used as currency).

#### The role of African middlemen
The trade was dominated by African coastal merchants—such as the Ijo, Efik, and Itsekiri. These merchants acted as **middlemen**. They barred Europeans from travelling inland. The middlemen bought palm oil from the interior producers, transported it down the rivers in large canoes, and sold it to the European merchants (supercargoes) waiting on ships at the coast. This trade made some coastal rulers and merchants incredibly wealthy and powerful, such as **Jaja of Opobo** and **Nana Olomu** of the Itsekiri.

#### The Trust System
Because cash was not widely used, trade operated on the **Trust System**. European merchants would give African middlemen a large quantity of manufactured goods on credit ("trust"). The middlemen would take these goods into the interior, exchange them for palm oil, and return to the coast to pay off their debt. 

While it generated wealth, the trust system frequently broke down. Disputes constantly broke out over delayed payments, bad quality oil, or sudden drops in prices dictated by the European markets.

#### The push into the interior
After the Lander brothers discovered the Niger's mouth (1830) and Baikie proved quinine could prevent malaria (1854), European merchants realised they no longer had to wait at the coast. They began sailing their own steamships directly up the Niger to buy oil straight from the producers, bypassing the African middlemen. This led to violent conflicts, as the coastal states fought to protect their traditional monopolies.

### Accounting for the rise of consular authority

**Objective vi: account for the rise of consular authority.**

A **Consul** is an official appointed by a government to live in a foreign area to protect its citizens and commercial interests. 

As legitimate commerce grew, the British government found it necessary to establish a permanent political presence on the Nigerian coast. In **1849**, Britain appointed **John Beecroft** as the first British Consul for the Bights of Benin and Biafra.

#### Reasons for the rise of consular authority
1. **Protecting legitimate trade and settling disputes:** The constant quarrels between European merchants and African middlemen over the trust system threatened profits. A consul was needed to maintain order, protect British traders, and set up institutions like the **Courts of Equity** (mixed courts of African and European traders used to settle debts).
2. **Enforcing anti-slavery treaties:** The British needed an official on the ground to ensure that coastal rulers did not secretly go back to selling slaves.
3. **Protecting missionaries:** As European missionaries moved into the interior (e.g., Abeokuta), consuls were tasked with ensuring their safety.
4. **Keeping out European rivals:** As competition with France and Germany increased, Britain used the consuls to sign treaties of "protection" with African rulers to secure the territory for British interests.

#### From diplomacy to interference
Although consuls were supposed to be diplomats, they increasingly acted like rulers, using British naval gunboats to enforce their will. Consular authority became a direct stepping stone to colonial rule. 
- In 1851, Consul Beecroft orchestrated the bombardment of Lagos.
- In 1861, a British consul oversaw the outright annexation of Lagos, making it a British colony. 
- In the Niger Delta, consuls regularly deposed African kings (such as Jaja of Opobo in 1887) who refused to obey British trade rules.

## Christian missionary activities

**Objective iv: examine missionary and European activities in the area.**

*(Note: While exploration, trade, and consular politics are all "European activities", this objective focuses heavily on the arrival of Christian missionaries and the massive social changes they drove.)*

### The arrival of the missionaries
The 19th-century missionary push into Nigeria was heavily encouraged by the **"Saro"**—freed slaves from the Yoruba hinterland who had been rescued by the British navy, taken to Sierra Leone, converted to Christianity, and given a Western education. When many of them returned home to Badagry and Abeokuta in the late 1830s, they wrote to European missionary societies asking them to come and preach.

- **1842:** The **Wesleyan Methodist Mission**, led by **Thomas Birch Freeman**, arrived in Badagry. 
- **1842:** Shortly after, the **Church Missionary Society (C.M.S.)** arrived, led by **Henry Townsend** and **Samuel Ajayi Crowther** (a returned Saro slave who later made history as the first African Anglican Bishop). They soon moved inland to Abeokuta.
- **1846:** The **Church of Scotland (Presbyterian)** mission, led by **Hope Masterton Waddell**, arrived in Calabar at the request of Efik kings.
- **1850s–1860s:** The **Southern Baptist Convention** (led by Thomas J. Bowen) pushed into Yorubaland, and the **Catholic Church** (Society of African Missions) established a permanent base in Lagos.

### Missionary activities and their impact
The missionaries did much more than just preach the gospel; they transformed the society:

1. **Western Education:** This was their most powerful tool. To convert people, they needed them to read the Bible. The missionaries built the first primary schools (the first in Badagry in 1843) and secondary schools (the CMS Grammar School in Lagos in 1859). This education created a new African elite of clerks, teachers, and professionals.
2. **Translation and Printing:** Missionaries studied local languages. Samuel Ajayi Crowther famously translated the Bible into Yoruba. In 1859, Henry Townsend established the first newspaper in Nigeria, ***Iwe Irohin***, in Abeokuta.
3. **Health and Medicine:** They built clinics, dispensaries, and hospitals to treat diseases, which helped win the trust of the local people.
4. **Promotion of Legitimate Trade ("The Bible and the Plough"):** Missionaries actively encouraged farming and introduced new cash crops (like cotton and cocoa) to ensure the people had a profitable alternative to the slave trade.
5. **Social Reforms:** Missionaries campaigned aggressively against traditional practices they viewed as evil. The most famous example is **Mary Slessor**, a Scottish Presbyterian missionary who worked in Calabar and successfully campaigned to stop the traditional practice of killing twins.

## The activities of the trading companies

**Objective v: assess the activities of the European trading companies.**

As legitimate commerce expanded, numerous British, French, and German trading companies rushed to the Niger Delta and the River Niger to buy palm oil. 

### Intense competition and George Goldie
By the 1870s, the fierce competition was ruining profits for the British companies. They were undercut by French rivals and outmaneuvered by powerful African middlemen. 

A British businessman, **George Taubman Goldie**, realised that to survive, the British firms needed to stop fighting each other. In **1879**, he successfully merged all the British companies on the Niger to form the **United African Company (UAC)**, which was later renamed the **National African Company (NAC)**. Goldie then fought a ruthless price war to drive the French companies out of the region.

### The Royal Niger Company (1886)
Because Goldie's company had secured a total monopoly on the lower Niger, the British government was able to claim the territory at the Berlin Conference (1884–1885). In **1886**, the British government rewarded Goldie's firm by granting it a Royal Charter, renaming it the **Royal Niger Company (RNC)**.

#### Activities of the Royal Niger Company:
1. **Economic Monopoly:** The RNC used its charter to create a total monopoly. It passed harsh regulations that prevented African coastal middlemen and other Europeans from trading directly in its territory. 
2. **Quasi-Governmental Powers:** The company was not just a business; it acted as a government. It established its headquarters at Asaba and Lokoja, raised its own private army (the Royal Niger Constabulary), set up courts, and levied heavy taxes and customs duties on the local population.
3. **Treaty-Making:** The company agents travelled into Northern Nigeria, signing hundreds of treaties with traditional rulers, including the Emirs of the Sokoto Caliphate. These treaties were used by Britain to claim the territory, even though the local rulers often did not realise they were signing away their sovereignty.
4. **Violent Conflict (The Akassa Raid):** The RNC's monopoly destroyed the livelihoods of the traditional African middlemen. The Nembe people (Brassmen) of the Niger Delta were particularly badly hit. In **1895**, led by King Koko, the angry Brassmen launched a massive attack on the RNC's headquarters at Akassa, destroying properties and taking hostages. 

### Revocation of the Charter
The Akassa Raid embarrassed the British government and proved that a private trading company could not safely govern a huge territory. Furthermore, the RNC was not strong enough to stop the military advances of the French in the north. Consequently, the British government revoked the RNC's charter in 1899. On **January 1, 1900**, the British government officially took over the territory, laying the immediate foundation for colonial rule.

## Impact of European activities on the coast and the hinterland

The combined activities of the explorers, the anti-slavery naval squadron, the merchants, the consuls, and the missionaries totally transformed the Nigerian area during the 19th century.

| Sphere of Impact | Details |
| --- | --- |
| **Economic** | The region was forcefully integrated into the global capitalist economy. The shift to legitimate commerce made Nigeria a producer of cheap raw materials (palm oil, cotton) and an importer of European manufactured goods. This influx of cheap European goods led to the decline of local indigenous industries (like local cloth weaving and iron smelting). New currencies eventually replaced cowrie shells. |
| **Social and Cultural** | Christianity spread rapidly, particularly in the south. Western education created a new, literate elite who would later become the pioneer nationalists (like Herbert Macaulay). English gradually became the language of administration and inter-ethnic commerce. Traditional customs such as human sacrifice and the killing of twins were heavily reduced, but traditional rulers also lost much of their religious and cultural authority. |
| **Political** | The most significant impact was the gradual **loss of African sovereignty**. What began as trade and missionary work evolved into consular interference. Rulers who resisted European trade rules were deposed or exiled (like Jaja of Opobo and Nana of Itsekiri). The treaties signed by the Royal Niger Company were used to legally justify British colonial claims. By 1900, European penetration had paved the way for the total British conquest of the Nigerian area. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse the explorers:** Mungo Park discovered the *direction* the Niger flowed (eastwards). The Lander brothers discovered its *mouth* (where it emptied into the Atlantic). William Baikie proved the effectiveness of *quinine* against malaria.
- **Legitimate commerce vs. Triangular trade:** The triangular trade refers to the trans-Atlantic slave trade. Legitimate commerce refers to the trade in palm oil and other cash crops that replaced it.
- **The RNC was not the British government:** The Royal Niger Company was a private business that ruled by charter. Its charter was revoked in 1899 to allow the actual British government to take over on Jan 1, 1900.
- **Understand the role of a Consul:** In theory, a consul is just a diplomat protecting trade. In practice, British consuls in the Niger Delta deposed kings, used gunboats, and directly paved the way for colonialism.
- **First Missionaries:** The Wesleyan Methodists (Thomas Birch Freeman) arrived in Badagry slightly before the C.M.S. in 1842.

## Quick summary

1. In the late 18th and 19th centuries, European explorers, driven by scientific, economic, and missionary motives, mapped the Nigerian interior and solved the mystery of the River Niger.
2. The trans-Atlantic slave trade was suppressed due to the Industrial Revolution, humanitarian campaigning, and naval force (the Preventive Squadron), culminating in treaties and actions like the 1851 bombardment of Lagos.
3. Trade shifted to "legitimate commerce", primarily the export of palm oil from the Niger Delta, operated through African middlemen using the trust system.
4. To protect this trade and enforce anti-slavery treaties, Britain appointed Consuls (starting with John Beecroft in 1849), who increasingly interfered in local politics and eroded African sovereignty.
5. Christian missionaries arrived in the 1840s, spreading the gospel, building schools and hospitals, promoting legitimate trade, and attacking traditional customs like the killing of twins.
6. To end cut-throat competition, British merchants merged into the Royal Niger Company under George Goldie, which used its royal charter to monopolize trade, govern territories, and sign treaties until it was taken over by the British government in 1900.
7. European penetration fundamentally changed the Nigerian area, integrating it into a capitalist economy, creating a Western-educated elite, and directly paving the way for British colonial conquest.

**1. What was the primary geographical puzzle European explorers sought to solve in West Africa during the 19th century?**
- A. The source of the River Nile
- B. The course and mouth of the River Niger
- C. The quickest land route to South Africa

**Answer: B.** The African Association sponsored explorers like Mungo Park and the Lander brothers specifically to find out where the River Niger started, which way it flowed, and where it entered the sea.

**2. The successful use of quinine to combat malaria on the Niger expedition of 1854 was achieved by**
- A. Mungo Park
- B. Richard Lander
- C. William Baikie
- D. George Goldie

**Answer: C.** William Baikie successfully used quinine as a prophylactic against malaria, proving that Europeans could survive the interior.

- A. The discovery of large gold deposits in West Africa
- B. The Industrial Revolution in Europe
- C. The collapse of the cowrie shell currency system

**Answer: B.** The Industrial Revolution meant Europe needed raw materials (like palm oil) and markets for manufactured goods more than it needed slave labour on plantations.

- A. the Trust system
