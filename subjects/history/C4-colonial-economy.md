## The Nature of the Colonial Economy (Objective i)

Before examining the specific sectors of the colonial economy, it is important to understand its overall nature and how it affected Nigerian society. 

The colonial economy was fundamentally an **extractive economy** (often called an export-oriented economy). Its main purpose was to serve the economic interests of Great Britain, the colonial power. Britain needed two things from Nigeria:
1. **Raw materials:** Agricultural products (like palm oil, cocoa, groundnuts) and minerals (like tin and coal) to feed British factories.
2. **Markets:** A place to sell British manufactured goods (like textiles, soap, and tools).

To achieve this, the British actively discouraged local manufacturing and focused entirely on making it easy to export raw materials and import finished goods. This structure fundamentally changed Nigerian society in several ways:

- **Monetization:** Society shifted from bartering and traditional currencies to a cash economy. People needed cash to pay taxes and buy imported goods.
- **Urbanization:** New cities grew around mining camps (Jos, Enugu), railway hubs (Kaduna, Kafanchan), and ports (Port Harcourt, Lagos). 
- **Rural-Urban Migration:** Young men left their villages to seek wage labor in the cities, mines, or on the railways, altering traditional family structures and farming.
- **New Social Classes:** A new class system emerged. It included a small Western-educated elite (clerks, teachers), a wage-earning working class (miners, railway workers), wealthy middlemen traders, and a large population of peasant farmers who produced cash crops.

## Currency, Taxation and Forced Labour (Content a)

To integrate Nigeria into the British economic system, the colonial administration had to change how people traded, how the government made money, and how heavy work was done.

### 1. Currency Transition
Before colonial rule, Nigerians used various currencies, including **cowrie shells, manillas (copper rings), brass rods, and gold dust**, as well as the barter system. 

The British introduced standard British coins and paper money to unify the economy and make trade easier for European merchants. 
- In **1912**, the British established the **West African Currency Board (WACB)** to issue currency for Nigeria, Gold Coast (Ghana), Sierra Leone, and Gambia. 
- The new currency was tied to the British pound sterling. 
- Traditional currencies were gradually **demonetized** (banned from being used as legal tender). This forced Nigerians to sell their goods and labor for British money, increasing their dependence on the colonial economy.

### 2. Taxation
The colonial government needed money to run its administration, pay British officials, and build infrastructure. Rather than bringing money from Britain, they expected the colonies to pay for themselves.
- **Direct Taxation:** Lord Lugard introduced direct taxation through the **Native Revenue Ordinance**. Taxes had to be paid in British currency. 
- To get money to pay the tax, Nigerian men had to grow cash crops or work for wages, exactly as the British wanted.
- Taxation was deeply unpopular and led to major revolts. The most famous was the **Aba Women's War (or Riot) of 1929** in Eastern Nigeria, sparked by rumors that women were going to be taxed along with men.

### 3. Forced Labour
In the early days of colonial rule, there was a shortage of willing wage laborers to build the massive infrastructure projects the British wanted.
- The colonial government used **forced labour** to build railways, roads, and government buildings, and to work in the tin mines of Jos.
- Traditional chiefs and warrant chiefs were given quotas to supply strong young men from their communities.
- These men worked under harsh conditions for very low pay, or sometimes no pay at all. Over time, as the cash economy grew and international pressure against forced labor increased, the practice was phased out, and a voluntary wage-labor system took its place.

## Infrastructure: Transportation, Post and Telecommunications (Content b)

The British built infrastructure not to connect Nigerian communities to one another, but to connect raw materials in the interior to the coastal ports for export to Europe.

### Transportation
- **Railways:** This was the most important transport investment. 
  - The Western line started from **Lagos in 1898**, reached Ibadan in 1901, and got to **Kano around 1911/1912**. It was used to export cocoa, cotton, and groundnuts.
  - The Eastern line started from **Port Harcourt in 1913** and reached **Enugu in 1916**. Its primary purpose was to transport newly discovered coal from Enugu to the coast.
- **Roads:** The government built "feeder roads." These were dirt or laterite roads designed to connect agricultural villages to the nearest railway stations. 
- **Harbours (Ports):** Deep-water ports were developed at **Lagos (Apapa)** and **Port Harcourt** so that large European steamships could dock and load raw materials. Port Harcourt was completely built from scratch by the British specifically for exporting coal.

### Post and Telecommunications
The British established a postal system and laid **telegraph lines** across the country. 
- Telegraph lines often followed the railway tracks. 
- **Purpose:** They allowed British administrators in different regions (and merchants in the cities) to communicate rapidly with each other and with London. It was an essential tool for maintaining security and coordinating the export trade.

## Agriculture (Content c)

Agriculture was the backbone of the colonial economy. Unlike in the Belgian Congo or parts of East Africa where Europeans set up large plantations, the British in Nigeria relied on **peasant farming**. 

Nigerian farmers were encouraged (and sometimes pressured by taxation) to grow **cash crops** (crops grown for export) rather than just food crops for survival (subsistence farming). 

### Major Export Crops
| Crop | Main Producing Region | Use in Europe |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Palm Oil & Kernels** | Eastern and Western Regions | Soap making, industrial lubricants, margarine |
| **Cocoa** | Western Region | Chocolate manufacturing |
| **Groundnut (Peanut)** | Northern Region (especially Kano) | Cooking oils, animal feed |
| **Cotton** | Northern and Western Regions | Textile manufacturing |
| **Rubber** | Mid-Western Region (Benin/Sapele) | Tires, industrial rubber products |

### The Marketing Boards
During and after the Second World War, the colonial government established **Marketing Boards** (such as the Cocoa Marketing Board and the Groundnut Marketing Board). 
- **Stated aim:** The government claimed these boards would protect farmers from fluctuating world prices by buying their crops at a stable, fixed price.
- **Actual outcome:** The boards usually paid farmers far less than the crops were worth on the world market. The government kept the massive profits (the surplus) to fund its administration and post-war recovery in Britain. It was essentially a hidden tax on Nigerian farmers.

## Mining (Content d)

Before colonialism, Nigerians mined iron, salt, and small amounts of tin. Under colonial rule, the British passed laws declaring that all mineral resources belonged to the British Crown. Mining was done exclusively for export and domestic colonial needs (like powering trains).

1. **Tin Mining on the Jos Plateau:** 
   - Commercial tin mining began in the early 1900s, dominated by the Royal Niger Company and later the United Africa Company (UAC).
   - It attracted thousands of migrant workers from other parts of Nigeria, rapidly turning Jos into a cosmopolitan city.
2. **Coal Mining in Enugu:**
   - Coal was discovered in Enugu in **1909** and mining began in **1915**.
   - The coal mines were owned by the colonial government. 
   - Coal was crucial for powering the colonial railway engines and the first electricity generating plants in Nigeria. It also led to the birth and rapid growth of Enugu city.

## Industry (Content e)

Under colonial rule, **manufacturing industry was deliberately discouraged**. 
- If Nigeria built its own factories to make clothes, soap, or tools, it would stop buying those goods from British factories.
- Therefore, the only "industries" allowed were **processing industries**. These were small factories designed to process raw materials just enough to make them lighter and cheaper to ship to Europe.
- **Examples of processing industries:** Cotton ginneries (removing seeds from cotton), groundnut decorticators (removing the shells), and palm oil presses.
- There was no heavy industry, steelmaking, or advanced manufacturing. This left Nigeria economically dependent on imported goods, a problem that continued long after independence.

## Commerce (Content f)

Colonial commerce (trade) was heavily skewed in favor of Europeans.

### Expatriate Trading Companies
Large European firms controlled the import and export trade. The most powerful was the **United Africa Company (UAC)**, formed in 1929 from the merger of the Niger Company (the former Royal Niger Company) and the African and Eastern Trade Corporation. Other major companies included **John Holt**, **PZ (Paterson Zochonis)**, and the French companies **CFAO** and **SCOA**.
- These companies bought cash crops cheaply from Nigerians and sold imported European goods at high prices.
- They formed alliances to fix prices, meaning Nigerian farmers could not negotiate for better pay.

### The Role of Middlemen
Before colonialism, wealthy Nigerian merchants (like Jaja of Opobo and Nana Olomu of Itsekiri) acted as wealthy middlemen between the interior and European ships. 
- During the colonial era, the British bypassed or crushed these indigenous middlemen so they could deal directly with the interior.
- The colonial government later favored **Lebanese and Syrian immigrants**, allowing them to take over the middleman role (distributing manufactured goods to local markets). This severely marginalized Nigerian businessmen.

## Banking (Content g)

To support the European trading companies, the British introduced modern banking to Nigeria.

### British Banks
The first successful bank was the **Bank of British West Africa (BBWA)**, established in **1894** (this bank later became First Bank of Nigeria). In 1917, the **Colonial Bank** began operating in Nigeria (Barclays took it over in 1925, and it later became Union Bank).
- **Discrimination:** These British banks existed almost entirely to serve the colonial government and European merchants. They routinely denied loans and credit to Nigerian businessmen, claiming that Nigerians lacked acceptable collateral (like registered land titles).

### The Rise of Indigenous Banks
Frustrated by this discrimination, wealthy Nigerians began establishing their own banks to support local businesses. 
- In 1933, the **National Bank of Nigeria** was founded.
- In 1945, **Agbonmagbe Bank** was established.
- In 1947, Nnamdi Azikiwe founded the **African Continental Bank (ACB)** to help Nigerian entrepreneurs who were being ignored by the British banks. 
- While many early indigenous banks failed due to lack of capital and experience, they represented a strong push for Nigerian economic independence.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Extractive economy | An economy designed primarily to export raw materials and import manufactured goods, rather than developing local manufacturing. |
| Monetization | The transition of an economy from bartering and traditional exchange to the use of modern cash and coins. |
| Demonetization | The act of stripping a currency (like cowries or manillas) of its status as legal tender. |
| West African Currency Board (WACB) | Established in 1912 to issue standard British currency across British West African colonies. |
| Direct Taxation | A tax paid directly to the government by the person it is imposed on; introduced by Lugard via the Native Revenue Ordinance. |
| Forced Labour | Compulsory work demanded by the colonial government from locals, often for infrastructure projects, for little or no pay. |
| Peasant farming | Small-scale farming by local families on their own land, which the British relied on for cash crop production in Nigeria. |
| Cash crop | Agricultural crops grown specifically for sale and export (e.g., cocoa, cotton) rather than for local consumption. |
| Marketing Boards | Colonial agencies that bought cash crops from farmers at fixed, often low prices, to sell on the world market. |
| Processing industry | Light industry focused on preparing raw materials for export (e.g., cotton ginning) rather than manufacturing finished goods. |
| Expatriate firms | Foreign-owned trading companies, such as UAC and John Holt, that dominated colonial commerce. |
| Middlemen | Traders who buy goods from producers and sell them to merchants or consumers. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Understand the direction of trade:** The colonial economy exported *raw materials* (agricultural produce, minerals) and imported *manufactured goods*. Do not select options suggesting the British exported manufactured goods *from* Nigeria.
- **Purpose of railways:** The British built railways to evacuate raw materials from the interior to the coastal ports, **not** to improve passenger travel for Nigerians or connect local communities for internal trade.
- **Enugu and Jos:** Always associate Enugu with **coal** mining (started 1915) and Jos with **tin** mining. 
- **Marketing Boards:** Their official aim was to stabilize prices, but their *actual historical effect* was to accumulate capital for the government by paying farmers less than world market prices.
- **Industrialization:** Remember that the British deliberately discouraged manufacturing in Nigeria. Only "processing" industries existed. 
- **Indigenous banks:** Know that Nigerian-owned banks (like ACB) were created because British banks (like BBWA) discriminated against local businesses and refused to grant them credit.

## Quick summary

1. The colonial economy was extractive; its purpose was to supply raw materials to British industries and serve as a market for British manufactured goods.
2. The British replaced traditional currencies with British money (issued by the WACB in 1912) and introduced direct taxation to force Nigerians into the cash economy.
3. Railways and ports were built primarily to transport cash crops and minerals to the coast for export to Europe.
4. Agriculture relied on Nigerian peasant farmers growing cash crops like palm produce (East), cocoa (West), and groundnut/cotton (North). 
5. Marketing boards bought these crops at low prices, keeping the surplus profits for the colonial government.
6. The colonial government owned all mineral rights, focusing heavily on tin mining in Jos and coal mining in Enugu.
7. Commerce and banking were dominated by European firms (like UAC and BBWA) that marginalized Nigerian traders and refused them credit, leading to the rise of indigenous banks.
8. The colonial economy drastically changed Nigerian society by causing rapid urbanization, rural-urban migration, monetization, and the emergence of new social classes.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary purpose of railway construction by the colonial administration in Nigeria was to:**
- A. make passenger travel comfortable for Nigerians
- B. connect all traditional ethnic groups together
- C. evacuate raw materials from the interior to the coast
- D. encourage the growth of heavy industries in the interior

**Answer: C.** The railways were built specifically to transport cash crops (like groundnuts and cotton) and minerals (like coal and tin) to ports for export to Great Britain. 

**2. The introduction of British currency and the demonetization of cowries and manillas forced Nigerians to:**
- A. trade only with the French and Germans
- B. abandon agriculture and become bankers
- C. engage in the colonial wage and cash crop economy to survive
- D. return entirely to the barter system

**Answer: C.** By making traditional money worthless and requiring taxes to be paid in British currency, Nigerians had to work for wages or grow cash crops to get the new money.

**3. Which of the following minerals was primarily mined in Enugu during the colonial period?**
- A. Tin
- B. Coal
- C. Gold
- D. Petroleum

**Answer: B.** Coal was discovered in Enugu in 1909 and mined commercially from 1915 to power railways and electricity generation. Tin is associated with Jos.

**4. The colonial administration deliberately discouraged the establishment of heavy manufacturing industries in Nigeria because they:**
- A. lacked the technical knowledge to build factories
- B. wanted Nigeria to remain a market for British manufactured goods
- C. wanted to protect the Nigerian environment from pollution
- D. believed Nigerians were not interested in factory work

**Answer: B.** Britain's economic model required colonies to supply raw materials and buy finished goods. If Nigeria manufactured its own goods, British factories would lose a major market.

**5. Why did wealthy Nigerians establish indigenous banks like the National Bank of Nigeria and the African Continental Bank?**
- A. They wanted to print their own local currencies.
- B. Expatriate banks like BBWA discriminated against them and denied them credit.
- C. The colonial government ordered them to handle agricultural taxes.
- D. They wanted to lend money to the British government after World War II.

**Answer: B.** British banks existed mainly to serve European firms and the colonial government, routinely refusing loans to Nigerian businessmen, which forced Nigerians to start their own banks.

**6. The West African Currency Board (WACB) was established in:**
- A. 1898
- B. 1912
- C. 1929
- D. 1945

**Answer: B.** The WACB was established in 1912 to supply a uniform currency for all British West African colonies.

**7. Which region of colonial Nigeria was the highest producer of groundnut and cotton for export?**
- A. Eastern Region
- B. Mid-Western Region
- C. Western Region
- D. Northern Region

**Answer: D.** The Northern Region, particularly around Kano, was the center for groundnut and cotton production. The West produced cocoa, and the East produced palm oil.

**8. The actual economic effect of the colonial Marketing Boards on Nigerian peasant farmers was that they:**
- A. made farmers incredibly wealthy by paying above world market prices
- B. accumulated surplus funds for the government by paying farmers less than the world price
- C. helped farmers build their own processing factories
- D. stopped farmers from paying the direct tax

**Answer: B.** While the stated aim was price stabilization, the boards paid farmers a fraction of what the crops were worth internationally, essentially taxing them heavily to fund the government.

**9. The most dominant expatriate trading firm in colonial Nigeria, formed in 1929, was the:**
- A. Royal Niger Company
- B. CFAO
- C. United Africa Company (UAC)
- D. John Holt

**Answer: C.** The United Africa Company (UAC) was formed in 1929 from the merger of the Niger Company (the former Royal Niger Company) and the African and Eastern Trade Corporation, and it dominated the import-export trade.

**10. How did the colonial economy most directly affect rural society?**
- A. It caused a massive migration of young men from rural villages to urban centers for wage labor.
- B. It made traditional chiefs richer than British administrators.
- C. It completely destroyed all forms of traditional religion.
- D. It caused all peasant farmers to become factory workers.

**Answer: A.** The demand for wage labor in mines, railways, and ports drew young men away from rural villages to new urban centers, altering traditional agricultural and family life.
