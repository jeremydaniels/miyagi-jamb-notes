## Introduction to the 19th-Century West African Jihads

In the 19th century, West Africa experienced a wave of Islamic revolutions known as **jihads** (holy wars). These movements were led by Muslim scholars (clerics) who wanted to purify the practice of Islam, eliminate syncretism (mixing Islam with traditional African religions), overthrow corrupt or oppressive rulers, and establish strong, centralized states based on **Sharia** (Islamic law). 

The most famous of these was the **Sokoto Jihad** of 1804 led by Usman dan Fodio in the Hausa states (covered in detail in `B1-the-sokoto-caliphate.md`). However, Sokoto was not the only one. The success in Sokoto inspired and directly influenced other major reform movements across the western Sudan (the savanna belt of West Africa), particularly those of **Seku Ahmadu** in Macina and **Al-Hajj Umar** in the Senegambia and Mali regions. Later in the century, **Samori Toure** used Islam to build a massive resistance empire against the French.

## The Relationship between the Sokoto Jihad and other Jihads

**Objective i: establish the relationship between the Sokoto Jihad and other Jihads in West Africa.**

**Content a: Relationship between Sokoto and other Jihads.**

The Sokoto Jihad (1804) was the pioneer of the 19th-century West African Islamic revolutions. It served as a blueprint, an inspiration, and sometimes a direct training ground for the leaders of other jihads. The relationship between Sokoto and the other movements can be seen in several ways:

### 1. Ideological and Spiritual Inspiration
Usman dan Fodio provided the intellectual justification for jihad in West Africa. He wrote numerous books and poems explaining when a jihad was necessary (e.g., against rulers who mixed Islam with paganism or oppressed Muslims). These writings were copied, circulated widely across West Africa, and read by other scholars, providing them with the legal and religious arguments to launch their own holy wars. Both Sokoto and Seku Ahmadu's movement belonged to the **Qadiriyya** Islamic brotherhood, sharing a common spiritual bond.

### 2. The Flag-Bearer Tradition and Legitimacy
When Usman dan Fodio launched his jihad, he gave flags to his commanders (emirs) to legitimize their rule on his behalf. Other jihadists sought this same legitimacy. 
- **Seku Ahmadu** of Macina originally applied to Sokoto for a flag and blessing to start his jihad. According to most historical accounts, Usman dan Fodio or his son Muhammad Bello sent a flag, but the exact political relationship became complicated. Seku Ahmadu eventually claimed independent authority and took the title of Caliph (Commander of the Faithful) himself, making his state independent of Sokoto, though they maintained a respectful correspondence.

### 3. Direct Contact and Training
The relationship was not just through books; there was direct personal contact.
- **Al-Hajj Umar** actually lived in the Sokoto Caliphate for several years on his way back from his pilgrimage to Mecca. He was deeply influenced by Sokoto's administration and military success. He became a close friend and advisor to Sultan Muhammad Bello (Usman dan Fodio's son and successor) and even married one of Bello's daughters (Mariatu). Umar studied how Sokoto organized its army, raised taxes, and governed its vast territories. When Umar left Sokoto to launch his own jihad in the west, he took with him the military and administrative lessons he had learned.

### 4. Shared Aims and State-Building
All these jihads shared common characteristics modelled after Sokoto:
- They were initially led by the **Fulani (Fula)** ethnic group (except for Samori Toure, who was Mandinka).
- They aimed to replace ethnic loyalty with religious unity.
- They replaced traditional chiefdoms with centralized Islamic states (emirates or caliphates) governed by Sharia law.
- They promoted Islamic education and literacy in Arabic.

**Note on differences:** While Sokoto inspired them, the later jihads had different brotherhoods (Al-Hajj Umar belonged to the rival **Tijaniyya** order) and sometimes clashed with each other (Al-Hajj Umar eventually attacked and destroyed Seku Ahmadu's Macina Empire because he believed they were not true Muslims). 

## The Jihads of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar

**Objective ii: compare the achievements of the Jihads of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar.**

**Content b: The Jihads of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar.**

Though both were inspired by Islamic reform, the jihads of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar occurred in different areas, faced different enemies, and produced different types of states.

### The Jihad of Seku Ahmadu (The Macina Empire)

**Background:** 
Seku Ahmadu (also spelt Shehu Ahmadu or Seku Amadu) was a Fulani Islamic scholar born around 1776 in Macina (Massina), an area in the inland delta of the Niger River in present-day Mali. The region was controlled by the powerful animist **Bambara Kingdom of Segou**. The local Fulani chiefs (called *Ardos*) paid tribute to Segou and were often accused of being corrupt and practicing syncretism.

**Causes of the Jihad:**
Seku Ahmadu, influenced by the Qadiriyya brotherhood and Usman dan Fodio's teachings, began preaching against the syncretism of the local Fulani *Ardos* and the oppression of the Bambara overlords. His strict teachings attracted many followers but angered the *Ardos*, who reported him to the King of Segou. When Segou sent an army to crush Ahmadu's followers around 1818, Ahmadu declared a jihad.

**Course and State Building:**
Ahmadu's forces defeated the Bambara and their Fulani allies at the **Battle of Noukouma** (around 1818). Following this victory, he established an independent Islamic state known as the **Macina Empire** (or the Caliphate of Hamdallahi). 

**Key Achievements of Seku Ahmadu:**
1. **Creation of a strict Theocracy:** He built a new capital called **Hamdallahi** ("Praise be to God"). The state was highly centralized and ruled strictly by Sharia law.
2. **The Grand Council:** Unlike many other empires ruled by a single military dictator, Macina was governed by a Grand Council of 40 Islamic scholars who advised Seku Ahmadu on all matters. The rule of law applied to everyone, including the Caliph.
3. **Economic and Social Reforms:** He ordered the sedentarization (settling down) of the nomadic Fulani cattle herders to ensure they attended mosques, received Islamic education, and paid taxes. He standardized weights and measures in the markets and established a strong economy based on agriculture and trade.
4. **Promotion of Education:** He made Islamic education compulsory and built hundreds of Qur'anic schools. He banned alcohol, tobacco, and un-Islamic music and dancing.

Seku Ahmadu died in 1845 and was succeeded by his son (Amadu II) and later his grandson (Amadu III). The empire remained stable until it was conquered by Al-Hajj Umar in 1862.

### The Jihad of Al-Hajj Umar (The Tukolor/Tijaniyya Empire)

**Background:**
Al-Hajj Umar (often spelt Umar Tall) was a Tukolor (a branch of the Fulani) Islamic scholar born around 1797 in Futa Toro (modern Senegal). While Seku Ahmadu and Usman dan Fodio belonged to the Qadiriyya brotherhood, Umar joined the **Tijaniyya** brotherhood, a newer, more egalitarian Islamic order. 

**The Pilgrimage and Preparation:**
In the 1820s, Umar went on a long pilgrimage to Mecca (*Al-Hajj*), where he was appointed the Caliph (leader) of the Tijaniyya order for the whole of West Africa. On his return journey, he spent time in Borno and several years in Sokoto, marrying into the royal family and learning statecraft and military strategy. Returning to the west, he settled in **Dinguiraye** (in modern Guinea), bought European firearms, and built a heavily armed following.

**Causes and Course of the Jihad:**
In 1852, Umar declared a jihad. His aims were to convert pagans, spread the Tijaniyya brotherhood, and create a massive Islamic empire. 
- He first conquered the Bambara kingdoms of Kaarta and Segou, forcing mass conversions.
- Unlike previous jihadists, Umar encountered European imperialists. He clashed with the advancing French in Senegal but was blocked, forcing him to direct his conquests eastward.
- **Conflict with Macina:** In a highly controversial move, Umar attacked the Muslim Macina Empire (Seku Ahmadu's state). He justified this by claiming Macina had allied with the pagan Bambara against him and that they belonged to the rival Qadiriyya order. He conquered Hamdallahi in 1862.

This action alienated many Muslims. The people of Macina rebelled, and Al-Hajj Umar was killed in 1864 while suppressing the revolt. His vast **Tukolor Empire** was inherited by his son, Ahmadu Seku (not to be confused with Seku Ahmadu), who struggled to hold it together against internal revolts and the invading French.

**Key Achievements of Al-Hajj Umar:**
1. **Military Expansion:** He created one of the largest empires in 19th-century West Africa, stretching across modern Senegal, Guinea, and Mali.
2. **Spread of the Tijaniyya:** He successfully popularized the Tijaniyya brotherhood, which today remains one of the largest Islamic orders in West Africa.
3. **Military Innovations:** He equipped his army with European firearms obtained from coastal trade and built massive defensive fortresses known as **tatas**.
4. **Anti-Colonial Resistance:** Though ultimately unsuccessful, he organized early armed resistance against French colonial expansion in the Senegambia region.

### Comparing the Achievements of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar

Both leaders successfully created large Islamic states, but their methods, focus, and legacies were very different.

| Feature / Achievement | Seku Ahmadu (Macina Empire) | Al-Hajj Umar (Tukolor Empire) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Islamic Brotherhood** | **Qadiriyya** (older, traditional, hierarchical). | **Tijaniyya** (newer, egalitarian, exclusive). |
| **Nature of the Jihad** | Mainly an internal revolt against corrupt local rulers and local pagans (Bambara). | A massive, expansionist war of conquest against pagans, other Muslims, and the French. |
| **Administration** | Highly bureaucratic and legalistic. Ruled through a **Grand Council of 40 scholars**. Emphasis on justice and Sharia. | Highly militaristic. Ruled through military governors and his own relatives/sons. |
| **Military Approach** | Relied on traditional Fulani cavalry and local weapons. Focused on defense of Macina. | Relied on an organized standing army, **imported European firearms**, and stone fortresses (*tatas*). |
| **Economic & Social Achievements** | Settled nomadic herders, built schools, created a stable, peaceful, and prosperous internal economy. | Focused less on internal civil administration and more on constant warfare, taxation to fund the army, and forced conversions. |
| **Relationship with the French** | Empire was relatively isolated from the coast; no major clashes with Europeans in his lifetime. | Clashed directly with the French in Senegal; one of the early leaders of anti-colonial resistance. |
| **Downfall of the Empire** | Destroyed by fellow Muslims (Al-Hajj Umar) in 1862. | Weakened by internal rebellions and finally conquered by the French in the 1890s. |

## The Activities of Samori Toure and the Mandinka Empire

**Objective iii: examine the activities of Samori Toure of the Mandinka Empire.**

**Content c: The activities of Samori Toure.**

While Usman dan Fodio, Seku Ahmadu, and Al-Hajj Umar were primarily Islamic scholars who became state builders, **Samori Toure** (born around 1830) was a trader and a warrior who used Islam as a tool for state-building and resistance. He created the **Mandinka Empire** (also known as the Wassoulou Empire), centered in modern-day Guinea, Mali, and Ivory Coast.

### Background and Rise to Power
Samori was born into a Mandinka (Malinke) trading family (the Dyula). He was not a traditional Islamic scholar. As a young man, he learned the military trade to rescue his mother, who had been captured in a local war. He proved to be a military genius and gradually built his own private army, subduing local chiefs. By the 1870s, he had conquered a vast territory, making the trading city of **Kankan** a major center of his empire. 

To unite his diverse empire, Samori adopted Islam as the state religion. In 1884, he took the religious title of **Almamy** (Commander of the Faithful) and urged his subjects to convert to Islam, though he was more tolerant of traditional practices than the earlier Fulani jihadists.

### Samori Toure's Activities and Achievements in State Building

Samori is remembered as one of the greatest state-builders in African history. His activities included:

1. **Creation of the *Sofa* Army:** 
   Samori built a highly professional, disciplined standing army. His infantry soldiers were known as the **Sofas**. Promotion in his army was based on merit and bravery, not birth or ethnicity. 
2. **Modernization of Weapons:**
   He recognized the superiority of European weapons. He traded gold and ivory with the British in Sierra Leone to buy modern repeating rifles. Even more impressively, he employed local blacksmiths to repair and actively **manufacture copies of European rifles** and ammunition, making his army less dependent on European supplies.
3. **Administrative Organization:**
   He divided his vast empire into 162 cantons (districts), grouped into provinces. Each province was governed by a military commander and a civil administrator, ensuring strict control, efficient taxation, and loyalty.
4. **Promotion of Trade and Agriculture:**
   As a former trader, Samori protected the trade routes. He ensured that agriculture continued even during wartime, storing food to feed his massive army.

### Resistance against the French Imperialists

Samori Toure is most famous for his legendary, long-running resistance against French colonial expansion, which lasted for sixteen years (1882–1898).

- **Diplomacy and Warfare:** When the French advanced into his territory in 1882, Samori initially fought them, but realizing their artillery was too strong, he used diplomacy. He signed the **Treaty of Bissandugu (1886)** with the French, agreeing to a border to buy time to reorganize his army.
- **The British Refusal:** He tried to play the British and French against each other, even offering his empire to the British for protection. The British, having agreed to spheres of influence with the French, refused to help him and stopped selling him weapons.
- **Scorched-Earth Policy and Moving the Empire:** When full-scale war resumed in 1891, Samori used brilliant guerrilla tactics. He realized he could not defeat the French artillery in open battle. He adopted a **scorched-earth policy**: as he retreated eastward, his army destroyed crops, burned villages, and poisoned wells, leaving nothing for the advancing French army to eat or use. 
- Amazingly, he organized the mass migration of his entire population and army eastwards, conquering new lands (such as Kong; his long siege of Sikasso in 1887–1888 failed) and rebuilding his empire in a completely new location (modern Ivory Coast/Ghana borders) while fighting off the French.

### Fall and Exile

Despite his brilliance, Samori was cut off from weapons supplies, and his people were exhausted by constant migration and warfare. In **1898**, a surprise French column captured him at his camp in Guelemou. He was exiled to an island in Ndjolé, Gabon, where he died in 1900.

**Significance:** Samori Toure is celebrated as a hero of African resistance. He demonstrated exceptional organizational, military, and diplomatic skills, holding off a modernized European army far longer than almost any other African ruler of his time.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Jihad** | A holy war or struggle; in this context, 19th-century wars to purify Islam and create Islamic states. |
| **Caliphate** | An Islamic state ruled by a Caliph (a supreme religious and political leader). |
| **Qadiriyya** | An older Islamic Sufi brotherhood, to which Usman dan Fodio and Seku Ahmadu belonged. |
| **Tijaniyya** | A newer, more egalitarian Islamic brotherhood championed and spread by Al-Hajj Umar. |
| **Syncretism** | The mixing of different religious beliefs (e.g., combining Islam with traditional African religion). |
| **Ardo** | Traditional title for local Fulani leaders in the Macina region before the jihad. |
| **Sofa** | The professional infantry soldiers of Samori Toure's army. |
| **Almamy** | A title meaning "Commander of the Faithful" (from Arabic Al-Imam), adopted by Samori Toure in 1884. |
| **Tata** | A massive fortified mud or stone wall/fortress built by Al-Hajj Umar to defend his territories. |
| **Scorched-earth policy** | A military strategy of destroying crops, buildings, and resources that might be useful to an invading enemy (used brilliantly by Samori Toure). |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse Seku Ahmadu with Ahmadu Seku:** *Seku Ahmadu* founded the Macina Empire. *Ahmadu Seku* was the son of Al-Hajj Umar who inherited the Tukolor Empire. JAMB frequently tests this confusion.
- **Match the Brotherhoods:** Remember that Sokoto and Macina (Seku Ahmadu) were **Qadiriyya**, while Al-Hajj Umar was **Tijaniyya**.
- **Know the Capitals:** Seku Ahmadu built **Hamdallahi**. Al-Hajj Umar's early base was **Dinguiraye**. Samori Toure's early capital was **Bissandugu** (and he controlled Kankan).
- **Al-Hajj Umar's connection to Sokoto:** He did not fight against Sokoto; he stayed there, learned from them, and married into the royal family. He *did* fight against Macina, however.
- **Samori's background:** Unlike the others, Samori Toure was not Fulani (he was Mandinka), and he started as a trader and warrior, not an Islamic cleric. 
- **The spelling of Mandinka:** Remember that if you see "Madinka" in the exam options, it refers to the Mandinka (or Malinke) Empire of Samori Toure.

## Quick summary

1. The Sokoto Jihad of 1804 inspired other West African reform movements ideologically, politically, and through direct contact, such as Al-Hajj Umar's extended stay in Sokoto.
2. Seku Ahmadu led a jihad in 1818 against the Bambara and corrupt Fulani *Ardos*, establishing the strict, well-administered Macina Empire (Caliphate of Hamdallahi) ruled by a council of 40 scholars.
3. Al-Hajj Umar launched a massive military jihad in 1852, building the Tukolor Empire, spreading the Tijaniyya brotherhood, and controversially destroying the Muslim Macina Empire.
4. While Seku Ahmadu's state was peaceful and bureaucratic, Al-Hajj Umar's state was militaristic, expansionist, and relied heavily on European firearms and fortresses.
5. Samori Toure built the Mandinka (Wassoulou) Empire in the late 19th century. A former trader, he adopted the title *Almamy* to unite his state through Islam.
6. Samori created a highly disciplined standing army (the *Sofa*), manufactured his own rifles, and used a brilliant scorched-earth policy to resist the French for 16 years.
7. Samori Toure was captured by the French in 1898 and exiled to Gabon, marking the end of his empire.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary relationship between the Sokoto Jihad and the later jihads in West Africa was that Sokoto:**

- A. conquered the other jihad leaders and forced them to pay tribute
- B. provided the ideological inspiration and administrative model for them
- C. supplied the other movements with European artillery
- D. was established by the Tijaniyya brotherhood to rival the Qadiriyya

**Answer: B.** Sokoto served as the pioneer, providing the religious justification (through Usman dan Fodio's writings) and the political blueprint for other states like Macina and the Tukolor Empire.

**2. Which of the following leaders founded the Caliphate of Hamdallahi (Macina Empire)?**

- A. Usman dan Fodio
- B. Ahmadu Seku
- C. Seku Ahmadu
- D. Al-Hajj Umar

**Answer: C.** Seku Ahmadu founded the Macina Empire and its capital, Hamdallahi. A common mistake is choosing Ahmadu Seku, who was actually the son of Al-Hajj Umar.

**3. A major difference between the achievements of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar is that Seku Ahmadu:**

- A. spread the Tijaniyya brotherhood, while Umar spread the Qadiriyya
- B. governed through a Grand Council of scholars, while Umar relied on a military administration
- C. fought mostly against the French, while Umar fought only traditional rulers
- D. built his empire using imported European firearms

**Answer: B.** Seku Ahmadu's administration was highly bureaucratic and advised by a 40-man council. Al-Hajj Umar's was a military dictatorship. Umar was the one who spread the Tijaniyya and relied heavily on European firearms.

**4. Al-Hajj Umar’s jihad was controversial among some Muslims primarily because he:**

- A. refused to perform the pilgrimage to Mecca
- B. allied with the French against local rulers
- C. attacked and conquered the Muslim Macina Empire
- D. abolished the use of the Arabic language

**Answer: C.** Umar attacked Seku Ahmadu's successors in Macina in 1862, justifying it by saying they were allied with pagans and belonged to a rival brotherhood. This angered many Muslims and led to the rebellion that killed him.

**5. The professional infantry soldiers that formed the backbone of Samori Toure’s army were known as the:**

- A. Ardos
- B. Sofa
- C. Almamy
- D. Dyula

**Answer: B.** The *Sofa* were his highly disciplined infantrymen. *Almamy* was his own title, *Ardo* refers to Fulani chiefs, and *Dyula* is a Mandinka trading class.

**6. Which Islamic brotherhood was actively promoted and spread across West Africa by Al-Hajj Umar?**

- A. Qadiriyya
- B. Tijaniyya
- C. Sanusiyya
- D. Mahdiyya

**Answer: B.** Al-Hajj Umar was the Caliph of the Tijaniyya in West Africa. Sokoto and Macina were associated with the Qadiriyya.

**7. To avoid capture by the superior artillery of the French army, Samori Toure famously adopted a strategy of:**

- A. surrendering his capital and retreating to Sokoto
- B. retreating eastward while destroying crops and resources behind him
- C. hiding his army in the deep coastal forests of Liberia
- D. allying with the Bambara to attack the French from the north

**Answer: B.** This is the "scorched-earth policy", a brilliant military tactic where he denied the advancing French any resources by destroying everything as he moved his empire to the east.

**8. According to most historical accounts, Al-Hajj Umar’s direct relationship with the Sokoto Caliphate included:**

- A. leading an army to conquer Sokoto for the Tijaniyya order
- B. living in Sokoto, studying its administration, and marrying into the royal family
- C. receiving the original flag of jihad from Usman dan Fodio in 1804
- D. being exiled to Sokoto by the French colonial government

**Answer: B.** Umar stayed in Sokoto on his way back from Mecca, became close to Sultan Muhammad Bello, and married his daughter. He learned much about statecraft there before starting his own jihad.

**9. Samori Toure differed from earlier 19th-century jihad leaders like Usman dan Fodio and Seku Ahmadu in that Samori:**

- A. was a traditional Islamic scholar who had never been to war before
- B. started his career as a warrior and trader rather than a cleric
- C. belonged to the Fulani ethnic group
- D. banned the use of firearms in his army

**Answer: B.** Samori was a Mandinka trader (Dyula) and soldier who later adopted Islam (taking the title *Almamy*) to unify his empire. The others were Fulani Islamic scholars from the start.

**10. In what year was Samori Toure finally captured by the French, bringing an end to the Mandinka Empire?**

- A. 1864
- B. 1884
- C. 1898
- D. 1914

**Answer: C.** Samori was captured in 1898 after 16 years of resistance. 1864 is the year Al-Hajj Umar died, and 1884 is when Samori took the title of *Almamy*.
