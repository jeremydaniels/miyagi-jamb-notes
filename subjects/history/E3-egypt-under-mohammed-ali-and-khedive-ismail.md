# JAMB History Notes: Egypt under Mohammed Ali and Khedive Ismail

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section E, Topic 3 of the JAMB History syllabus: Egypt under Mohammed Ali and Khedive Ismail. It covers the transformation of Egypt in the 19th century, from its modernization to its eventual occupation by the British.

The syllabus includes four content items:
- a. The rise of Mohammad Ali and his reforms
- b. Mohammad Ali’s relations with the Europeans
- c. Ismail’s fiscal policies
- d. The British occupation of Egypt

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
1. determine the factors that aided Mohammad Ali’s rise to power and his reforms;
2. establish the relationship between Mohammad Ali’s Empire and the Europeans;
3. account for the fiscal policies of Ismail;
4. examine the reasons for the British occupation of Egypt.

> **Note on the syllabus wording:** The syllabus spells the name as both **Mohammed Ali** (in the topic title) and **Mohammad Ali** (in the contents and objectives). In most modern history textbooks, he is called **Muhammad Ali** of Egypt. We will use "Mohammad Ali" in these notes to match the syllabus objectives exactly.

## The rise of Mohammad Ali and his reforms

At the end of the 18th century, Egypt was officially a province of the Ottoman Empire (based in modern-day Turkey). However, real power in Egypt was held by the **Mamluks**, a powerful military class of former slaves who had ruled for centuries. 

In **1798**, the French general Napoleon Bonaparte invaded Egypt. The French easily defeated the traditional Mamluk cavalry, proving that Egypt's military was entirely outdated. Although the French were driven out by a joint British and Ottoman force in **1801**, their short occupation changed Egypt forever by breaking the power of the Mamluks and introducing European ideas.

### Factors that aided Mohammad Ali's rise to power and his reforms

Mohammad Ali was an Albanian commander in the Ottoman army who arrived in Egypt in 1801 to help drive out the French. Within a few years, he made himself the absolute ruler of Egypt.

**Factors that aided his rise to power:**
1. **The power vacuum:** The French invasion had severely weakened the Mamluks, and the British forces withdrew shortly after, leaving no single group in firm control. 
2. **Loyalty of his troops:** Mohammad Ali commanded a highly disciplined force of Albanian soldiers who were loyal to him personally.
3. **Diplomatic cunning:** He successfully played his rivals against one another, siding with the Mamluks against the Ottoman governors, and then turning against the Mamluks.
4. **Support of the people:** He allied himself with the Egyptian religious leaders (the *Ulama*) and the local people, promising to reduce the heavy taxes imposed by the Mamluks.
5. **Ottoman recognition:** Seeing that Mohammad Ali was the only man capable of restoring order, the Ottoman Sultan officially recognized him as the **Wāli** (Governor) of Egypt in **1805**.
6. **Elimination of rivals:** In **1811**, Mohammad Ali invited the remaining Mamluk leaders to a banquet at the Citadel in Cairo. Once they were inside, he had the gates locked and massacred them. This brutal act wiped out his last internal opposition and gave him absolute power.

**Factors that aided his reforms:**
Once in power, Mohammad Ali's main goal was to build a modern, European-style army so he could become independent of the Ottoman Empire. Several factors made his massive reforms possible:
1. **Absolute control:** With the Mamluks dead, there was no traditional nobility left to resist his changes.
2. **State monopolies (wealth):** He abolished the old tax-farming system (*Iltizam*), confiscated land, and made the government the sole owner of all land and major exports. This monopoly system gave him the huge revenues needed to fund his army and factories.
3. **European expertise:** He knew he needed Western technology. He freely hired European (especially French) military officers, engineers, and doctors, and sent Egyptian students to study in Europe.
4. **Mass conscription:** Instead of relying on unreliable foreign mercenaries, he forced Egyptian peasant farmers (the *fellahin*) to serve in his new army.

> **Common mistake:** Students often think Mohammad Ali was an Egyptian nationalist who wanted to free the Egyptian people. He was actually an ambitious Albanian commander who saw Egypt as a base to build his own personal empire, and he taxed and conscripted the Egyptian peasants heavily.

### Mohammad Ali's modernization reforms

To achieve his goals, Mohammad Ali totally reorganized the Egyptian state:

- **Military reforms:** He created the *Nizam-i-Jedid* (New Order), a modern army trained by European officers. He built a modern navy and opened military schools.
- **Agricultural reforms:** He ordered the planting of **long-staple cotton**, a high-quality crop that became highly demanded by British textile mills. Cotton became the backbone of the Egyptian economy. He also built dams and the Mahmoudiya Canal to improve irrigation.
- **Industrial reforms:** He set up state-owned factories to produce weapons, ships, uniforms, and textiles so his army would not have to depend on European imports.
- **Educational reforms:** He established modern secular schools (medical, engineering, and language schools), brought in foreign teachers, and set up the first Arabic printing press in Bulaq to translate European technical books.

## Mohammad Ali's relations with the Europeans

### Relationship between Mohammad Ali's Empire and the Europeans

Mohammad Ali's relationship with the European powers was complex. On one hand, he admired their technology and relied heavily on French experts to modernize his state. On the other hand, his growing military power and his economic monopolies became a major threat to European interests, particularly those of Britain.

**Expansionism and military clashes:**
With his modern army, Mohammad Ali fought campaigns to expand his empire:
1. **Arabia and Sudan:** He fought the Wahhabis in Arabia on behalf of the Ottoman Sultan, and conquered Sudan in the 1820s to capture slaves for his army and secure gold.
2. **Greece (1827):** He sent his modern fleet to help the Ottoman Sultan crush a rebellion in Greece. However, the European powers (Britain, France, and Russia) supported the Greeks and destroyed Mohammad Ali's fleet at the **Battle of Navarino**.
3. **Syria and the Oriental Crisis (1831–1841):** Mohammad Ali turned against the Ottoman Sultan, invaded Syria, and completely defeated the Ottoman army. He was on the verge of capturing the Ottoman capital (Constantinople).

**European intervention:**
Britain was deeply alarmed by Mohammad Ali's success. The British government wanted to keep the Ottoman Empire alive as a weak "buffer" state to block Russian expansion and to protect the vital British trade routes to India. Britain did not want a strong, independent Egyptian empire controlling the Middle East. Furthermore, British merchants hated Mohammad Ali's state monopolies, which locked them out of the Egyptian market.

- **Treaty of Balta Liman (1838):** Britain pressured the Ottoman Sultan to sign this treaty, which abolished all monopolies in the Ottoman Empire (including Egypt). This devastated Mohammad Ali's state-run economy.
- **The Treaty of London (1841):** When Mohammad Ali refused to leave Syria, a coalition of European powers (led by Britain) intervened militarily and defeated his forces. They forced him to sign the Treaty of London. 
  - **The terms:** He had to reduce his army to a mere 18,000 men and give up Syria and Arabia. In return, the Ottoman Sultan granted him and his descendants the hereditary right to rule Egypt. 

The Europeans had successfully contained Mohammad Ali, ensuring that Egypt remained subordinate to Western economic and political interests.

## Ismail's fiscal policies

Mohammad Ali died in 1849. In 1863, his grandson **Ismail** came to power. Ismail was granted the prestigious title of **Khedive** (Viceroy) by the Ottoman Sultan. 

Ismail's vision was to completely Europeanize Egypt. He famously declared, *"My country is no longer in Africa; we are now part of Europe."*

### Accounting for the fiscal policies of Ismail

Ismail's reign is famous for rapid modernization, but it ended in spectacular financial ruin. His fiscal (financial) policies were characterized by massive borrowing, wasteful spending, and a reliance on temporary agricultural booms.

**1. The Cotton Boom:**
During the American Civil War (1861–1865), American cotton exports to Europe stopped. European textile mills turned to Egypt. The price of Egyptian long-staple cotton skyrocketed, bringing enormous wealth into Egypt. Believing this high income would last forever, Ismail began borrowing heavily against it. When the American war ended, cotton prices crashed, but Ismail’s spending continued.

**2. Ambitious infrastructure and modernization:**
Ismail spent vast sums on modernizing the country. He built thousands of miles of railways and telegraph lines, established postal services, and expanded the education system. He modernized Cairo, building European-style boulevards, opera houses, and palaces. 

**3. The Suez Canal (1869):**
The most famous project of his reign was the Suez Canal, constructed by a French company to connect the Mediterranean Sea to the Red Sea. Ismail spent fortunes supporting the construction and threw a lavish, incredibly expensive international party for its opening in 1869, inviting European royalty.

**4. Expansionist wars:**
Ismail tried to expand his empire in Africa. He pushed further into Sudan and attempted to conquer Ethiopia, but his modern army was disastrously and expensively defeated at battles like the Battle of Gura in 1876.

**5. Debt and Bankruptcy:**
To pay for all this, Ismail took huge loans from European bankers (in Britain and France) at extremely high interest rates. When he could no longer make the repayments, he tried a desperate measure:
- **Sale of Suez Canal shares (1875):** Ismail sold Egypt’s 44% share in the Suez Canal Company to the British government (organized by Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli) for £4 million. 

**6. The Dual Control (1876):**
Selling the shares only delayed the inevitable. In 1876, Egypt essentially declared bankruptcy. To ensure they got their money back, European creditors forced Ismail to accept the *Caisse de la Dette Publique* (Public Debt Commission). This evolved into the **Dual Control**, a system where a British official controlled Egypt's revenues and a French official controlled its spending. Egypt had lost its financial independence.

> **Common mistake:** Do not confuse who built what. Mohammad Ali built the Mahmoudiya Canal (for irrigation); his grandson Khedive Ismail oversaw the completion of the Suez Canal (for international shipping).

## The British occupation of Egypt

Ismail’s massive debts and the resulting European interference made him unpopular. In 1879, Britain and France pressured the Ottoman Sultan to depose Ismail and replace him with his weak son, **Tewfik Pasha**.

### Reasons for the British occupation of Egypt

The installation of a weak Khedive and the imposition of Dual Control created an explosive situation in Egypt, leading directly to the British invasion of 1882.

**1. The Urabi Revolt (1881–1882):**
Under Dual Control, the European officials raised taxes on the Egyptian peasants to pay off foreign debts. They also cut the size of the Egyptian army and gave the best jobs to Europeans or Turkish-Circassian elites. This caused deep anger. A nationalist movement emerged, led by an Egyptian army officer named **Colonel Ahmed Urabi**. Using the slogan *"Egypt for the Egyptians"*, Urabi forced Khedive Tewfik to form a nationalist government that aimed to end European financial control.

**2. Protecting the Suez Canal:**
Britain viewed the Suez Canal as the most strategic waterway in the world—it was the "jugular vein" of the British Empire, dramatically cutting the travel time to their most valuable colony, India. The British government feared that Urabi's nationalist government would close the canal to British shipping or refuse to pay the debts owed to European banks.

**3. Protecting European lives and investments:**
Tensions between Egyptians and Europeans grew. In June 1882, anti-European riots broke out in the port city of Alexandria, leaving about 50 Europeans dead. Britain used these riots as a justification to intervene, claiming they needed to restore "law and order" and protect the Khedive from the rebels.

**4. The Invasion and Occupation (1882):**
In July 1882, the British navy bombarded the forts of Alexandria. The French government decided not to join the attack, leaving Britain to act alone. British troops landed in Egypt and crushed Urabi’s forces at the **Battle of Tel el-Kebir** in September 1882. Urabi was exiled, and the British occupied Cairo.

**The result:** The British government claimed the occupation was only "temporary" to restore the authority of the Khedive. In reality, British troops remained in Egypt for over 70 years. Egypt became a **veiled protectorate**: officially it was still an Ottoman province ruled by the Khedive, but the actual ruler was the British Consul-General (the most famous being Lord Cromer).

## Key terms

| --- | --- |
| **Mamluks** | A powerful military class of former slaves who dominated Egypt before Mohammad Ali destroyed them in 1811. |
| **Wāli** | The official title for an Ottoman Governor. |
| **Khedive** | The title of Viceroy, granting more autonomy than a Wāli. First used widely by Mohammad Ali, officially recognized during Ismail's reign. |

| **Long-staple cotton** | A high-quality cash crop introduced by Mohammad Ali that became the foundation of Egypt's export economy. |

| **Veiled Protectorate** | A system where Egypt remained officially part of the Ottoman Empire, but was secretly and practically ruled by the British. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **The Mamluk massacre:** Remember that Mohammad Ali eliminated the Mamluks at the Citadel (1811) to secure his absolute power. This is a very common exam question.
- **The importance of cotton:** Long-staple cotton was the key to Egypt's modernization under Mohammad Ali, and the temporary cotton boom during the American Civil War fueled Ismail's reckless spending.
- **Why Europeans intervened:** The Treaty of Balta Liman (1838) and the Treaty of London (1841) were forced upon Egypt because Britain wanted to protect its trade routes to India and maintain the Ottoman Empire as a buffer state.
- **Selling the canal:** It was Khedive Ismail (not Mohammad Ali) who sold Egypt's shares in the Suez Canal to Britain in 1875 to pay off his debts.
- **The Urabi Revolt:** This was a nationalist uprising against foreign interference. It was the direct excuse the British used to bombard Alexandria and occupy Egypt in 1882.
- **Tel el-Kebir:** The battle in 1882 where British forces defeated Urabi, leading to the long-term British occupation of Egypt.

## Quick summary

1. Following a power vacuum created by the French invasion of 1798, Mohammad Ali, an Albanian commander, rose to power and became the governor of Egypt in 1805.
2. He secured absolute power by massacring the Mamluks in 1811, allowing him to launch massive agricultural, military, and industrial reforms.
3. Mohammad Ali modernized Egypt using European experts, state monopolies, and the mass cultivation of long-staple cotton. 
4. His military expansion into Syria threatened the Ottoman Empire, prompting Britain and other European powers to intervene and restrict his power through the Treaty of London (1841).
5. His grandson, Khedive Ismail, spent recklessly on infrastructure, the Suez Canal, and wars, funded by heavy borrowing from European banks.
6. When Egypt went bankrupt, Ismail sold the Suez Canal shares to Britain (1875), leading to British and French "Dual Control" over Egypt's finances.
7. Anger over European control sparked the nationalist Urabi Revolt (1881–1882).
8. To protect the Suez Canal and European investments, Britain defeated Urabi in 1882 and occupied Egypt, turning it into a "veiled protectorate".

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following factors significantly aided Mohammad Ali's rise to power in Egypt?**
- A. The military support of the French army against the Ottomans
- B. The power vacuum left by the withdrawal of French and British forces
- C. The construction of the Suez Canal
- D. His inheritance of the throne from his father

**Answer: B.** The French invasion destroyed the power of the Mamluks, and the subsequent British withdrawal left a vacuum that Mohammad Ali successfully exploited using his loyal Albanian troops and diplomatic skill.

**2. How did Mohammad Ali permanently eliminate his major internal rivals in 1811?**
- A. He exiled them to Sudan
- B. He defeated them at the Battle of Navarino
- C. He massacred the Mamluk leaders at a banquet in the Citadel
- D. He forced them to work on the Suez Canal

**Answer: C.** Mohammad Ali invited the Mamluk leaders to the Citadel in Cairo and had them massacred, wiping out his internal opposition and gaining absolute power. 

**3. The agricultural reform that transformed Egypt's economy under Mohammad Ali was the introduction of**
- A. cocoa as a major export
- B. the mechanized farming of wheat
- C. long-staple cotton as a cash crop
- D. the plantation system for sugarcane

**Answer: C.** He introduced long-staple cotton, which became highly demanded by European textile mills and formed the financial backbone of his modernization programs.

**4. Why did Britain and other European powers force Mohammad Ali to sign the Treaty of London in 1841?**
- A. To stop his expansion, protect the Ottoman Empire, and safeguard the route to India
- B. To force him to build the Suez Canal
- C. To help him defeat the Wahhabis in Arabia
- D. To prevent him from allying with the United States

**Answer: A.** Britain feared that a strong, independent Egypt would destroy the Ottoman Empire and threaten British trade routes to India, so they intervened to reduce his army and territory.

**5. Which global event caused a massive but temporary boom in Egypt's revenues during the reign of Khedive Ismail?**
- A. The discovery of gold in Sudan
- B. The American Civil War
- C. The invention of the steam engine
- D. The French Revolution

**Answer: B.** The American Civil War stopped American cotton exports, creating a massive demand for Egyptian cotton, which temporarily brought huge wealth to Ismail's government.

**6. The major infrastructure project that symbolized Ismail's modernization but contributed heavily to his bankruptcy was the**
- A. Mahmoudiya Canal
- B. Aswan High Dam
- C. Suez Canal
- D. Cairo Railway

**Answer: C.** The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, was a massively expensive project that, along with Ismail's lavish spending, plunged Egypt deep into debt.

**7. In 1875, what desperate measure did Khedive Ismail take to pay his mounting debts?**
- A. He sold Egypt's shares in the Suez Canal to the British government
- B. He invaded Ethiopia to capture gold mines
- C. He declared war on the Ottoman Empire
- D. He expelled all European bankers from Egypt

**Answer: A.** Facing bankruptcy, Ismail sold Egypt's 44% share in the Suez Canal Company to Britain for £4 million.

**8. What was the "Dual Control" established in Egypt in 1876?**
- A. An alliance between Egypt and the Ottoman Empire
- B. Joint administration of Egypt's finances by British and French officials
- C. The division of the Egyptian army into two commands
- D. The sharing of the Suez Canal between Egypt and Sudan

**Answer: B.** Because of Egypt's inability to pay its debts, European creditors established a system where a British official controlled revenues and a French official controlled expenditures.

**9. The nationalist uprising in Egypt that protested against European financial control and the Khedive's weak leadership was led by**
- A. Mohammad Ali
- B. Lord Cromer
- C. Ahmed Urabi
- D. Tewfik Pasha

**Answer: C.** Colonel Ahmed Urabi led a nationalist movement with the slogan "Egypt for the Egyptians," opposing the heavy taxes and foreign interference caused by the Dual Control.

**10. The primary strategic reason Britain invaded and occupied Egypt in 1882 was to**
- A. stop the spread of Islam into Europe
- B. protect their financial investments and the Suez Canal route to India
- C. help Khedive Ismail regain his throne
- D. establish a colony for British farmers

**Answer: B.** Britain used the Urabi revolt and the Alexandria riots as an excuse to invade, mainly because they feared a nationalist government would close the vital Suez Canal and refuse to pay European debts.
