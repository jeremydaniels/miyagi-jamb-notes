## The frontier wars

**Objective i: determine the factors that led to the frontier wars**

### Background to the conflict
In 1652, the Dutch East India Company established a small refreshment station at the Cape of Good Hope (in modern-day South Africa) to supply their ships travelling to Asia. Over time, the Dutch settlers (who became known as **Boers**, meaning "farmers", or **Afrikaners**) expanded deeper into the interior of South Africa to find new grazing land for their livestock. These semi-nomadic Boer farmers were called **Trekboers**.

As the Trekboers moved eastwards during the 18th century, they displaced the indigenous Khoikhoi herders and San hunter-gatherers. However, by the late 1770s, around the Great Fish River (in the Zuurveld region), the advancing Trekboers met the **Xhosa** people. The Xhosa were a large, well-organised group of Bantu-speaking cattle herders and farmers who were also expanding westwards in search of land.

This meeting sparked a series of bitter conflicts known as the **Frontier Wars** (or the Cape Frontier Wars, sometimes called the Xhosa Wars), which lasted for about a hundred years (from around 1779 to 1878). 

### Factors that led to the frontier wars

The wars were not a single continuous battle, but a cycle of raids, counter-raids, and treaties that were frequently broken. The main causes were:

1. **Competition for land:** Both the Trekboers and the Xhosa were pastoralists (cattle herders). Their survival depended on having vast amounts of grazing land. As both populations grew, they competed fiercely for the fertile pastures of the Zuurveld region.
2. **Scarcity of water:** Southern Africa is a dry region. Access to reliable water sources, especially rivers and springs, was a matter of life and death for the cattle of both groups.
3. **Cattle rustling (stealing):** Cattle were the most important measure of wealth and status for both the Boers and the Xhosa. When grazing land overlapped, cattle inevitably strayed or were stolen. Raids to steal cattle, and retaliatory raids to get them back, frequently escalated into full-scale wars.
4. **Different views on land ownership:** The Boers believed that if they settled on a piece of land and registered it with the Cape government, it belonged to them exclusively. The Xhosa, like most African societies, believed that land belonged to the community and was held in trust by the chief. A chief might grant a Boer permission to use land, but the Boer believed he now owned it permanently, leading to serious misunderstandings.
5. **Shifting boundaries and broken treaties:** The Cape government (first the Dutch, later the British) repeatedly tried to draw fixed borders (such as the Fish River) to keep the Boers and Xhosa apart. Neither side respected these borders. When treaties were signed with one Xhosa chief, other independent Xhosa chiefs did not feel bound by them.

## British intervention in Boer-African relations

**Objective ii: account for British intervention in the Boer-African relations**

### Why the British intervened
In 1795, and permanently in 1806, the British took control of the Cape Colony from the Dutch to protect their sea route to India during the Napoleonic Wars. At first, the British just wanted a peaceful and cheap colony. However, they soon found themselves dragged into the expensive frontier wars between the Boers and the Xhosa. 

The British intervened in the relations between the Boers and the indigenous Africans for several reasons:

1. **Maintaining peace and reducing costs:** Frontier wars were bad for trade and very expensive for the British government to fight. The British wanted to establish strict borders and laws to stop Boers and Africans from fighting.
2. **Humanitarian and Missionary influence:** In the early 19th century, there was a strong humanitarian movement in Britain. Missionaries in South Africa, particularly the **London Missionary Society (LMS)** led by figures like Dr John Philip, sent reports back to Britain detailing the cruel treatment of the Khoikhoi, San, and enslaved people by the Boer farmers. The missionaries pressured the British government to protect the Africans.
3. **Establishing the Rule of Law:** The British wanted to replace the informal, frontier lifestyle of the Boers with British administration and courts, treating all inhabitants as subjects of the British Crown.

### Key actions of British intervention

The British government introduced several laws and policies that deeply angered the Boers:

*   **The Black Circuit (1812):** The British set up a travelling court system. For the first time, Boer farmers could be taken to court and put on trial based on accusations of cruelty made by their Khoikhoi servants or slaves. The Boers felt deeply insulted that the word of an African could be used against a white Christian in court.
*   **The 50th Ordinance (1828):** Pressured by missionaries, the British passed this law, which gave the "free persons of colour" (the Khoikhoi and mixed-race people) the same legal rights as white people. It allowed them to own land and removed the pass laws that had forced them to work for Boer farmers.
*   **Abolition of Slavery (1834):** In 1833, Britain passed a law abolishing slavery throughout the British Empire, which took effect in the Cape in 1834. The Boers were slave owners. They were angry not only about losing their unpaid labour but also because the compensation money offered by the British was too small and had to be collected in London.

**Note:** For the Boers, these British interventions were intolerable. The Boers believed in a strict racial hierarchy where white people were masters and black people were servants. The British idea of legal equality between races destroyed the Boers' traditional way of life.

## The nature of the Great Trek

**Objective iii: describe the nature of the Great Trek**

### What was the Great Trek?
The **Great Trek** was a mass migration of thousands of Boer farmers (known as **Voortrekkers**, meaning "pioneers" or "those who trek ahead") who left the British-ruled Cape Colony between 1835 and the 1840s to settle in the interior of South Africa. 

They left to escape British laws, the abolition of slavery, the influence of missionaries, and the lack of protection against Xhosa raids on the eastern frontier. They wanted to create independent republics where they could preserve their language, culture, and belief in white supremacy.

### How the Trek was organised
The Great Trek was not a single, chaotic movement. It was a planned migration characterised by the following:

1. **Groups and Leaders:** The Voortrekkers moved in several organised parties led by strong figures such as **Piet Retief**, **Andries Pretorius**, **Hendrik Potgieter**, and **Louis Tregardt**. 
2. **The Ox-Wagon:** The symbol of the Great Trek was the covered ox-wagon (*kakebeenwa*). The wagons served as transport, homes, and mobile fortresses. When attacked, the Boers would draw their wagons into a defensive circle called a **laager**, tying them together and filling the gaps with thorn bushes.
3. **Weapons and Religion:** The Voortrekkers were heavily armed with modern, muzzle-loading rifles, and they travelled on horseback, making them a highly mobile and deadly fighting force. They were also deeply religious Calvinists, carrying their Dutch Bibles and believing that God had chosen them to inherit the interior of Africa.
4. **African Servants:** Although the Boers were escaping the abolition of slavery, they did not travel alone. Almost every Voortrekker party included Khoikhoi servants and formerly enslaved people who drove the cattle, led the oxen, and assisted in fighting.

### Encounters with African States
The Voortrekkers moved northwards and crossed the Orange River into territories that had recently been devastated by the **Mfecane** (the wars and population movements caused by the rise of the Zulu kingdom). The Boers wrongly assumed the land was empty. In reality, the displaced African societies were just temporarily scattered or regrouping.

The Trekkers soon clashed with two major military powers:
*   **The Ndebele (Matabele):** Under their king, Mzilikazi, the Ndebele attacked the Voortrekkers to protect their territory. In battles such as Vegkop (1836), the Boers survived using their wagon *laagers* and firearms, eventually driving the Ndebele north across the Limpopo River into present-day Zimbabwe.
*   **The Zulu:** Under King Dingane (Shaka's successor), the Zulu saw the arrival of Piet Retief's party in Natal as a massive threat. Dingane tricked Retief and his men and had them killed at his royal kraal in early 1838. In December 1838, a revenge force of Boers led by Andries Pretorius crushed the Zulu army at the **Battle of Blood River**, killing thousands of Zulu warriors while suffering no fatalities themselves.

## Consequences of the Great Trek

**Objective iv: examine its consequences**

The Great Trek was a major turning point in South African history. Its consequences affected the Boers, the indigenous Africans, and the British.

### Consequences for the Boers
1. **Creation of Independent Republics:** The main political consequence was the establishment of independent Boer states in the interior. The two most important were the **South African Republic (the Transvaal)** across the Vaal River, and the **Orange Free State** between the Orange and Vaal rivers. 
2. **British Recognition:** In the 1850s, the British temporarily gave up trying to control the interior and officially recognised the independence of these Boer republics through the Sand River Convention (1852) and the Bloemfontein Convention (1854).
3. **Preservation of Boer Culture:** The Trek allowed the Boers to maintain their language (Afrikaans), their Calvinist religion, and their isolated, agricultural way of life away from British liberal ideas.

### Consequences for African Societies
1. **Loss of Land and Independence:** The Great Trek led to the permanent dispossession of African land in the interior. Powerful kingdoms like the Ndebele were pushed completely out of South Africa, while the Zulu suffered significant territorial losses.
2. **Subjugation and Labour:** Africans who remained in the areas claimed by the Boers lost their political independence. They were forced to live as "squatters" on what was now white-owned land, providing cheap or forced labour to the Boer farmers in exchange for being allowed to keep a few cattle.
3. **Foundation of Apartheid:** The constitutions of the new Boer republics explicitly stated that there would be no equality between white and black people in state or church. This strict policy of racial segregation and white supremacy laid the early foundations for the 20th-century system of **apartheid**.

### Consequences for the British
1. **Enlargement of the Conflict Zone:** The British goal of a cheap, peaceful Cape Colony failed. The Great Trek simply dragged the zone of conflict deeper into the African continent.
2. **Future Imperial Wars:** When diamonds and gold were later discovered in the Boer republics (in the late 19th century), the British changed their policy and tried to bring the Boers back under British rule. This tension directly caused the devastating **Anglo-Boer Wars**.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Don't confuse the Trekboers and the Voortrekkers.** *Trekboers* were the early nomadic farmers expanding slowly in the 18th century. *Voortrekkers* were the specific group of Boers who deliberately left the Cape Colony in the 1830s in an organised migration (the Great Trek) to escape the British.
*   **The main reason for the Frontier Wars was land and cattle.** Do not select options that say the Xhosa wanted to conquer the Cape; they were fighting to protect their grazing lands and cattle in the Zuurveld.
*   **Understand the Boer grievance against the British.** The Boers did not leave because of high taxes. They left because British laws (the Black Circuit, 50th Ordinance, abolition of slavery) gave Africans legal rights, which destroyed the Boers' socio-economic system of racial inequality.
*   **Know the Battle of Blood River (1838).** This is a very common exam question. It was fought between the Voortrekkers (led by Andries Pretorius) and the Zulu (ruled by Dingane).
*   **African lands were not empty.** Textbooks often note that Boers claimed they moved into "empty" land. Explain that the land only *appeared* empty because communities had been temporarily scattered or moved to high ground because of the *Mfecane*.

## Quick summary

1. In the late 18th century, expanding Trekboers clashed with Xhosa herders in the Zuurveld over land, water, and cattle, sparking a century of Frontier Wars.
2. The British took control of the Cape in 1806 and intervened in Boer-African relations to maintain peace and respond to missionary pressure.
3. British policies such as the 50th Ordinance (giving Africans legal rights) and the abolition of slavery in 1834 deeply angered the Boers.
4. To escape British rule and preserve their way of life, thousands of Boers (Voortrekkers) migrated into the interior in the 1830s, an event known as the Great Trek.
5. The Voortrekkers travelled in ox-wagons, fighting major battles against the Ndebele (Vegkop) and the Zulu (Battle of Blood River in 1838).
6. As a result of the Great Trek, the Boers established independent republics (the Transvaal and Orange Free State).
7. For Africans, the Great Trek meant a massive loss of land, loss of political independence, and being subjected to early policies of racial segregation that foreshadowed apartheid.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary cause of the frontier wars between the Boers and the Xhosa was the competition for**

- A. control of the diamond mines
- B. grazing land and cattle
- C. access to the Indian Ocean ports
- D. religious superiority

**Answer: B.** Both the Boers and the Xhosa were pastoralists (cattle herders), and their expansion led to intense competition over fertile grazing land and water, leading to cattle raids and wars.

**2. The British government introduced the 50th Ordinance in 1828 to**

- A. grant legal equality to the Khoikhoi and other people of colour
- B. force the Voortrekkers to return to the Cape Colony
- C. establish the Orange Free State
- D. declare war on the Xhosa chiefs

**Answer: A.** Influenced by missionaries, the 50th Ordinance removed pass laws and gave "free persons of colour" the same legal rights as white people in the Cape, which angered the Boers.

**3. Which of the following was a major reason why the Boers undertook the Great Trek?**

- A. The discovery of gold in the Transvaal
- B. The invasion of the Cape by the French
- C. The abolition of slavery and British policies of racial equality
- D. The desire to establish trading posts in the interior

**Answer: C.** The Boers left the Cape mainly to escape British administration, which had abolished slavery in 1834 and attempted to enforce equal legal rights for Africans, destroying the Boers' traditional way of life. (Gold was discovered decades later).

**4. The Voortrekkers frequently defended themselves during battles by forming a**

- A. phalanx of spearmen
- B. trench system
- C. laager of ox-wagons
- D. cavalry charge

**Answer: C.** A *laager* was a defensive circle of ox-wagons tied together, which protected the Boers and their families from spears while they fired their rifles from the gaps.

**5. During the Great Trek, the Voortrekkers clashed violently with the Zulu army at the**

- A. Battle of Isandlwana
- B. Battle of Vegkop
- C. Battle of Blood River
- D. Battle of Majuba Hill

**Answer: C.** The Battle of Blood River occurred in December 1838, where a Boer force led by Andries Pretorius defeated the Zulu army sent by King Dingane. (Isandlwana was a later battle between the Zulu and the British).

**6. Which African leader was responsible for the killing of Piet Retief and his party?**

- A. Shaka
- B. Mzilikazi
- C. Moshoeshoe
- D. Dingane

**Answer: D.** King Dingane, who succeeded Shaka, saw the arrival of the Voortrekkers as a threat and ordered the execution of Retief and his men during treaty negotiations.

**7. A significant political consequence of the Great Trek was the**

- A. immediate start of the apartheid system by the British
- B. establishment of the independent Boer republics
- C. unification of all African tribes against the British
- D. withdrawal of the British from the Cape Colony

**Answer: B.** The Great Trek led to the creation of the South African Republic (Transvaal) and the Orange Free State, which the British eventually recognised in the 1850s.

**8. The British initially intervened in the frontier relations between the Boers and the Africans mostly because they**

- A. wanted to steal Boer cattle
- B. wanted to reduce the high financial costs of continuous frontier wars
- C. were secretly allied with the Xhosa chiefs
- D. wanted to expand the colony to the Zambezi river

**Answer: B.** The British wanted a cheap and peaceful colony. The continuous frontier wars were disrupting trade and costing the British government too much money. 

**9. How did the Great Trek affect the indigenous African societies in the interior?**

- A. They gained new modern weapons and defeated the British.
- B. They lost their land and political independence to the Boers.
- C. They formed a permanent military alliance with the Voortrekkers.
- D. They were forcibly relocated to the Cape Colony by the British.

**Answer: B.** Africans whose lands were claimed by the new Boer republics lost their sovereignty and were reduced to providing cheap labour as squatters on white-owned farms. 

**10. The missionaries in the Cape Colony, such as those from the London Missionary Society, played a role in Boer-African relations by**

- A. supplying the Boers with extra guns to fight the Xhosa
- B. translating the Bible into Afrikaans
- C. exposing the cruel treatment of Africans and pressuring Britain for legal reforms
- D. leading the Great Trek into the interior

**Answer: C.** Missionaries like Dr John Philip sent reports to Britain detailing the abuses suffered by the Khoisan and enslaved people, which pushed the British government to introduce laws like the Black Circuit and the 50th Ordinance.
