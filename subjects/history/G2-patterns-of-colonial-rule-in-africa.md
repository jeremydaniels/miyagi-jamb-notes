# JAMB History Notes: Patterns of Colonial Rule in Africa

## Examining the Patterns of Colonial Rule

**Objective i: examine and compare the patterns of colonial rule by the various European powers.**

After the Scramble for Africa and the Berlin Conference (1884–1885), European nations had to set up administrations to govern their new territories. Though their ultimate goals were the same—economic exploitation, resource extraction, and asserting imperial power—they used different methods and philosophies to rule the African people. The administrative styles are broadly grouped into **Indirect Rule** (mostly British), **Direct Rule** (German), and various forms of **Assimilation or Paternalism** (French, Portuguese, Belgian, and Spanish).

## The British Pattern: Indirect Rule

**Content a: The British**

The British Empire in Africa (which included Nigeria, Gold Coast, Sierra Leone, Gambia, Kenya, Uganda, and parts of Southern Africa) primarily used a system called **Indirect Rule**. 

Indirect rule was a system of administration in which the British colonial government ruled the local people through their traditional rulers and established traditional institutions. The British officials acted as "advisers" or "residents," while the day-to-day administration was left to the local chiefs or kings. 

The architect of this system in West Africa was **Lord Frederick Lugard**, who first applied it successfully in Northern Nigeria.

### Features of British Indirect Rule
1. **The Native Authority:** This was the traditional ruler (like an Emir in Northern Nigeria or an Oba in Yorubaland). The ruler was the head of the local administration, passing down orders from the British District Officers to the people.
2. **The Native Treasury:** Taxes were collected locally. A portion was sent to the British colonial government, and the rest was kept in the Native Treasury to pay the traditional ruler, his officials, and fund local projects (like dispensaries or roads).
3. **The Native Courts:** Traditional laws and customs (including Islamic Sharia law in the North) were used to settle disputes, as long as they did not clash with British laws.

### Reasons the British adopted Indirect Rule
- **Shortage of funds:** The British government did not want to spend money from London to run the colonies. The colonies had to pay for themselves.
- **Shortage of personnel:** Britain did not have enough white administrative officers to directly govern millions of Africans across vast territories.
- **Language and cultural barriers:** British officials did not speak the local languages or understand the local customs, making direct communication difficult.
- **Preservation of traditional systems:** Lugard believed that abrupt changes would cause rebellion, so using the existing traditional system would keep the peace.
- **Success in other empires:** The British had successfully used similar systems in parts of India and Fiji.

### Successes and Failures of Indirect Rule
- **Northern Nigeria:** It was highly successful here because the Sokoto Caliphate already had a centralized administration, a recognized system of taxation, and powerful Emirs.
- **Western Nigeria (Yorubaland):** It was partially successful. The Obas were respected, but their power was traditionally limited by checks and balances (like the Oyomesi in Oyo). The British gave the Obas more autocratic power than tradition allowed, which sometimes caused unrest.
- **Eastern Nigeria (Igboland):** It failed completely. Igbo society was largely acephalous (non-centralized) and egalitarian, without supreme kings. The British appointed artificial **"Warrant Chiefs"** who became corrupt and oppressive, leading to the Aba Women's Riot of 1929.
- **Other areas:** It worked well in centralized kingdoms like Buganda (Uganda) but faced resistance in places like Asante (Gold Coast) where the British had previously exiled the king.

## The French Pattern: Assimilation and Association

**Content b: The French**

The French controlled vast territories, especially in West and Equatorial Africa (including Senegal, Mali, Côte d'Ivoire, Dahomey/Benin, and Niger). French colonial policy went through two main phases: **Assimilation** and later **Association**.

### The Policy of Assimilation
The French based their colonial rule on the belief that French culture, language, and civilization were superior. **Assimilation** was the policy aimed at turning Africans into "Black Frenchmen." 

If an African learned to speak fluent French, adopted French culture, converted to Christianity, and reached a certain level of education, they could become an *évolué* (an evolved person) and be granted full French citizenship. 

**Key features of Assimilation (Direct Rule):**
- **Direct Administration:** The French bypassed traditional rulers. The territories were divided into *cercles* (circles or districts) ruled by French commandants. Traditional chiefs were reduced to mere messengers and tax collectors for the French.
- **Centralization:** Laws for the colonies were made in Paris and Dakar (the capital of French West Africa), not locally.
- **Education:** The curriculum was completely French. Students were taught French history, famously reciting "our ancestors the Gauls."
- **The Four Communes of Senegal:** Assimilation was only truly successful in the four coastal towns of Senegal (Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque, and Dakar). Africans born here were granted French citizenship and could even elect a representative to the French parliament in Paris (such as Blaise Diagne, the first African deputy, elected in 1914).
- **The *Indigénat*:** For the vast majority of Africans outside the Four Communes who were not assimilated, life was harsh. They were classified as subjects, not citizens, and were ruled by the *Indigénat*—a brutal legal code that allowed French officials to enforce forced labour, arbitrary arrests, and summary punishments without trial.

### The Shift to Association
By the early 20th century, the French realized that Assimilation was failing and shifted to the policy of **Association**. 

**Reasons for the failure of Assimilation:**
- **Cost:** Educating millions of Africans to become French citizens and giving them equal social benefits was too expensive.
- **Cultural resistance:** Africans, especially Muslims, strongly resisted giving up their religion, culture, and Islamic laws.
- **Political threat:** The French realized that if millions of Africans became voting citizens, they could outvote the white French population in the Paris parliament.

**Features of Association:**
Under Association, the French accepted that Africans had their own culture and stopped trying to make everyone a French citizen. Instead, they sought to "associate" Africans with French colonial goals. However, unlike British indirect rule, the French still did not rule *through* traditional institutions. African chiefs were still subordinate employees of the French government, rather than respected native authorities.

## The Portuguese Pattern: Indigenato and Assimilado

**Content c: The Portuguese**

Portugal was the first European nation to arrive in Africa and the last to leave. Its main colonies were Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau, Cape Verde, and São Tomé and Príncipe.

### Philosophy: Overseas Provinces
The Portuguese did not legally consider their African territories as colonies, but as **Overseas Provinces** (*Ultramar*) of Portugal. Their stated aim was a "civilizing mission" (*missão civilizadora*).

### The Assimilado System (Indigenato)
Like the French, the Portuguese used a system of cultural assimilation. The population was strictly divided into two legal classes:

1. **The *Assimilados* (Citizens):** To become an *assimilado*, an African had to prove they could read and write fluent Portuguese, had abandoned African customs, lived a "civilized" European lifestyle, and earned a regular income. *Assimilados* were granted Portuguese citizenship and exempted from forced labour. However, the test was extremely strict, and less than 1% of the African population ever achieved this status.
2. **The *Indígenas* (Subjects):** The unassimilated vast majority were ruled under the *Indigenato* system. They had no political rights, could not vote, were subjected to strict curfews, and were forced to carry passbooks. 

### Brutality and Forced Labour (*Chibalo*)
Portuguese rule was extremely harsh. Under the authoritarian dictatorship of António Salazar in Portugal (from 1932 to 1968), the colonies were squeezed for maximum profit. The most infamous feature was ***chibalo*** (forced labour). *Indígenas* were forced to work on European-owned cotton and coffee plantations, mines, and public works for little or no pay. 

Because Portugal was a poor country and refused to grant independence in the 1960s when other European powers did, their rigid and brutal system eventually led to long, bloody wars of liberation in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea-Bissau.

## The Belgian Pattern: Paternalism

**Content d: The Belgians**

Belgium’s main colony was the Belgian Congo (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo).

### Background: The Congo Free State
Initially, from 1885 to 1908, the Congo was not a colony of Belgium but the personal, private property of King Leopold II, called the **Congo Free State**. This period was marked by unimaginable brutality; Africans were killed, mutilated (hands chopped off), and worked to death to extract wild rubber. After an international outcry over the atrocities, the Belgian government took control from the king in 1908, creating the Belgian Congo.

### Philosophy: Paternalism
The official policy of the Belgian government was **Paternalism** (treating Africans like children). The Belgians believed that Africans were like children who needed to be guided, fed, and disciplined, but should not be given any adult responsibilities or political rights. Their motto was "dominate to serve" (*dominer pour servir*).

### Features of Belgian Rule
- **The "Unholy Trinity":** The Congo was ruled strictly by an alliance of three powers: the Belgian State (administration), the Catholic Church (education and morals), and big foreign corporations (mining).
- **Economic Exploitation:** The Congo was incredibly rich in minerals (copper, diamonds, uranium). The focus was on extracting this wealth using African labour.
- **Education for Work, not Politics:** The Catholic Church provided widespread primary education and vocational training to make Africans good carpenters, mechanics, and miners. However, **higher education was strictly blocked**. The Belgians did not want a class of educated elites (like lawyers or journalists) who might demand political rights.
- **No Political Rights:** Until the late 1950s, no African in the Congo was allowed to vote or participate in government. 

Because the Belgians actively prevented the creation of an educated African leadership, when they suddenly granted independence in 1960 to avoid a colonial war, the Congo fell into immediate political chaos (the Congo Crisis).

## The German Pattern: Direct and Militaristic Rule

**Content e: The Germans**

Germany arrived late to the Scramble for Africa. Its colonies included Togo, Kamerun (Cameroon), German East Africa (Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi), and German South-West Africa (Namibia). 

**Note on textbook dates:** German rule was the shortest of all the major powers. It lasted only 30 years, from 1884 until the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. After Germany lost the war in 1918, the League of Nations took away all its colonies and handed them to Britain, France, and South Africa to manage as "mandates."

### Features of German Rule
- **Direct and Authoritarian:** German rule was heavily militaristic. The colonies were run by military officers and bureaucrats using direct rule, often ignoring or deposing traditional chiefs who did not fully submit. (However, in some remote areas like northern Cameroon and Rwanda-Burundi, they used a limited form of indirect rule out of necessity).
- **Plantations and Infrastructure:** The Germans focused heavily on economic development for the mother country. They built impressive railways, roads, and plantations (e.g., in Cameroon and Togo) very quickly, but this was achieved using brutal forced labour.
- **Harsh Retaliation:** Any resistance was met with extreme violence. When the Herero and Nama people rebelled in German South-West Africa (1904–1907), the German military committed the first genocide of the 20th century, driving them into the desert to starve and putting survivors in concentration camps. Similarly, the Maji Maji rebellion in German East Africa (1905–1907) was crushed by deliberately destroying crops and creating a famine.

## The Spanish Pattern: Minimalist Paternalism

**Content f: The Spanish**

Spain’s African colonies were small: Equatorial Guinea (Rio Muni on the mainland and the island of Fernando Po), Spanish Sahara (Western Sahara), and a small strip of northern Morocco.

### Features of Spanish Rule
- **Low Investment:** Spain was not as wealthy or industrialized as Britain or France, so it invested very little in developing its African territories. 
- **Paternalism and the *Emancipados*:** Similar to Portugal, Spain used a highly paternalistic system. They treated Africans as minors who needed the protection of the Catholic Church. They had a small-scale assimilation policy where an African could become an *emancipado* (an emancipated person) with full rights, but very few achieved this.
- **Plantation Economy:** In Equatorial Guinea (especially Fernando Po), the Spanish set up large cocoa plantations. Because the local Bubi people resisted forced labour, the Spanish relied heavily on imported labour, particularly migrant workers from Eastern Nigeria (Igboland) and Liberia, treating them very poorly.
- **Direct Administration:** The Spanish used direct rule, relying heavily on Catholic missionaries to run schools, hospitals, and civilize the local population according to Spanish Catholic values.

## Comparing the Colonial Systems

**Objective i: examine and compare the patterns of colonial rule by the various European powers.**

### Table 1: The Classic Comparison (British Indirect Rule vs. French Assimilation)

This is the most common comparison in JAMB exams.

| Feature | British Indirect Rule | French Assimilation / Direct Rule |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Philosophy** | Preservation of African culture; separate development. | Destruction of African culture; making Africans into French people. |
| **Role of Chiefs** | Paramount. Chiefs were powerful leaders of the Native Authority. | Minimal. Chiefs were just messengers and tax collectors for the French. |
| **Administration** | Decentralized. Laws adapted to local customs (e.g., Native Courts). | Highly centralized in Paris/Dakar. Uniform French laws everywhere. |
| **Education** | Taught in local languages first. Respected local traditions. | Exclusively in French. Taught French history and culture. |
| **Legal Status** | Africans were British "subjects," ruled by local customary law. | Unassimilated Africans faced the harsh *Indigénat* law. |

### Table 2: Summary of all European Patterns

| Colonial Power | Main Policy / Philosophy | View of Traditional Rulers | Key Characteristics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Britain** | Indirect Rule | Highly respected and used to govern (except where absent, e.g., Igboland). | Native Authority, Native Treasury, cheaper to run, preserved local culture. |
| **France** | Assimilation, later Association | Ignored or reduced to junior civil servants. | Centralized, direct administration, aimed to create Black Frenchmen, strict *Indigénat* code. |
| **Portugal** | Assimilado / Overseas Provinces | Ignored. Replaced by direct Portuguese administration. | Extremely brutal, *chibalo* (forced labour), delayed independence leading to armed struggles. |
| **Belgium** | Paternalism | Ignored. | State + Church + Business alliance. Provided primary education but strictly blocked higher education and political rights. |
| **Germany** | Direct / Militaristic | Mostly deposed if they resisted. | Very short duration (lost after WWI), brutal suppression of revolts, forced labour on plantations. |
| **Spain** | Paternalism | Bypassed. | Minimal investment, reliance on Catholic missions, plantation economy using migrant labour. |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Indirect Rule** | British system of governing colonies through existing traditional rulers and institutions. |
| **Assimilation** | French policy of forcing Africans to adopt French language, culture, and identity to become citizens. |
| **Association** | Later French policy that respected African culture but still tied the colonies firmly to French administrative control. |
| **Paternalism** | The Belgian and Spanish policy of treating Africans like children who need care and discipline but no political rights. |
| **Warrant Chiefs** | Artificial chiefs appointed by the British in acephalous societies like Igboland, which led to corruption and riots. |
| **Four Communes** | The four towns in Senegal (Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque, Dakar) where French assimilation was actually successful. |
| **Indigénat** | Harsh French legal code used to discipline, tax, and force labour from unassimilated Africans. |
| **Assimilado** | An African in Portuguese colonies who had adopted Portuguese culture and earned citizenship rights. |
| **Indigenato (Portugal)** | The system under which the vast majority of unassimilated Africans were ruled in Portuguese colonies. |
| **Chibalo** | The brutal system of forced labour in Portuguese colonies. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mistaking Association for Indirect Rule:** A very common mistake. When the French shifted to Association, they still did *not* use traditional rulers the way the British did. French chiefs remained powerless civil servants, unlike British Emirs or Obas.
- **The Architect of Indirect Rule:** Always remember Lord Lugard introduced Indirect Rule in West Africa, starting in Northern Nigeria.
- **Igbo Warrant Chiefs:** Know that Indirect Rule failed in Eastern Nigeria because there were no central kings, so the British created "Warrant Chiefs," causing the Aba Women's Riot.
- **Assimilation's Success:** Assimilation was only truly implemented in the Four Communes of Senegal. Everywhere else, it was largely a failure because of cost and cultural resistance.
- **German Rule duration:** Remember that Germany lost its colonies in 1918 after World War I. They did not decolonize in the 1960s like the others.
- **Belgian Policy:** Connect Belgium to "Paternalism," the Congo, and the refusal to allow higher education, which caused chaos at independence.

## Quick summary

1. The British used **Indirect Rule**, governing through traditional chiefs (Native Authority) to save money and personnel, and preserving local culture.
2. Indirect rule was highly successful in Northern Nigeria, partially successful in the West, and failed completely in the East (Warrant Chiefs).
3. The French initially used **Assimilation** (making Africans into Frenchmen), using Direct Rule and centralizing power in Paris and Dakar. 
4. Because Assimilation was too expensive and Africans resisted cultural destruction, the French changed to **Association**, though they still did not give traditional rulers real power.
5. The Portuguese ruled their colonies as overseas provinces, dividing people into a tiny elite of **Assimilados** and a vast majority of subjects subjected to forced labour (*chibalo*).
6. The Belgians applied **Paternalism** in the Congo, providing basic needs and primary education but strictly banning higher education and political rights.
7. German rule was highly militaristic, relied on direct rule and forced labour, and ended abruptly after World War I.
8. Spanish rule was characterized by minimal investment, heavy reliance on Catholic missionaries, and paternalism.

## Practice questions

**1. The primary reason the British adopted the system of Indirect Rule in West Africa was to:**
- A. rapidly westernize the local population
- B. solve the problem of scarce funds and personnel
- C. prepare the colonies for immediate independence
- D. destroy the powers of the traditional rulers

**Answer: B.** The British used Indirect Rule because they lacked the money and the white administrative officers needed to govern vast African territories directly.

**2. Which of the following is a major difference between British Indirect Rule and French Assimilation?**
- A. The British used direct rule, while the French used traditional rulers.
- B. The British relied on traditional chiefs, while the French centralized administration and bypassed chiefs.
- C. The British forced Africans to speak English, while the French preserved local languages.
- D. The British gave Africans full citizenship, while the French did not.

**Answer: B.** The core difference is that British Indirect Rule elevated traditional rulers, while French Direct Rule (Assimilation) bypassed them in favour of centralized French administration.

**3. The policy of Assimilation was only genuinely successful in which part of French West Africa?**
- A. The interior of Mali
- B. The coastal towns of Dahomey
- C. The Four Communes of Senegal
- D. The forest regions of Côte d'Ivoire

**Answer: C.** Assimilation was largely restricted to the Four Communes of Senegal (Dakar, Gorée, Rufisque, Saint-Louis), where Africans actually received French citizenship.

**4. Under which colonial system were the colonies regarded strictly as "Overseas Provinces" of the mother country?**
- A. The British system
- B. The Belgian system
- C. The German system
- D. The Portuguese system

**Answer: D.** Portugal officially considered its colonies (like Angola and Mozambique) as *Ultramar* (Overseas Provinces), not separate colonies.

**5. Why did the British system of Indirect Rule fail in Eastern Nigeria?**
- A. The people were already heavily assimilated into French culture.
- B. The society was acephalous and lacked traditional centralized kings.
- C. The area was heavily influenced by Islamic law.
- D. Lord Lugard refused to implement it there.

**Answer: B.** Igbo society was non-centralized (acephalous). Because there were no existing kings to rule through, the British created "Warrant Chiefs," which the people rejected.

**6. The colonial policy that deliberately restricted higher education to prevent the rise of an African political elite was used by the:**
- A. French in Senegal
- B. British in Gold Coast
- C. Belgians in the Congo
- D. Spanish in Equatorial Guinea

**Answer: C.** The Belgians used Paternalism in the Congo, providing primary and vocational education but strictly blocking higher education so Africans would not demand political rights.

**7. Why did the French eventually abandon Assimilation in favour of Association?**
- A. Assimilation was too expensive and faced strong cultural resistance.
- B. The British forced them to change their policy after World War I.
- C. All Africans had successfully become French citizens.
- D. Traditional rulers in the colonies demanded more power.

**Answer: A.** The French realized it was financially impossible to turn millions of Africans into equal French citizens, and many Africans (especially Muslims) resisted losing their culture.

**8. The legal code used by the French to impose forced labour and arbitrary punishment on unassimilated Africans was known as the:**
- A. Indigenato
- B. Assimilado
- C. Indigénat
- D. Chibalo

**Answer: C.** The *Indigénat* was the harsh French legal code for subjects. (Note: *Indigenato* and *chibalo* are Portuguese terms).

**9. Which European power lost all its African colonies at the end of the First World War?**
- A. Spain
- B. Portugal
- C. Germany
- D. Belgium

**Answer: C.** Germany lost its colonies (Togo, Kamerun, German East Africa, and German South-West Africa) in 1918 after WWI, and they were handed over to the League of Nations.

**10. What was the position of a traditional chief under the French colonial administration?**
- A. He was the sovereign ruler of his people with full legal authority.
- B. He acted as an equal partner to the French District Officer.
- C. He was highly respected and ran the Native Treasury.
- D. He was reduced to a subordinate civil servant used mainly to collect taxes.

**Answer: D.** Unlike the British system, the French did not respect traditional authority. Chiefs were just low-level employees used to pass on French orders and collect taxes.
