## Identifying the various types of drama

**Objective a.i: identify the various types of drama.**

Drama is a genre of literature meant to be performed on a stage in front of an audience. Over the centuries, playwrights have developed different categories (or **genres**) of drama based on the mood of the play, the types of characters involved, and how the story ends. 

To identify the type of a drama, you must look at three main clues:
1. **The tone and mood:** Is the play serious and dark, or lighthearted and funny? Does it exaggerate emotions?
2. **The characters:** Are they noble heroes facing serious dilemmas, everyday people in funny situations, or exaggerated stereotypes (like the pure hero and the pure villain)?
3. **The resolution (ending):** Does the play end in death and disaster, or does it end in a happy celebration (like a wedding)? 

By examining these three elements, you can group any play into one of the major types of drama: **Tragedy**, **Comedy**, **Tragicomedy**, **Melodrama**, **Farce**, or **Opera**.

## Analysing the contents of the various types of drama

**Objective a.ii: analyse the contents of the various types of drama.** (This also covers **Content item a**)

Below is a detailed analysis of the contents, features, and examples of each type of drama listed in the JAMB syllabus.

### 1. Tragedy

A **tragedy** is a serious play that ends in disaster, sorrow, or death for the main character. It deals with dark themes like human suffering, fate, betrayal, and mortality. The purpose of a tragedy is to make the audience feel pity and fear, eventually leaving them with a sense of emotional release (a process the Greek philosopher Aristotle called **catharsis**).

**Key contents and features of a Tragedy:**
*   **The Tragic Hero:** The main character is usually someone of high status or noble birth (like a king, a prince, or a great warrior). They are not entirely good or entirely bad, but they are great enough that their fall affects a whole society.
*   **Hamartia (Tragic Flaw):** The hero has a fundamental character weakness, such as excessive pride (**hubris**), greed, jealousy, or ambition, which causes them to make a fatal mistake.
*   **The Reversal of Fortune (Peripeteia):** The hero starts the play in a high, successful position but rapidly falls into ruin, misery, or madness because of their tragic flaw.
*   **Disastrous Ending:** The play usually ends with the death of the hero and often several other characters. 

**Examples:** 
*   William Shakespeare's *Macbeth* (ambition leads to his downfall), *Othello* (jealousy is his tragic flaw), and *Romeo and Juliet*.
*   J.P. Clark's *Song of a Goat* (a Nigerian tragedy where Zifa's pride leads to the destruction of his family).
*   Wole Soyinka's *Death and the King's Horseman*.

### 2. Comedy

A **comedy** is a lighthearted, amusing play that aims to make the audience laugh. It deals with everyday human problems and always has a happy ending, usually resolving in harmony, forgiveness, or a marriage.

**Key contents and features of a Comedy:**
*   **Ordinary Characters:** The characters are usually everyday people with common flaws (like foolishness, vanity, or greed) rather than noble kings.
*   **Comic Situations:** The plot often relies on misunderstandings, mistaken identities, deceptions, and witty dialogue. 
*   **Satire and Social Correction:** Many comedies mock the foolish behaviors of society to correct them. This is called satire.
*   **Happy Ending:** No matter how complicated the problems get, everything is happily resolved by the end. The "good" characters are rewarded, and the foolish ones are mildly punished or made to see their errors.

**Examples:**
*   William Shakespeare's *A Midsummer Night's Dream*.
*   Wole Soyinka's *The Lion and the Jewel* (a comic play that satirises the clash between modern and traditional ways through the characters of Lakunle and Baroka).

### 3. Tragicomedy

A **tragicomedy** is a play that blends elements of both tragedy and comedy. It contains serious, potentially disastrous situations that make it look like a tragedy, but it ultimately avoids disaster and ends on a happy or bittersweet note.

**Key contents and features of a Tragicomedy:**
*   **High Stakes:** The characters face severe threats (like death or financial ruin), creating a serious mood for most of the play.
*   **Comic Relief:** The serious plot is interrupted by funny moments, jokes, or clownish characters that ease the tension.
*   **Averted Disaster:** Just when it seems a tragic ending is certain, a sudden twist, a discovery, or an act of mercy changes the direction, leading to a peaceful resolution.

**Examples:** 
*   William Shakespeare's *The Merchant of Venice* (Antonio is seconds away from being killed, but he is saved by a clever legal argument, ending the play happily).

### 4. Melodrama

A **melodrama** is an overly dramatic play that appeals strongly to the audience's emotions rather than their logic. Historically, melodramas featured background music to heighten the emotional impact (the word comes from *melo* meaning music, and *drama*).

**Key contents and features of a Melodrama:**
*   **Stereotypical Characters:** The characters are "flat" and do not change. There is always a purely wicked **villain**, a brave and flawless **hero**, and an innocent **damsel in distress** (a victim).
*   **Sensational Plot:** The plot is highly exaggerated, featuring kidnappings, murders, secret documents, and miraculous rescues. The events are often highly unlikely or illogical.
*   **Poetic Justice:** In a melodrama, good always triumphs completely, and evil is totally punished. The audience is left with a strong sense of moral satisfaction.

**Examples:** 
*   Many early Nollywood films follow a strong melodramatic structure (e.g., a pure, suffering orphan is abused by an entirely wicked stepmother, but in the end, the stepmother confesses her crimes and goes mad, while the orphan marries a prince).

### 5. Farce

A **farce** is a highly exaggerated, ridiculous sub-genre of comedy. Its only goal is to make the audience laugh as hard as possible, without carrying any deep moral or social message.

**Key contents and features of a Farce:**
*   **Absurd Situations:** The plot relies on highly improbable situations and incredible coincidences.
*   **Physical Humor (Slapstick):** A lot of the humor comes from physical actions—people falling over, hiding in cupboards, running in and out of doors, or chasing each other across the stage.
*   **Fast-Paced Action:** The timing of the play is extremely fast, with characters constantly entering and exiting just in time to miss each other.
*   **Mistaken Identity:** Characters frequently disguise themselves or are mistaken for someone else, leading to total chaos.

**Examples:**
*   Oscar Wilde's *The Importance of Being Earnest* contains many farcical elements.
*   Many modern sitcoms and short comic stage sketches rely on farce.

### 6. Opera etc.

An **opera** is a form of drama where the characters sing their parts rather than speaking them. It is a theatrical art form that combines a dramatic script with continuous or nearly continuous musical scores.

**Key contents and features of an Opera:**
*   **Libretto and Score:** The written text of the opera (the words) is called the **libretto**, while the music is the **score**.
*   **Arias and Recitatives:** When a character sings a highly emotional solo song, it is called an **aria**. When they sing in a way that sounds like natural speech to move the plot forward, it is called **recitative**.
*   **Spectacle:** Operas are usually massive productions featuring large orchestras, huge choruses, elaborate costumes, and grand scenery.

**Note on "etc.":** Other musical dramatic forms include the **Operetta** (a short, lighthearted opera with some spoken dialogue) and the **Musical** (a modern play where spoken dialogue is mixed with songs and dance routines). 

**Examples:**
*   Classical European operas like Verdi's *Aida*.
*   In Nigeria, the late Hubert Ogunde pioneered the **Yoruba Folk Opera**, which blended traditional African music, dance, and dramatic storytelling.

## Comparing and contrasting the features of different types of drama

**Objective a.iii: compare and contrast the features of different types of drama.**

JAMB often tests your ability to distinguish between genres that seem similar. Use the tables below to compare and contrast their features.

### Table 1: Tragedy vs. Comedy

| Feature | Tragedy | Comedy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Tone/Mood** | Serious, dark, and heavy. | Lighthearted, humorous, and amusing. |
| **Ending** | Disastrous (ends in death, madness, or ruin). | Happy (ends in marriage, reconciliation, or a feast). |
| **Main Character** | A noble, great person (e.g., a king) who has a fatal flaw. | An ordinary person with relatable, everyday flaws. |
| **Audience Reaction** | Feels pity, fear, and eventually emotional release (catharsis). | Feels amusement, joy, and laughter. |
| **Themes** | Fate, death, ambition, human suffering. | Love, mistaken identity, societal foolishness. |

### Table 2: Tragedy vs. Melodrama

*Both involve serious, high-stakes plots, but they are treated very differently.*

| Feature | Tragedy | Melodrama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Character Depth** | Characters are complex; the hero is mostly good but makes a fatal mistake. | Characters are flat stereotypes; the hero is perfectly good, the villain is perfectly evil. |
| **Cause of Conflict** | The hero's internal flaw (hamartia) causes their own downfall. | External forces (a wicked villain or bad luck) cause the hero's suffering. |
| **Plot Realism** | The plot follows a logical, inevitable sequence of cause and effect. | The plot is exaggerated, highly unlikely, and relies heavily on coincidence. |
| **Ending / Justice** | Unfair or tragic; the punishment often outweighs the crime. | Perfect poetic justice; good always wins, and evil is completely defeated. |

### Table 3: Comedy vs. Farce

*Farce is a sub-genre of comedy, but they differ in how they create laughter.*

| Feature | Comedy | Farce |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source of Humor** | Witty dialogue, character quirks, and satire. | Physical comedy (slapstick), running around, hiding. |
| **Believability** | The situations could happen in real life, even if they are exaggerated. | The situations are completely absurd, highly improbable, and chaotic. |
| **Purpose** | Often aims to correct society by mocking foolish behavior. | Has no serious message; exists purely to make the audience laugh. |

### Table 4: Tragicomedy compared to Tragedy and Comedy

| Type | How it compares |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Tragicomedy** | Like **tragedy**, it features serious threats (death, ruin) and high tension. However, like **comedy**, the disaster is averted at the last minute, and it ends happily or peacefully. |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Tragedy** | A serious play that ends in disaster or death for the main character. |
| **Comedy** | A lighthearted play that aims to amuse and always ends happily. |
| **Tragicomedy** | A play that mixes serious, dangerous situations with a happy or averted ending. |
| **Melodrama** | An exaggerated, emotional play with stereotypical heroes and villains, ending in poetic justice. |
| **Farce** | A fast-paced, highly exaggerated comedy relying on physical humor and absurd situations. |
| **Opera** | A dramatic work where all or most of the dialogue is sung to orchestral music. |
| **Hamartia** | The tragic flaw or character weakness that causes a hero's downfall. |
| **Hubris** | Excessive pride or arrogance (a very common tragic flaw). |
| **Catharsis** | The purging of pity and fear that the audience feels at the end of a tragedy. |
| **Poetic Justice** | A literary outcome where good is ultimately rewarded and evil is punished. |
| **Slapstick** | Physical, clumsy, or aggressive humor commonly used in farce. |
| **Libretto** | The written text (the words) of an opera. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Don't confuse Melodrama with Tragedy.** In JAMB, if a question mentions "flat characters", "exaggerated emotion", or "a clear division between good and evil", the answer is **Melodrama**, not Tragedy. Tragedy involves a complex hero who brings about their *own* downfall.
*   **Don't confuse Farce with Comedy.** If the question emphasizes "highly improbable situations," "absurdity," or "horseplay/physical humor," the exact answer is **Farce**.
*   **Remember the terms for Tragedy.** JAMB frequently tests terms related to tragedy. Remember that *Hamartia* = Tragic Flaw, and *Catharsis* = Emotional purging/cleansing.
*   **Opera means singing.** If a question describes a play where the dialogue is predominantly sung to musical accompaniment, it is an Opera. Do not confuse it with a standard play that just happens to have a song in it. 
*   **Read the ending.** The defining difference between a Tragedy and a Tragicomedy is the ending. If a play has murders, ghosts, and battles but ends with the hero successfully becoming king and everyone celebrating, it is a Tragicomedy (or Romance), not a Tragedy.

## Quick summary

1. Drama is divided into different types (genres) based on tone, character types, and how the play ends.
2. **Tragedy** is a serious play where a noble hero's tragic flaw leads them to a disastrous end, creating catharsis for the audience.
3. **Comedy** is an amusing, lighthearted play featuring ordinary people, which ends happily, often in marriage.
4. **Tragicomedy** combines the serious, high-stakes danger of a tragedy with the happy, resolved ending of a comedy.
5. **Melodrama** relies on exaggerated emotions, stereotypical characters (hero vs. villain), and ends with strict poetic justice.
6. **Farce** is a ridiculous, fast-paced comedy that relies on physical humor, mistaken identity, and highly unlikely situations.
7. **Opera** is a form of drama where the text (libretto) is entirely or mostly sung to the accompaniment of music.

## Practice questions

**1. A dramatic work that evokes pity and fear and ends in disaster for the protagonist is known as a**
- A. farce
- B. comedy
- C. melodrama
- D. tragedy

**Answer: D.** A tragedy is defined by its serious tone, disastrous ending for the hero, and its ability to evoke pity and fear (catharsis) in the audience.

**2. Which of the following is a primary characteristic of a farce?**
- A. A noble hero with a fatal flaw
- B. Highly improbable situations and physical humor
- C. Continuous singing instead of speaking
- D. A serious tone with an unhappy ending

**Answer: B.** A farce is a sub-genre of comedy characterized by absurd, highly improbable situations, fast-paced action, and physical (slapstick) humor.

**3. In drama, the purging of the audience's emotions, particularly pity and fear, is called**
- A. hamartia
- B. peripeteia
- C. catharsis
- D. hubris

**Answer: C.** Catharsis is the emotional release or cleansing that the audience experiences after watching a tragedy.

**4. A play that features flat characters, exaggerated emotions, and clear poetic justice is best described as a**
- A. melodrama
- B. tragicomedy
- C. farce
- D. comedy

**Answer: A.** Melodrama is noted for its highly exaggerated plots, flat stereotypical characters (hero vs. villain), and endings where good always completely triumphs over evil (poetic justice).

**5. The text containing the words of an opera is referred to as the**
- A. score
- B. libretto
- C. aria
- D. script

**Answer: B.** The libretto is the written text of an opera, while the score refers to the written music.

**6. Which of the following best differentiates a tragicomedy from a tragedy?**
- A. A tragicomedy lacks a clear protagonist.
- B. A tragicomedy includes physical slapstick humor.
- C. A tragicomedy averts disaster and ends happily.
- D. A tragicomedy is meant to be sung.

**Answer: C.** While a tragicomedy contains serious, threatening situations similar to a tragedy, the expected disaster is avoided, and the play ends happily or peacefully.

**7. A common weakness or flaw in a tragic hero that leads to their downfall is called**
- A. catharsis
- B. hubris
- C. hamartia
- D. climax

**Answer: C.** Hamartia is the Greek term for the tragic flaw or error in judgment that causes the hero's ruin. Hubris (excessive pride) is just one specific type of hamartia. 

**8. The primary purpose of satire in a comedy is to**
- A. make the audience cry
- B. mock and correct foolish social behaviors
- C. provide background music for the actors
- D. create purely physical humor

**Answer: B.** Satirical comedies use humor to mock human weaknesses or societal flaws in order to highlight them and encourage correction.

**9. Which of the following genres strictly requires the dialogue to be sung to musical accompaniment?**
- A. Opera
- B. Tragicomedy
- C. Melodrama
- D. Farce

**Answer: A.** Opera is a theatrical form where the dramatic text is entirely or predominantly sung, rather than spoken. 

**10. When a play begins in a state of confusion and misunderstanding among ordinary people but resolves in harmony and marriage, it is most likely a**
- A. tragedy
- B. comedy
- C. melodrama
- D. tragicomedy

**Answer: B.** Comedies focus on ordinary characters facing everyday confusion or obstacles, and they characteristically end happily, very often with a wedding or a feast.
