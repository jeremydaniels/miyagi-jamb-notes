## Narrative Techniques: Point of View

**Content b.i: Point of view (Omniscient, First Person, Second person, Third person, Stream of consciousness, Epiphany)**

**Point of view (POV)** is the perspective or "lens" from which a story is told. It determines who the narrator is and how much they know about the events and the other characters' thoughts.

### 1. First Person Point of View
In the first-person point of view, the narrator is a character *inside* the story. 
*   **Pronouns used:** "I", "me", "my", "we", "us".
*   **Effect:** It makes the story feel very intimate, personal, and real because the reader is inside the narrator's head.
*   **Limitation:** The reader only knows what the narrator knows, sees, or feels. If the narrator is hiding something or does not understand what is happening, the reader might be misled. This is called an **unreliable narrator**.
*   **Example (made up for teaching):** *"I walked into the Lagos market and held my bag tightly. I didn't trust the man smiling at me from the yam stall."*

### 2. Second Person Point of View
In the second-person point of view, the narrator speaks directly to the reader, treating the reader as the main character.
*   **Pronouns used:** "You", "your".
*   **Effect:** It pulls the reader directly into the action, making them feel responsible for the choices made in the story. It is very rare in novels, but sometimes used in short stories or self-help books.
*   **Example (made up for teaching):** *"You walk into the room. You see the letter on the table, and your heart skips a beat. What do you do next?"*

### 3. Third Person Point of View
In the third-person point of view, the narrator is an outside observer who is *not* a character in the story.
*   **Pronouns used:** "He", "she", "it", "they", and characters' names (e.g., "Chike", "Ngozi").
*   **Third Person Limited:** The narrator only knows the thoughts and feelings of **one** character. The reader follows the story through that single character's eyes, even though it is told from the outside.
*   **Example (made up for teaching):** *"Amina stared at the test paper. She felt a wave of panic and wondered if the teacher could see her shaking."*

### 4. Omniscient Point of View
The word "omniscient" means "all-knowing". This is a specific type of third-person narration where the narrator knows the thoughts, feelings, and pasts of **all** the characters in the story.
*   **Effect:** The narrator can jump from one character's mind to another's (sometimes called "head-hopping"). The reader gets a "God-like" overview of everything happening.
*   **Example (made up for teaching):** *"Amina stared at the test paper in panic. Across the room, Mr. Ade noticed her shaking and felt sorry for her, wishing he had made the questions easier."* (Here, the narrator knows both Amina's and Mr. Ade's thoughts).

### 5. Stream of Consciousness
Stream of consciousness is a narrative technique that tries to capture the continuous, unfiltered flow of a character's thoughts and feelings as they happen.
*   **Features:** It often ignores standard punctuation or grammar. Thoughts jump quickly from one idea to another without clear connections, just like human minds do in real life.
*   **Textbook variation:** As noted, JAMB categorises this under "Point of view", but most literary scholars define it as a stylistic narrative technique often used *within* first-person or third-person limited POVs.
*   **Example (made up for teaching):** *"The bus is late again always late like that time in Abuja yellow buses moving heat rising why did I wear this heavy shirt I need water..."*

### 6. Epiphany
An epiphany is a sudden, powerful moment of realisation or a "flash of insight" experienced by a character, which changes their understanding of themselves or their situation completely.
*   **Textbook variation:** While JAMB lists this under Point of view, textbooks classify an epiphany as a plot device or a stage in character development.
*   **Example (made up for teaching):** *After years of chasing wealth in the city, Emeka looked at the lonely, empty mansion he had built. In a sudden flash of clarity, he realised that the money meant nothing without his family to share it with.*

## Identifying Narrative Techniques
**Objective b.i: identify the narrative techniques used in each of the prescribed texts.**

When you study your prescribed prose texts (*So the Path Does not Die*, *Redemption Road*, *Path of Lucas*), you must ask yourself:
1.  **Who is telling the story?** Is it a character inside the story ("I") or a voice outside it ("he/she")?
2.  **How much does the narrator know?** Do they know everyone's thoughts (omniscient), or only one person's (limited)?
3.  **Are there moments of sudden realisation?** Look for key plot points where a character experiences an epiphany.

## Characterisation

**Content b.ii: Characterisation (Round, flat, foil, hero, antihero, villain, heroine, etc.)**

**Characterisation** is the method an author uses to create, develop, and reveal the personality of characters in a story. 
*   **Direct characterisation:** The author directly tells the reader what the character is like (e.g., *"Kunle was a wicked, greedy man"*).
*   **Indirect characterisation:** The author shows the reader what the character is like through their actions, speech, appearance, and how other characters react to them (e.g., *"Kunle snatched the last piece of bread from the crying child and laughed"*).

**Objective b.iii: distinguish between one type of character from another.**

### Types of Characters

1.  **Hero / Heroine:** 
    *   The **hero** (male) or **heroine** (female) is the main character who possesses noble qualities like courage, morality, and strength. They fight for good. 
    *   *(Note: The broader term for the main character is the **protagonist**, who doesn't necessarily have to be perfectly "good".)*
2.  **Villain:** 
    *   The main bad character who works against the hero. They are usually malicious, selfish, or evil.
    *   *(Note: The broader term for whoever opposes the protagonist is the **antagonist**.)*
3.  **Antihero:** 
    *   A central character (protagonist) who lacks traditional heroic qualities. An antihero might be cowardly, lazy, cynical, or morally flawed, but the reader still follows their journey and often sympathises with them.
4.  **Flat Character:** 
    *   A simple, one-dimensional character with only one or two personality traits. They do not undergo any significant growth or change during the story. (Also known as a **static** character).
5.  **Round Character:** 
    *   A complex, fully developed, multi-dimensional character. They have flaws, conflicting emotions, and they change or grow as the story progresses. (Also known as a **dynamic** character).
6.  **Foil:** 
    *   A character whose traits deliberately contrast with the traits of another character (usually the protagonist). The author uses a foil to highlight and emphasise the qualities of the main character. For example, if the hero is very patient, their foil will be extremely hot-tempered.

### Comparison Tables for Characters

**Comparing Hero and Antihero**

| Feature | Hero / Heroine | Antihero |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Morality** | Highly moral, fights for what is right. | Morally ambiguous; may do bad things for good reasons, or good things for selfish reasons. |
| **Qualities** | Brave, selfless, strong, noble. | Can be cowardly, clumsy, selfish, or flawed. |
| **Role in Story** | The main character (protagonist). | The main character (protagonist). |
| **Example trait** | Risks their life to save a stranger. | Saves a stranger only because they owe them money. |

**Comparing Flat and Round Characters**

| Feature | Flat (Static) Character | Round (Dynamic) Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Complexity** | Simple, one-dimensional. | Complex, multi-dimensional. |
| **Development** | Remains the same from the beginning to the end of the story. | Grows, changes, or learns a lesson as a result of the story's events. |
| **Purpose** | To fill a background role or represent a single idea (e.g., the strict teacher, the funny friend). | To seem like a real, believable human being whom the reader cares about. |

## Language, Grammar, and Diction

**Content b.iii: Language**
**Objective b.iv: Grammar, diction and clarity of expression.**

The way an author uses language is a vital part of storytelling. Authors do not just choose words randomly; they carefully select their language to fit the characters, the setting, and the mood of the story.

### 1. Diction (Word Choice)
**Diction** refers to the author's choice of words. Diction can be:
*   **Formal:** Elevated, serious, and using complex vocabulary (often used for serious non-fiction or historical novels).
*   **Informal / Colloquial:** Everyday, relaxed language (e.g., using "gonna" or "kid").
*   **Slang / Pidgin:** In many African novels, authors use Pidgin English or local slang to make the dialogue realistic and to show a character's social class or background.
*   **Concrete vs. Abstract:** Concrete diction uses specific, physical words (e.g., "a red plastic chair"), while abstract diction uses ideas and feelings (e.g., "love", "freedom").

### 2. Grammar and Syntax
**Grammar** and **Syntax** refer to how words are arranged into sentences. An author can manipulate grammar for special effects:
*   **Short, fragmented sentences:** Create a feeling of speed, panic, or tension. (e.g., *"He ran. The footsteps followed. Closer. Closer still."*)
*   **Long, flowing, complex sentences:** Create a calm, thoughtful, or descriptive mood.
*   **Non-standard grammar:** Sometimes authors intentionally break grammar rules in dialogue to show that a character is uneducated, young, or speaks a different dialect.

### 3. Clarity of Expression
**Clarity of expression** refers to how clearly and effectively the author communicates their ideas to the reader. 
*   A good writer usually strives for high clarity so the reader understands the plot.
*   However, sometimes an author uses **intentional ambiguity** (making things slightly unclear) to force the reader to think, guess, or feel confused, matching the confusion of the characters.

## Determining an Author's Narrative Style

**Objective b.ii: determine an author’s narrative style.**

**Style** is the unique "fingerprint" of an author. It is the combination of all the techniques discussed above. To determine an author's narrative style in your prescribed texts, you must look at how they combine different elements:

1.  **Examine the Diction:** Does the author use big, poetic words, or simple, everyday language? Do they use local proverbs and African idioms (like Chinua Achebe), or very Western, formal English?
2.  **Look at the Syntax (Grammar):** Are the paragraphs long and detailed, or short and punchy? 
3.  **Check the Point of View:** Does the author prefer an intimate first-person style, or a distant, all-knowing omniscient style?
4.  **Identify the Tone:** Tone is the author’s attitude towards the subject. Is the writing sarcastic, humorous, sad, or serious?
5.  **Notice Literary Devices:** Does the author use a lot of similes, metaphors, imagery, or irony to tell the story?

By combining these factors, you can describe an author's style. For example, an author's style might be described as *"simple, highly colloquial, and humorous, using first-person narration to build intimacy."*

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Don't confuse Hero with Protagonist:** A protagonist is just the main character. An antihero is a protagonist, but they are *not* a hero.
*   **Don't confuse Flat and Round with Good and Bad:** A villain can be a round character (complex, with a backstory and mixed emotions), and a good guy can be a flat character (simple, only has one personality trait).
*   **Omniscient vs. Third-Person Limited:** If the text says "He thought..." and "She felt..." in the same scene, it is omniscient. If you only ever hear what *one* "He" or "She" is thinking, it is limited.
*   **Stream of Consciousness looks messy:** If you see a passage with little punctuation, run-on sentences, and jumping thoughts, it is stream of consciousness. 
*   **Foil is about contrast:** A foil isn't necessarily an enemy; they can be the hero's best friend. They exist just to be the opposite of the hero (e.g., a quiet hero with a very loud best friend).

## Quick summary

1.  **Point of View (POV)** is the angle from which a story is told. First person uses "I", second person uses "You", and third person uses "He/She". 
2.  An **omniscient narrator** is all-knowing and can see into the minds of every character in the story.
3.  **Stream of consciousness** captures the raw, unfiltered flow of a character's mind, while an **epiphany** is a moment of sudden, life-changing realisation.
4.  A **round character** is complex and changes throughout the story, whereas a **flat character** is one-dimensional and remains the same.
5.  An **antihero** is a main character who lacks traditional heroic traits, unlike the noble and brave **hero**.
6.  A **foil** is a character used to contrast with another character to highlight specific traits.
7.  **Diction** (word choice) and **grammar/syntax** (sentence structure) are key elements of language that affect a story's mood.
8.  An author's **narrative style** is determined by combining their choice of POV, diction, syntax, tone, and literary devices.

## Practice questions

**1. A narrator who knows the thoughts, feelings, and motives of all characters in a novel is using which point of view?**
- A. First-person limited
- B. Second-person
- C. Third-person omniscient
- D. Stream of consciousness

**Answer: C.** An omniscient narrator is "all-knowing" and can see into the minds of every character, unlike a limited narrator who only knows the thoughts of one character.

**2. A main character who is lazy, cowardly, and morally flawed, but who the reader still roots for, is best described as an:**
- A. Antagonist
- B. Antihero
- C. Villain
- D. Foil

**Answer: B.** An antihero is a protagonist (main character) who lacks the traditional noble qualities of a hero, such as bravery or high morality.

**3. In literature, an epiphany refers to:**
- A. A sudden moment of great realisation or insight by a character
- B. The climax of a physical fight between the hero and villain
- C. The author's choice of difficult vocabulary
- D. A character who never changes

**Answer: A.** An epiphany is a "flash of insight" where a character suddenly understands something profound about themselves or their situation.

**4. A character whose personality traits contrast sharply with those of the protagonist, serving to highlight the protagonist's qualities, is called a:**
- A. Flat character
- B. Foil
- C. Round character
- D. Heroine

**Answer: B.** A foil is designed specifically to contrast with another character (usually the protagonist) to draw attention to their specific traits.

**5. Which of the following best describes "diction" in prose?**
- A. The perspective from which the story is told
- B. The author's deliberate choice of words
- C. The underlying moral lesson of the story
- D. The physical location where the story takes place

**Answer: B.** Diction refers strictly to word choice, such as deciding whether to use formal language, informal slang, or concrete terms.

**6. A character in a novel is described as complex, possessing both good and bad traits, and undergoes significant personal growth by the end of the story. This is an example of a:**
- A. Flat character
- B. Round character
- C. Static character
- D. Villain

**Answer: B.** Round characters are multi-dimensional and dynamic, meaning they have complex personalities and change over the course of the narrative.

**7. "I crept down the dark hallway, my heart pounding in my chest. I wondered if anyone else could hear it." This sentence is written in the:**
- A. Third-person limited point of view
- B. First-person point of view
- C. Second-person point of view
- D. Omniscient point of view

**Answer: B.** The use of the pronouns "I" and "my" indicates that the narrator is a character inside the story, which is the first-person point of view.

**8. Which narrative technique tries to capture the continuous, unedited, and often chaotic flow of a character's inner thoughts?**
- A. Epiphany
- B. Omniscient narration
- C. Direct characterisation
- D. Stream of consciousness

**Answer: D.** Stream of consciousness is used to mimic the natural, unfiltered jumping of the human mind from one thought to another.

**9. When an author writes short, fragmented sentences like, "He ran. Darkness fell. Silence. A scream.", they are manipulating what element to create tension?**
- A. Grammar and syntax
- B. Point of view
- C. Characterisation
- D. Epiphany

**Answer: A.** Manipulating sentence structure (syntax and grammar), such as using very short or fragmented sentences, is a stylistic choice used to create a fast pace or tension.

**10. How does a reader best determine an author’s narrative style?**
- A. By reading the author's biography
- B. By looking at the book's publication date
- C. By analysing their combination of diction, tone, syntax, and point of view
- D. By identifying the setting of the story

**Answer: C.** An author's narrative style is the unique way they write, which is determined by how they use language (diction and syntax), perspective (POV), and tone.
