## Gabriel Okara: *Once Upon a Time*

Gabriel Okara is a pioneer Nigerian poet whose work often explores the transition of African society from traditional communal life to modern individualism.

### Theme (Objective c.i)
The central theme is the **loss of cultural innocence and sincerity**, replaced by the **hypocrisy of modern, Westernised society**. The poem explores nostalgia for a past where human interactions were genuine, and expresses a deep desire to unlearn artificial behaviour and relearn childhood innocence.

### Socio-political Context (Objective c.ii)
The poem is set against the backdrop of post-colonial Africa. It reflects the cultural clash and transition occurring as traditional African values—which the poet portrays as sincere, warm, and communal—were being replaced by Western values that the poet sees as cold, individualistic, and deceptive. 

### Types, Form and Prosody (Objectives b.i, b.iii, b.iv)
The poem is a lyric written in **free verse**. It does not follow a rigid rhyme scheme or regular metre, which gives it the natural rhythm of everyday speech. The mechanics and punctuation support a conversational and intimate dialogue.

### Poetic Devices (Objective b.ii)
- **Repetition:** The phrase "Once upon a time" opens and closes the poem, giving it the tone of a folktale and emphasizing that the sincere past is now like a distant fairy tale.
- **Metaphor:** The persona says he has learned to wear "many faces / like dresses" (homeface, officeface, streetface), showing how modern people change their personalities to suit different situations. He also describes people having "ice-block-cold eyes," a powerful metaphor for emotionless and unfeeling interactions.
- **Simile:** Artificial smiles are compared to "a fixed portrait smile," and the persona describes his own fake smile as being "like a snake's bare fangs," revealing the hidden malice and danger behind modern politeness.
- **Juxtaposition:** The poem constantly contrasts the past (laughing "with their hearts") with the present (laughing "with their teeth").

### Style (Objective c.iii)
The style is highly conversational and confessional. The persona is a **father speaking directly to his son** (first-person point of view). He expresses deep regret that he has learned to shake hands without his heart and asks his son to help him unlearn these "muting things" so he can be genuine again.

## Niyi Osundare: *Not my Business*

Niyi Osundare is a Nigerian poet known for his accessible language, use of oral traditions, and strong pro-masses (Marxist) stance. 

### Theme (Objective c.i)
The poem deals with **political apathy and the illusion of safety**. It highlights the oppression, tyranny, and arbitrary arrests carried out by a totalitarian regime, and strongly warns about the **consequence of silence** and the necessity of collective responsibility.

### Socio-political Context (Objective c.ii)
The poem is a direct reflection of life under a military dictatorship in Nigeria (reminiscent of the regimes in the 1980s and 1990s). During this era, state terror was common, citizens were arbitrarily arrested or disappeared, and many people chose to look away out of fear for their own lives.

### Types, Form and Prosody (Objectives b.i, b.iii, b.iv)
The poem is a lyric structured in stanzas. The early stanzas end with a regular **refrain**. However, in the final stanza, the structural pattern and the refrain are broken. This mechanical shift perfectly mirrors the sudden disruption of the persona's false sense of security. 

### Poetic Devices (Objective b.ii)
- **Refrain:** The persona repeatedly asks, "What business of mine is it / So long they don't take the yam / From my savouring mouth?" This highlights his selfish indifference.
- **Symbolism:** The "yam" symbolises personal survival, sustenance, and comfort. The "jeep" that comes to take people away symbolises military power and state terror.
- **Irony:** The persona believes that minding his own business will keep him safe. The irony is that his failure to speak up for his neighbours (Akanni, Danladi, and Chinwe) ensures that there is no one left to speak for him when the oppressors finally come to his door.

### Style (Objective c.iii)
The persona is an ordinary, passive citizen. The poem is written in a simple, accessible style with a first-person point of view. The tone begins as casual, detached, and complacent, but shifts dramatically in the final stanza to shock and helplessness when a jeep waits outside the persona's own door.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Persona | The speaker or voice in a poem (e.g., the father in *Once Upon a Time*). |
| Refrain | A line or group of lines repeated at intervals in a poem, often at the end of a stanza. |
| Free verse | Poetry that does not have a regular rhythm or rhyme scheme. |
| Apathy | Lack of interest, enthusiasm, or concern; indifference to the suffering of others. |
| Hypocrisy | Claiming to have moral standards or beliefs to which one's own behaviour does not conform. |
| Personification | Giving human qualities or abilities to non-human things or abstract concepts. |
| Socio-political context | The social, cultural, and political environment in which a text is written or set. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Match the poet to the poem:** JAMB frequently asks straightforward questions about authorship. Memorise the list: Okara (*Once Upon a Time*), Kamara (*New Tongue*), Soyinka (*Night*), Osundare (*Not my Business*), Afriyie-Vidza (*Hearty Garlands*), Cheney-Coker (*The Breast of the Sea*).
- **Understand the central metaphors:** In Okara's poem, the "ice-block-cold eyes" and "fixed portrait smile" represent the artificiality of modern life.
- **Identify the refrain:** In Osundare's poem, the repetition of "What business of mine is it..." is a refrain that emphasizes the persona's dangerous apathy.
- **Don't confuse the personas:** Okara's persona is a regretful father wanting to learn from his son; Osundare's persona is a passive citizen who eventually becomes a victim of the regime he ignored.

## Practice questions

**1. Who is the poet of *Once Upon a Time*?**

- A. Wole Soyinka
- B. Niyi Osundare
- C. Gabriel Okara
- D. Syl Cheney-Coker

**Answer: C.** Gabriel Okara wrote *Once Upon a Time*, a poem reflecting on the loss of traditional African sincerity.

**2. In Niyi Osundare's *Not my Business*, the recurring lines where the persona claims he does not care as long as they don't take his "yam" serve as a:**

- A. Metaphor
- B. Refrain
- C. Simile
- D. Personification

**Answer: B.** The repeated lines act as a refrain, structurally emphasizing the persona's repeated apathy each time a neighbour is taken away.

**3. The primary theme of Gabriel Okara's *Once Upon a Time* is the:**

- A. Beauty of childhood
- B. Loss of traditional sincerity to modern hypocrisy
- C. Danger of political oppression
- D. Overwhelming power of nature

**Answer: B.** The poem contrasts the genuine, heartfelt interactions of the past with the fake, artificial behaviours of modern society.

**4. In *Once Upon a Time*, the persona speaking in the poem is a:**

- A. Dictator speaking to citizens
- B. Mother speaking to her daughter
- C. Father speaking to his son
- D. Citizen speaking to a soldier

**Answer: C.** The persona is a father who asks his son to help him unlearn his artificial habits and relearn how to be sincere.

**5. Wole Soyinka's *Night* heavily relies on which literary device to give human qualities and dominance to the darkness?**

- A. Simile
- B. Irony
- C. Personification
- D. Oxymoron

**Answer: C.** Soyinka addresses Night as a living, powerful entity, using personification (e.g., "Your hand is heavy, Night").

**6. In *Not my Business*, the "yam" in the persona's mouth primarily symbolizes:**

- A. The agricultural wealth of the nation
- B. Personal survival and comfort
- C. The oppression of the military
- D. The traditional African past

**Answer: B.** The yam represents the persona's own food, sustenance, and comfort, which he selfishly prioritizes over the safety of his neighbours.

**7. Which of the following prescribed poets is best known for addressing the consequences of political apathy during military regimes?**

- A. Elizabeth L.A. Kamara
- B. Gabriel Okara
- C. S.O.H. Afriyie-Vidza
- D. Niyi Osundare

**Answer: D.** Niyi Osundare's *Not my Business* directly tackles the issue of citizens ignoring state tyranny under military rule.

**8. Elizabeth L.A. Kamara's *New Tongue* primarily explores issues related to:**

- A. Political elections
- B. Language and cultural identity
- C. The beauty of the night
- D. Family disputes

**Answer: B.** The poem uses the metaphor of a "new tongue" to explore linguistic displacement and its effect on African identity.

**9. The dramatic shift in tone in the final stanza of *Not my Business* occurs because:**

- A. The persona finally gets enough to eat
- B. The oppressors arrive at the persona's own door
- C. The military regime is overthrown
- D. The persona's neighbours return safely

**Answer: B.** The complacent tone shatters when a jeep arrives for the persona, proving that his apathy did not keep him safe.

**10. In *Once Upon a Time*, the poet uses the phrase "ice-block-cold eyes" to show:**

- A. Intense hatred
- B. Deep sadness
- C. Emotionless artificiality
- D. Physical illness

**Answer: C.** The metaphor describes eyes that lack warmth, sincerity, and human emotion, representing the artificiality of modern interactions.
