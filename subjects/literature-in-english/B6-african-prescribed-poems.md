# JAMB Literature In English Notes: Set Texts: African Prescribed Poems

## What JAMB expects

This file is a study guide for Section B of the JAMB Literature in English syllabus: A List of Selected African and Non-African Plays, Novels and Poems. It covers the six prescribed **African poems**.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to apply the general poetry objectives to these specific texts:
1. Identify the types of poetry (Objective b.i).
2. Analyse the poetic devices used in each poem (Objective b.ii).
3. Appreciate the prosody (metre, rhyme) and mechanics of the poems (Objectives b.iii, b.iv).
4. Identify the themes in each poem (Objective c.i).
5. Understand the socio-political context of the poems (Objective c.ii).
6. Analyse the style of each poet (Objective c.iii).

**Important note on accuracy:** These notes only state what several independent study sources agree on. Where sources disagree, or where a detail could not be confirmed, a **[TO BE COMPLETED: ...]** marker is left. Always read each poem itself, because exam questions test the text. Line and stanza counts below come from study guides and should be checked against your own copy of the poems.

## Gabriel Okara: *Once Upon a Time*

Gabriel Okara is a Nigerian poet and novelist. The poem is reported to appear in his collection *The Fisherman's Invocation* (1978).
**[TO BE COMPLETED: Confirm the collection and year from the printed anthology]**

### Theme (Objective c.i)
The poem is about **insincerity and the loss of genuine human warmth**. The speaker looks back to a time when people were sincere and compares it with a present in which greetings and smiles are false. Commentators list the main themes as insincerity, lost innocence, nostalgia, conformity and the wish to be authentic. Some readings link the artificial behaviour to the influence of colonial or Western ways of living.

### Socio-political Context (Objective c.ii)
Some commentators connect the artificial behaviour in the poem to the effect of colonial influence and modern city life on African society. This is an interpretation, not a fact stated in the poem.
**[TO BE COMPLETED: Any specific setting or historical reference mentioned in the poem itself]**

### Types, Form and Prosody (Objectives b.i, b.iii, b.iv)
The poem is a reflective lyric. Study guides describe it as **free verse**, one giving 43 lines in seven stanzas. Another source claims a mostly iambic metre with variations, so the sources disagree on the metre.
**[TO BE COMPLETED: Confirm the exact number of stanzas and lines, and whether there is any regular metre, from the printed text]**

### Poetic Devices (Objective b.ii)
- **Metaphor:** The speaker describes the different "faces" he has learned to wear, which shows how people change their behaviour to suit different situations and hide their true feelings.
- **Simile:** A false smile is compared with a snake's fangs, suggesting hidden danger behind polite behaviour.
- **Contrast (juxtaposition):** The sincere past is set against the false present.

The poem is conversational and confessional. The persona is a **father speaking to his son**, who does not reply. The father regrets that people now exchange false smiles, and he asks his son to teach him to laugh sincerely again. This request is the emotional turning point of the poem.

## Elizabeth L.A. Kamara: *New Tongue*

Elizabeth L.A. Kamara is a Sierra Leonean educator, writer and poet. The poem appears in her collection *To Cross for a Daughter and Other Poems*.

### Theme (Objective c.i)
Study guides read the poem mainly as being about **cultural shift and cultural displacement**: one guide says it is not essentially about language change, but about the change of culture that comes with it. Other themes listed are **generational conflict, loss and individualism**. The poem draws attention to Africans placing foreign (Western or non-African) values over their own.

## Niyi Osundare: *Not my Business*

Niyi Osundare is a Nigerian poet.
**[TO BE COMPLETED: The collection and year. Sources conflict, so check a printed anthology]**

### Theme (Objective c.i)
The poem is about **indifference, silence and political oppression (tyranny)**. The speaker keeps saying that the injustice done to others is not his business, as long as his own comfort is not touched. The poem warns that staying silent does not keep anyone safe.

### Socio-political Context (Objective c.ii)
Commentators link the poem to a period of **military dictatorship in Nigeria**, when people were taken away by the state and many chose to say nothing. One account says security officials came to question Osundare about what his poems meant.

### Types, Form and Prosody (Objectives b.i, b.iii, b.iv)
The poem is **free verse** with no rhyme scheme. Sources disagree on the number of stanzas (one says four, another eight).
**[TO BE COMPLETED: Confirm the number of stanzas and the exact form of the repeated lines from the printed text]**

### Poetic Devices (Objective b.ii)
- **Repetition / refrain:** After each episode, the speaker repeats that it is "not my business", which stresses his selfish indifference.
- **Irony:** He thinks minding his own business will protect him, but the final stanza turns the poem back on him.
- **Concrete detail:** Named neighbours (Akanni and Danladi) and a jeep make the oppression feel real. The jeep stands for the power of the state.
**[TO BE COMPLETED: Meaning of any other symbols in the poem, checked against the text]**

### Style (Objective c.iii)
The persona is an ordinary citizen who speaks in the first person in simple language. His tone is complacent at first. In the last stanza a jeep waits on his own lawn, so the poem ends with the danger reaching him.
**[TO BE COMPLETED: Exact tone words and any wording from the final stanza, checked against the text]**

## Syl Cheney-Coker: *The Breast of the Sea*

Syl Cheney-Coker is a Sierra Leonean poet. The poem is from his 2008 collection *Stone Child and Other Poems*. Many sources spell the title *The Breasts of the Sea*, so expect the variation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Persona | The speaker or voice in a poem (e.g., the father in *Once Upon a Time*). |
| Refrain | A line or group of lines repeated at intervals in a poem. |
| Free verse | Poetry that does not have a regular rhythm or rhyme scheme. |
| Apathy | Lack of interest or concern; indifference to the suffering of others. |
| Personification | Giving human qualities to non-human things or abstract ideas. |
| Simile | A comparison using "like" or "as". |
| Tercet | A stanza of three lines. |
| Panegyric | A poem or speech of praise. |
| Socio-political context | The social, cultural and political environment in which a text is written or set. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Match the poet to the poem:** JAMB often asks about authorship. Memorise: Okara (*Once Upon a Time*), Kamara (*New Tongue*), Soyinka (*Night*), Osundare (*Not my Business*), Afriyie-Vidza (*Hearty Garlands*), Cheney-Coker (*The Breast of the Sea*).
- **Read the poems yourself:** These notes cannot replace the text. Quote only what you have checked in your own copy.
- **Do not confuse the personas:** Okara's persona is a father speaking to his son; Osundare's persona is a citizen who ignores the suffering of neighbours until danger reaches him.
- **Watch the title spelling:** The Cheney-Coker poem appears as both *Breast* and *Breasts* of the Sea.
- **Where sources differ** (for example on stanza counts or metre), trust the printed text in your own prescribed anthology.

## Quick summary

1. The six African prescribed poems cover cultural identity, political oppression, nature and fear, ageing and the environment.
2. Okara's *Once Upon a Time* has a father speaking to his son, regretting the false smiles of the present and asking to be taught to laugh sincerely again.
3. Kamara's *New Tongue* is read as being about cultural shift, in which a new generation moves away from its roots towards foreign values.
4. Soyinka's *Night* personifies night as a presence of fear and mystery, set against light.
5. Osundare's *Not my Business* shows a speaker who ignores his neighbours' suffering under a dictatorship until a jeep waits at his own place.
6. Afriyie-Vidza's *Hearty Garlands* is a tribute to a person who has reached 85, praising a long and fulfilled life.
7. Cheney-Coker's *The Breast of the Sea* (from *Stone Child and Other Poems*, 2008) pictures the sea as both nurturing and a dumping ground for waste and war.

## Practice questions

**1. Who is the poet of *Once Upon a Time*?**

- A. Wole Soyinka
- B. Niyi Osundare
- C. Gabriel Okara
- D. Syl Cheney-Coker

**Answer: C.** Gabriel Okara wrote *Once Upon a Time*, a poem about lost sincerity.

**2. In *Not my Business*, the speaker's repeated insistence after each episode that it is not his business is best described as a:**

- A. Metaphor
- B. Refrain
- C. Simile
- D. Personification

**Answer: B.** A line repeated at intervals is a refrain, and here it stresses the speaker's indifference.

**3. The main theme of Gabriel Okara's *Once Upon a Time* is:**

- A. The beauty of childhood
- B. Loss of sincerity in a world of false behaviour
- C. Danger of political oppression
- D. Overwhelming power of nature

**Answer: B.** The poem contrasts a sincere past with a present of false smiles and greetings.

**4. In *Once Upon a Time*, the persona is a:**

- A. Dictator speaking to citizens
- B. Mother speaking to her daughter
- C. Father speaking to his son
- D. Citizen speaking to a soldier

**Answer: C.** A father speaks to his son and asks to be taught to laugh sincerely again.

**5. Which device gives Night human qualities in Wole Soyinka's poem *Night*?**

- A. Simile
- B. Irony
- C. Personification
- D. Onomatopoeia

**Answer: C.** Night is presented as a living figure from the start of the poem, which is personification.

**6. In *Not my Business*, what is the first neighbour named in the poem, Akanni, taken away by?**

- A. A boat
- B. A jeep
- C. A train
- D. A lorry

**Answer: B.** A jeep takes Akanni away, and a jeep later waits at the speaker's own place. The jeep stands for the power of the state.

**7. *Not my Business* by Niyi Osundare is linked by commentators to which kind of rule in Nigeria?**

- A. Colonial rule
- B. Military dictatorship
- C. Traditional rule
- D. Parliamentary rule

**Answer: B.** Commentators link the poem to a period of military dictatorship.

**8. Elizabeth L.A. Kamara's *New Tongue* is mainly read as being about:**

- A. Political elections
- B. Cultural shift and loss of heritage
- C. The beauty of the night
- D. The wonders of the sea

**Answer: B.** Study guides read the poem as being about cultural shift, in which a new generation moves away from its roots towards foreign values.

**9. What happens in the final stanza of *Not my Business*?**

- A. The speaker finally helps a neighbour
- B. A jeep waits at the speaker's own place, so the danger reaches him
- C. The military regime is overthrown
- D. The neighbours return safely

**Answer: B.** The final stanza turns the poem back on the speaker, showing that his silence did not protect him.

**10. Read this made-up line: "His smile was like a knife behind a curtain." The device used is a:**

- A. Simile
- B. Personification
- C. Refrain
- D. Alliteration

**Answer: A.** The comparison uses the word "like", so it is a simile. (Okara's poem likewise uses a simile to compare a false smile with a snake's fangs.)
