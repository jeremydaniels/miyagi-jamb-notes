## Atmospheric Pressure

The Earth is surrounded by a thick layer of air known as the atmosphere, extending hundreds of kilometres above the surface. Because air has mass, gravity pulls it downwards. **Atmospheric pressure** is the pressure exerted by the weight of this atmospheric air on any surface within it. 

Even though we do not consciously feel it, the air above us is pressing down with a massive force. At sea level (for example, at Bar Beach in Lagos or the coastal areas of Port Harcourt), standard atmospheric pressure is about $1.013 \times 10^{5}\text{ Pa}$. This means roughly $100,000\text{ N}$ of force is pressing on every square metre. We do not collapse under this immense pressure because the fluids and gases inside our bodies push outward with an equal balancing pressure.

Everyday examples of atmospheric pressure in action include:
- **Drinking with a straw:** Sucking on a straw removes the air from inside it, lowering the pressure. The higher atmospheric pressure on the surface of the drink in the cup pushes the liquid up the straw.
- **Using a syringe:** Pulling the plunger creates a low-pressure area (a partial vacuum) inside the barrel, and atmospheric pressure forces the liquid medicine into the syringe.
- **Rubber suckers:** When a rubber sucker is pressed tightly against a smooth wall, air is forced out from underneath it. The atmospheric pressure pushing from the outside is greater than the pressure inside, holding the sucker firmly in place.

### Recognizing the S.I. Units of Pressure

**Pressure** is defined generally as the force acting perpendicularly (at right angles) per unit area of a surface. 

The standard formula for pressure is:
$$P = \frac{F}{A}$$

where:
- $P$ is the pressure,
- $F$ is the perpendicular force in Newtons ($\text{N}$),
- $A$ is the area over which the force acts, in square metres ($\text{m}^{2}$).

**The S.I. Unit**
The S.I. unit of pressure is the **Pascal ($\text{Pa}$)**, named after the French physicist Blaise Pascal. From the formula, $1\text{ Pascal}$ is equal to $1\text{ Newton per square metre}$:
$$1\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ N m}^{-2}$$

**Other Common Units (Textbook Variations):**
While the Pascal is the standard unit, JAMB questions sometimes use other units depending on the context of the question (especially for atmospheric and gas pressure):
- **Atmosphere ($\text{atm}$):** Used commonly for gas pressure. $1\text{ atm} \approx 1.013 \times 10^{5}\text{ Pa}$.
- **Millimetres of mercury ($\text{mmHg}$):** Used in barometers and for medical blood pressure measurements. Standard atmospheric pressure is $760\text{ mmHg}$.
- **Bar:** Often used in meteorology. $1\text{ bar} = 1.0 \times 10^{5}\text{ Pa}$.

[IMAGE: A solid rectangular block resting on its largest flat face (labelled 'Minimum pressure, largest area') next to the same block standing upright on its smallest face (labelled 'Maximum pressure, smallest area'). Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

**Worked Example 1: Pressure exerted by a solid block**
A rectangular block of mass $20\text{ kg}$ has dimensions $0.1\text{ m} \times 0.2\text{ m} \times 0.5\text{ m}$. Calculate the maximum pressure it can exert on a flat horizontal table. (Take $g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}$)

1. **State the formula:** $P = \frac{F}{A}$
2. **Calculate the force (weight of the block):** The force is the weight. $F = mg = 20\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m s}^{-2} = 200\text{ N}$.
3. **Determine the minimum area:** To generate the maximum pressure, the block must rest on its smallest face. The smallest dimensions are $0.1\text{ m}$ and $0.2\text{ m}$.
   $A_{\text{min}} = 0.1\text{ m} \times 0.2\text{ m} = 0.02\text{ m}^{2}$.
4. **Calculate the pressure:** 
   $P_{\text{max}} = \frac{200\text{ N}}{0.02\text{ m}^{2}} = 10,000\text{ Pa}$ (or $1.0 \times 10^{4}\text{ Pa}$).

### Identifying Pressure Measuring Instruments

Pressure is measured using specific instruments depending on whether we are measuring atmospheric pressure or the pressure of a confined fluid. The syllabus requires you to identify and understand the simple mercury barometer, the aneroid barometer, and the manometer.

#### 1. The Simple Mercury Barometer
A barometer is an instrument that measures atmospheric pressure. The simple mercury barometer was invented by the Italian physicist Evangelista Torricelli. 
- **Construction:** A thick-walled, heavy glass tube about $1\text{ metre}$ long, closed at one end, is completely filled with mercury. It is then inverted and its open end is submerged into a dish (reservoir) containing more mercury.
- **Observation:** The mercury in the tube falls until the weight of the mercury column is exactly balanced by the atmospheric pressure pushing down on the exposed surface of the mercury in the dish.
- **Torricellian Vacuum:** The empty space at the closed top of the tube contains no air, only a negligible amount of mercury vapour. This vacuum is known as the Torricellian vacuum.
- **Reading:** At sea level, the vertical height of the mercury column is usually **$76\text{ cm}$ (or $760\text{ mm}$)**. This is why standard atmospheric pressure is often stated as $760\text{ mmHg}$.
- **Note on tilt:** If the glass tube is tilted, the *vertical* height of the mercury remains exactly $76\text{ cm}$, even though the length of mercury measured along the slanted tube will increase.

[IMAGE: A simple mercury barometer. A long glass tube is inverted in a reservoir (dish) of mercury. Arrows point down on the mercury surface in the dish, labelled 'Atmospheric pressure'. A vertical bracket measures the height of the mercury in the tube from the dish's liquid surface to the top of the column, labelled 'h = 760 mm'. The empty space at the very top of the closed tube is labelled 'Torricellian vacuum'. Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

#### 2. The Aneroid Barometer
"Aneroid" translates to "without liquid". This is a highly portable barometer that uses no mercury or water.
- **How it works:** It contains a sealed, partially evacuated, corrugated metallic box (a vacuum chamber or capsule). When atmospheric pressure increases, the flexible box is squeezed slightly inward. When atmospheric pressure decreases, the box expands outward.
- **Display:** These tiny physical movements of the box are magnified by a complex system of delicate levers and springs, which rotate a pointer across a calibrated dial.
- **Advantages:** Because it contains no easily spilled liquids, it is portable, robust, and safe (mercury is highly toxic). It is the standard type of barometer used in aircraft, ships, and homes.

[IMAGE: A cross-section diagram of an aneroid barometer. Shows a corrugated metallic capsule (partially evacuated box) connected to a strong leaf spring. A mechanical lever and gear system connects the spring to a pointer moving over a circular dial marked with pressure values. Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

#### 3. The Manometer
A manometer is an instrument used to measure the pressure of a confined gas or liquid.
- **Structure:** It consists of a simple U-shaped glass tube partially filled with a liquid (usually water for low pressures or mercury for high pressures).
- **How it works:** One end of the U-tube is left open to the atmosphere ($P_{\text{atm}}$), and the other end is connected to the gas supply ($P_{\text{gas}}$) whose pressure is to be measured. 
- If the gas pressure is greater than the atmospheric pressure, the gas pushes the liquid down on its side and forces it up on the open side.
- **Formula:** The absolute gas pressure is the atmospheric pressure plus the pressure exerted by the liquid column difference ($h$):
  $$P_{\text{gas}} = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$$
  (Where $\rho$ is the density of the liquid in the manometer, $g$ is gravity, and $h$ is the difference in liquid levels between the two arms).

[IMAGE: A U-tube manometer. The left arm is connected to a round gas flask labelled 'Gas pressure (P_gas)'. The right arm is open to the air, with an arrow labelled 'Atmospheric pressure (P_atm)' pointing in. The liquid (e.g., mercury) is pushed down in the left arm and up in the right arm. A horizontal dotted line shows the lower liquid level in the left arm continuing across to the right arm. A vertical bracket measures the height difference 'h' between the liquid level in the right arm and the dotted line. Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**Worked Example 2: Calculating gas pressure with a manometer**
A U-tube manometer contains mercury of density $13,600\text{ kg m}^{-3}$. One end is connected to a gas cylinder and the other is open to the atmosphere. The mercury level in the open limb is $10\text{ cm}$ higher than the level in the closed limb connected to the gas. Calculate the absolute pressure of the gas. ($P_{\text{atm}} = 1.0 \times 10^{5}\text{ Pa}, g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}$)

1. **Convert the height difference to S.I. units (metres):** 
   $h = 10\text{ cm} = 0.1\text{ m}$.
2. **State the manometer formula:** 
   $P_{\text{gas}} = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$
3. **Calculate the excess pressure from the mercury column:**
   $\rho g h = 13,600\text{ kg m}^{-3} \times 10\text{ m s}^{-2} \times 0.1\text{ m} = 13,600\text{ Pa}$.
4. **Add this to atmospheric pressure:**
   $P_{\text{gas}} = 100,000\text{ Pa} + 13,600\text{ Pa} = 113,600\text{ Pa}$.
   *(This can also be written as $1.136 \times 10^{5}\text{ Pa}$.)*

### Relating the Variation of Pressure to Height

Atmospheric pressure is caused by the physical weight of the air column above a given point. Therefore, **atmospheric pressure decreases as altitude (height above sea level) increases.** 

- **At sea level** (e.g., Lagos, Port Harcourt, or Calabar), there is a maximum amount of air above pressing down, so the atmospheric pressure is highest (about $760\text{ mmHg}$).
- **At high altitudes** (e.g., Jos Plateau, Obudu Mountain Resort, or in an aeroplane), the column of air above is much shorter and thinner, meaning less air is pressing down, resulting in lower pressure. 

This drop in pressure with altitude explains several phenomena:
1. Water boils at a lower temperature in Jos than it does in Lagos (as explored in `A14-change-of-state.md`).
2. Mountaineers and high-altitude pilots sometimes require oxygen masks, as the lower pressure makes it harder for lungs to take in sufficient oxygen.

For relatively small changes in height (where the density of the air, $\rho_{\text{air}}$, can be assumed roughly constant), the pressure difference between two points is calculated as:
$$\Delta P = \rho_{\text{air}} g \Delta H$$
where $\Delta H$ is the height difference between the two locations.

### Using a Barometer as an Altimeter

Because atmospheric pressure drops in a predictable mathematical way as height increases, we can reverse the logic: we can use a barometer to measure altitude. 

When an **aneroid barometer** has its dial calibrated to read height directly instead of pressure, it is called an **altimeter**. 

Aircraft rely on altimeters to determine their cruising altitude. A mountaineer climbing a high peak can use a portable altimeter to know their exact elevation.

**Worked Example 3: Calculating height using barometer readings**
At the base of a hill, a mercury barometer reads $760\text{ mmHg}$, while at the top of the hill, it reads $730\text{ mmHg}$. If the average density of air is $1.2\text{ kg m}^{-3}$ and the density of mercury is $13,600\text{ kg m}^{-3}$, calculate the height of the hill. (Take $g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}$)

1. **Find the difference in the mercury column height ($\Delta h_{\text{Hg}}$):**
   $\Delta h_{\text{Hg}} = 760\text{ mm} - 730\text{ mm} = 30\text{ mm}$.
2. **Convert to S.I. units (metres):** 
   $\Delta h_{\text{Hg}} = \frac{30}{1000} = 0.03\text{ m}$.
3. **Equate the pressure differences:** The loss in air pressure from climbing the hill must equal the loss in pressure read by the mercury column.
   $\Delta P_{\text{air}} = \Delta P_{\text{Hg}}$
   $\rho_{\text{air}} \times g \times H = \rho_{\text{Hg}} \times g \times \Delta h_{\text{Hg}}$
4. **Cancel $g$ and rearrange to find $H$ (height of the hill):**
   $H = \frac{\rho_{\text{Hg}} \times \Delta h_{\text{Hg}}}{\rho_{\text{air}}}$
5. **Substitute the values:**
   $H = \frac{13,600 \times 0.03}{1.2} = \frac{408}{1.2} = 340\text{ m}$.
   *(The hill is $340\text{ m}$ high.)*

## Pressure in Liquids

Liquids, just like gases, have weight and therefore exert pressure on any object immersed in them and on the walls of the container holding them. Unlike gases, liquids are practically incompressible, meaning their density does not significantly change under pressure. 

### Determining the Relationship Between Pressure, Depth, and Density

The pressure at any point inside a liquid depends strictly on two factors: how deep the point is, and how dense the liquid is. We can mathematically derive this relationship.

**Deriving the Formula ($P = \rho g h$)**
Imagine a vertical column of liquid with a cross-sectional area $A$ and depth $h$.
1. The volume of this liquid column is $V = A \times h$.
2. From the definition of density ($\rho = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$), the mass of the liquid in the column is $m = \rho \times V = \rho \times A \times h$.
3. The weight (which is the downward force) of the liquid is $W = m \times g = \rho \times A \times h \times g$.
4. Pressure is defined as Force divided by Area ($P = \frac{F}{A}$).
5. Substituting the force, we get: $P = \frac{\rho \times A \times h \times g}{A}$.
6. The Area ($A$) on top and bottom cancels out, leaving:
   $$P = \rho g h$$

where:
- $P$ is the pressure exerted by the liquid (in $\text{Pa}$),
- $\rho$ is the density of the liquid (in $\text{kg m}^{-3}$),
- $g$ is acceleration due to gravity ($\approx 10\text{ m s}^{-2}$),
- $h$ is the depth below the free surface of the liquid (in $\text{m}$).

### Applying the Principle of Transmission of Pressure in Liquids

In 1653, Blaise Pascal discovered a vital property of confined liquids. **Pascal's Principle** states that:
*If a pressure is applied to an enclosed fluid, it is transmitted undiminished (equally) to every part of the fluid and to the walls of its container.*

If you push on a liquid trapped in a closed pipe, it immediately pushes back on the other end with the exact same pressure. 

**Applications of Pascal's Principle (Hydraulics)**
This principle is the foundation of **Hydraulic machines**. By utilizing cylinders of vastly different cross-sectional areas, we can dramatically multiply applied forces. 
- **The Hydraulic Press:** Used in factories to crush scrap metal or extract oil from seeds.
- **Hydraulic Jacks:** Used by mechanics to easily lift heavy cars with a small manual effort.
- **Hydraulic Car Brakes:** A small push from the driver's foot on the brake pedal transmits pressure through the brake fluid to push heavily on the brake pads at all four wheels.

[IMAGE: Diagram of a hydraulic press. Two vertical cylinders are connected by a horizontal pipe, all filled with a blue liquid. The left cylinder is narrow with a small piston (labelled 'Area A1, Force F1 pointing down'). The right cylinder is wide with a large piston (labelled 'Area A2, Force F2 pointing up'). Dotted lines in the fluid show pressure is equal throughout. Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**The Mathematics of the Hydraulic Press**
Because pressure is transmitted equally, the pressure generated at the small piston ($P_1$) is equal to the pressure exerted at the large piston ($P_2$):
$$P_{1} = P_{2}$$
$$\frac{F_{1}}{A_{1}} = \frac{F_{2}}{A_{2}}$$

Where:
- $F_1$ is the effort force applied to the small piston,
- $A_1$ is the area of the small piston,
- $F_2$ is the load force (or heavy weight lifted) by the large piston,
- $A_2$ is the area of the large piston.

Since $A_2$ is much larger than $A_1$, $F_2$ will be proportionally much larger than $F_1$. This setup acts as a **force multiplier** (this relates to topics in `A8-simple-machines.md`).

**Note on Radii/Diameters:** If the pistons are circular (as they usually are), their area is calculated using $A = \pi r^2$. The formula becomes:
$$\frac{F_{1}}{\pi r_{1}^{2}} = \frac{F_{2}}{\pi r_{2}^{2}} \implies \frac{F_{1}}{r_{1}^{2}} = \frac{F_{2}}{r_{2}^{2}}$$
*(JAMB frequently tests this exact variation. You must square the radii or diameters).*

**Worked Example 4: Hydraulic Jack Calculation**
In a mechanic's workshop, a hydraulic jack has a small piston with an area of $0.05\text{ m}^{2}$ and a large piston with an area of $0.5\text{ m}^{2}$. What force must be applied to the small piston to lift a car of mass $1000\text{ kg}$ resting on the large piston? ($g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}$)

1. **Calculate the force (weight) of the car on the large piston ($F_2$):**
   $F_{2} = mg = 1000\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m s}^{-2} = 10,000\text{ N}$.
2. **State Pascal's principle formula:**
   $\frac{F_{1}}{A_{1}} = \frac{F_{2}}{A_{2}}$
3. **Rearrange to find the effort force ($F_1$):**
   $F_{1} = F_{2} \times \frac{A_{1}}{A_{2}}$
4. **Substitute the values:**
   $F_{1} = 10,000\text{ N} \times \frac{0.05\text{ m}^{2}}{0.5\text{ m}^{2}} = 10,000 \times 0.1 = 1000\text{ N}$.
   *(A small force of $1000\text{ N}$ successfully lifts a heavy weight of $10,000\text{ N}$.)*

### Determining and Applying the Principle of Pressure in a Liquid

Understanding the specific traits of liquid pressure helps explain engineering designs and natural phenomena. 

**Characteristics of Liquid Pressure**
1. **Pressure increases with depth:** The deeper you go, the more liquid is pressing down. This is why major dams (like the Kainji Dam) are built with bases much thicker than their tops—to withstand the massive water pressure at the bottom.
2. **Pressure depends on density:** Mercury exerts a much greater pressure at a depth of $1\text{ m}$ than water does, because mercury is much denser.
3. **Pressure is the same at all points on a horizontal plane:** If a diver moves horizontally underwater without changing depth, the pressure remains absolutely constant.
4. **Pressure acts equally in all directions:** If you make a hole in the side of a plastic water bottle, water squirts out perpendicularly, showing pressure pushes sideways, upwards, and downwards.
5. **The Hydrostatic Paradox:** The pressure at the bottom of a container depends entirely on the vertical depth $h$, and not on the volume or the shape of the container. A narrow pipe filled with $10\text{ m}$ of water creates the exact same pressure at the bottom as a wide lake that is $10\text{ m}$ deep.

**Total (Absolute) Pressure Calculations**
The formula $P = \rho g h$ only calculates the pressure caused by the liquid itself (often called gauge pressure). However, in reality, the Earth's atmosphere is also pressing down on the exposed surface of the liquid. 

To determine the **Total (Absolute) Pressure** at a certain depth $h$, you must add the atmospheric pressure to the liquid's pressure:
$$P_{\text{total}} = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$$

**Worked Example 5: Calculating total pressure underwater**
A diver is exploring a deep borehole and is currently $15\text{ m}$ below the surface of the fresh water. Calculate the total absolute pressure acting on the diver. (Density of water = $1000\text{ kg m}^{-3}$, atmospheric pressure = $1.0 \times 10^{5}\text{ Pa}, g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}$)

1. **State the formula for total pressure:** 
   $P_{\text{total}} = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$
2. **Calculate the pressure due to the water alone ($\rho g h$):** 
   $P_{\text{water}} = 1000\text{ kg m}^{-3} \times 10\text{ m s}^{-2} \times 15\text{ m} = 150,000\text{ Pa}$.
3. **Add atmospheric pressure:**
   $P_{\text{total}} = 100,000\text{ Pa} + 150,000\text{ Pa} = 250,000\text{ Pa}$.
   *(This can be written as $2.5 \times 10^{5}\text{ Pa}$).*

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Wrong Unit Conversion:** You must always convert depths or heights given in centimetres (cm) or millimetres (mm) to metres (m) before substituting them into formulas like $P = \rho gh$.
- **Forgetting 'g':** In formulas requiring weight (like finding the pressure of a solid block resting on a table, or lifting a car with a jack), students often use the mass instead of multiplying by $g = 10 \text{ m s}^{-2}$. Weight ($F = mg$) must be in Newtons.
- **Total vs Liquid Pressure:** Read questions carefully! If JAMB asks for the *total* or *absolute* pressure on a diver or a submarine, you must add atmospheric pressure to the liquid pressure ($\rho gh$). If they ask for the pressure *due to the water*, just calculate $\rho gh$.
- **Squaring the radius:** In hydraulic press questions, if given the radius or diameter of the pistons instead of the area, you **must square them** ($F_1 / r_1^2 = F_2 / r_2^2$). A common trap is writing $F_1 / r_1 = F_2 / r_2$.
- **Altimeter logic:** Remember that atmospheric pressure goes **down** as height goes **up**.

## Quick summary

1. Pressure is defined as force per unit area ($P = F/A$). Its S.I. unit is the Pascal ($\text{Pa}$), which is exactly $1 \text{ N m}^{-2}$.
2. Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air pushing down and is measured using a simple mercury barometer or a liquid-free aneroid barometer.
3. Atmospheric pressure drops progressively as altitude increases. An aneroid barometer calibrated to read altitude directly is called an altimeter.
4. A manometer measures the pressure of confined gases using liquid columns in a U-tube ($P_{\text{gas}} = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$).
5. Pressure in a static liquid depends only on its density and its depth ($P = \rho g h$), and it acts equally in all directions at a specific depth. Total absolute pressure includes atmospheric pressure.
6. Pascal's Principle states that any pressure applied to an enclosed, incompressible liquid is transmitted equally and undiminished throughout the entire liquid.
7. Hydraulic machines (like the hydraulic press, jack, and car brakes) use Pascal's Principle to multiply forces, calculated using the relationship $F_1/A_1 = F_2/A_2$.

## Practice questions

**1. The S.I. unit of pressure is the Pascal. One Pascal is equivalent to:**
- A. $1 \text{ N m}^{-1}$
- B. $1 \text{ J m}^{-2}$
- C. $1 \text{ N m}^{-2}$
- D. $1 \text{ kg m}^{-3}$

**Answer: C.** Pressure is defined as Force divided by Area ($P = F/A$). Force is measured in Newtons ($\text{N}$) and Area is in square metres ($\text{m}^{2}$), giving $\text{N m}^{-2}$.

**2. Which of the following instruments is used to measure atmospheric pressure without using any liquids?**
- A. Manometer
- B. Simple mercury barometer
- C. Hydrometer
- D. Aneroid barometer

**Answer: D.** The aneroid barometer uses a partially evacuated corrugated metallic capsule to measure pressure, unlike the mercury barometer or manometer which rely on liquids. (A hydrometer measures density/relative density).

**3. A diver is $15 \text{ m}$ below the surface of a lake. If the density of water is $1000 \text{ kg m}^{-3}$ and atmospheric pressure is $1.0 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$, calculate the total pressure on the diver. ($g = 10 \text{ m s}^{-2}$)**
- A. $1.0 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$
- B. $1.15 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$
- C. $1.5 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$
- D. $2.5 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Formula: $P_{\text{total}} = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$
2. Liquid pressure: $\rho g h = 1000 \times 10 \times 15 = 150,000 \text{ Pa} = 1.5 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$.
3. Total pressure: $1.0 \times 10^5 + 1.5 \times 10^5 = 2.5 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$.
*Mistake behind option C:* Calculating only the liquid pressure and forgetting to add the atmospheric pressure on top of the lake.

**4. In a hydraulic press, the radii of the small and large pistons are $2 \text{ cm}$ and $10 \text{ cm}$ respectively. If a force of $50 \text{ N}$ is applied to the small piston, what is the load force exerted by the large piston?**
- A. $2 \text{ N}$
- B. $250 \text{ N}$
- C. $1250 \text{ N}$
- D. $2500 \text{ N}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Formula using radii: $F_1 / r_1^2 = F_2 / r_2^2$
2. Substitute: $50 / 2^2 = F_2 / 10^2 \implies 50 / 4 = F_2 / 100$
3. Solve: $F_2 = 50 \times (100 / 4) = 50 \times 25 = 1250 \text{ N}$.
*Mistake behind option B:* Forgetting to square the radii, leading to $50 \times (10 / 2) = 250 \text{ N}$.

**5. The use of a hydraulic jack to lift a heavy car relies primarily on:**
- A. Archimedes' principle
- B. Boyle's law
- C. Hooke's law
- D. Pascal's principle

**Answer: D.** Pascal's principle states that pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally to all parts of the fluid, which forms the operational basis of all hydraulic machines.

**6. At sea level, a mercury barometer reads $760 \text{ mm}$. At the top of a mountain, it reads $730 \text{ mm}$. Assuming the average density of air is $1.2 \text{ kg m}^{-3}$ and the density of mercury is $13,600 \text{ kg m}^{-3}$, calculate the height of the mountain.**
- A. $25 \text{ m}$
- B. $30 \text{ m}$
- C. $340 \text{ m}$
- D. $340,000 \text{ m}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Difference in mercury height: $\Delta h = 30 \text{ mm} = 0.03 \text{ m}$.
2. Pressure formula equating air and mercury columns: $H = (\rho_{\text{Hg}} \times \Delta h_{\text{Hg}}) / \rho_{\text{air}}$
3. $H = (13,600 \times 0.03) / 1.2 = 408 / 1.2 = 340 \text{ m}$.
*Mistake behind option D:* Failing to convert the height difference from millimetres to metres before calculating, resulting in $340,000 \text{ m}$.

**7. A solid rectangular block of mass $20 \text{ kg}$ has dimensions $0.1 \text{ m} \times 0.2 \text{ m} \times 0.5 \text{ m}$. What is the maximum pressure it can exert when resting on a horizontal table? ($g = 10 \text{ m s}^{-2}$)**
- A. $1,000 \text{ Pa}$
- B. $2,000 \text{ Pa}$
- C. $5,000 \text{ Pa}$
- D. $10,000 \text{ Pa}$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Maximum pressure occurs on the smallest possible area.
2. Minimum Area $A = 0.1 \text{ m} \times 0.2 \text{ m} = 0.02 \text{ m}^2$.
3. Force $F = mg = 20 \times 10 = 200 \text{ N}$.
4. $P = F/A = 200 / 0.02 = 10,000 \text{ Pa}$.
*Mistake behind option A:* Using the mass ($20$) instead of the weight ($200 \text{ N}$), resulting in $20 / 0.02 = 1000 \text{ Pa}$.

**8. An open U-tube manometer containing mercury (density $13,600 \text{ kg m}^{-3}$) is connected to a gas supply. The mercury level is $10 \text{ cm}$ higher in the open limb. If atmospheric pressure is $1.0 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$, the absolute gas pressure is:**
- A. $0.864 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$
- B. $1.01 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$
- C. $1.136 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$
- D. $1.46 \times 10^6 \text{ Pa}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Convert cm to m: $h = 0.1 \text{ m}$.
2. Since the level is higher in the open limb, the gas is pushing harder than the atmosphere, so gas pressure is greater.
3. Formula: $P_{\text{gas}} = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$.
4. $P_{\text{gas}} = 100,000 + (13,600 \times 10 \times 0.1) = 100,000 + 13,600 = 113,600 \text{ Pa}$ ($1.136 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$).
*Mistake behind option A:* Subtracting the column pressure instead of adding it (which would only be correct if the level was lower in the open limb).

**9. Which of the following statements about hydrostatic pressure in a liquid at rest is true?**
- A. It depends on the shape of the container.
- B. It decreases with an increase in depth.
- C. It acts equally in all directions at a given depth.
- D. It is greater on the sides of a container than on the bottom.

**Answer: C.** Hydrostatic pressure acts equally in all directions at any specific depth. It depends only on depth and density, not on the shape of the container (this is the hydrostatic paradox), and it increases (not decreases) with depth.

**10. A mechanic wants to lift a $1000 \text{ kg}$ car using a hydraulic jack. The area of the output piston is $0.5 \text{ m}^2$, and the area of the input piston is $0.05 \text{ m}^2$. What minimum force must the mechanic apply to the input piston? ($g = 10 \text{ m s}^{-2}$)**
- A. $100 \text{ N}$
- B. $1,000 \text{ N}$
- C. $10,000 \text{ N}$
- D. $100,000 \text{ N}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Force (weight) on the large piston: $F_2 = mg = 1000 \times 10 = 10,000 \text{ N}$.
2. Pascal's formula: $F_1 / A_1 = F_2 / A_2 \implies F_1 / 0.05 = 10,000 / 0.5$.
3. $F_1 = 10,000 \times (0.05 / 0.5) = 10,000 \times 0.1 = 1000 \text{ N}$.
*Mistake behind option A:* Forgetting to multiply the car's mass by $g$, thus using $1000$ instead of $10,000 \text{ N}$ for $F_2$, which yields an incorrect $100 \text{ N}$.
