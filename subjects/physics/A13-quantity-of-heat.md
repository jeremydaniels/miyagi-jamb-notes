## Heat as a Form of Energy

**Objective/Content item:** (i) heat as a form of energy.

**Heat** (also known as thermal energy) is a form of energy that flows from one region to another due to a difference in temperature. In physics, energy transfers naturally from a body at a higher temperature to a body at a lower temperature until both reach thermal equilibrium (the same steady temperature). Because it is a form of energy, the SI unit of heat is the **Joule** ($\text{J}$).

To fully grasp the concept of heat, we must look at the **Kinetic Molecular Theory** (which is covered in detail in `A18-structure-of-matter-kinetic-theory.md`). All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms and molecules) that are constantly vibrating, rotating, or moving. 

- **Heat (Thermal Energy)** is a measure of the *total* internal energy (both kinetic and potential) of all the vibrating molecules in a body. It depends on the size or mass of the substance.
- **Temperature** is a measure of the *average* kinetic energy of the molecules (the degree of hotness or coldness). It does not depend on the mass. 

If you have a cup of boiling water and a large bucket of boiling water, both are at $100^\circ\text{C}$ (they have the same temperature). However, the bucket contains much more heat energy because it has far more water molecules. If you were to melt ice, the bucket of boiling water could melt significantly more ice than the cup.

*Historical note:* Older textbooks and questions may sometimes measure heat in **calories**. One calorie ($1\ \text{cal}$) is the amount of heat needed to raise $1\ \text{g}$ of water by $1^\circ\text{C}$. The mechanical equivalent of heat establishes that $1\ \text{cal} \approx 4.184\ \text{J}$. In JAMB, you will use Joules.

## Definition of Heat Capacity and Specific Heat Capacity

**Objective/Content item:** (ii) definition of heat capacity and specific heat capacity of solids and liquids.

When heat is added to a solid or a liquid, its temperature usually rises. (The only time temperature does not rise when heat is added is during a change of state, such as melting or boiling. This involves *latent heat*, which is covered fully in `A14-change-of-state.md`). However, different substances require entirely different amounts of heat to achieve the same temperature rise. 

### Heat Capacity ($C$)
**Heat capacity** (sometimes called *thermal capacity*) is defined as the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of an entire body or system by $1\ \text{K}$ (or $1^\circ\text{C}$).
- **Formula:** $C = \frac{Q}{\Delta\theta}$
- **Where:** 
  - $Q$ is the heat energy supplied in Joules ($\text{J}$).
  - $\Delta\theta$ is the change in temperature in Kelvin ($\text{K}$) or degrees Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$).
  - $C$ is the heat capacity.
- **Unit:** Joules per Kelvin ($\text{J K}^{-1}$) or Joules per degree Celsius ($\text{J}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$).

Because heat capacity applies to the *entire* object, it depends strongly on the mass. A heavy copper block has a higher heat capacity than a light copper wire.

### Specific Heat Capacity ($c$)
To compare the thermal properties of different materials fairly, physicists use **specific heat capacity**. The word "specific" in physics usually means "per unit mass". 

**Specific heat capacity** is defined as the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of exactly **$1\ \text{kg}$ mass** of a substance by $1\ \text{K}$ (or $1^\circ\text{C}$). 
- **Formula:** $c = \frac{Q}{m \Delta\theta}$ 
- **Standard working formula:** 
  $$Q = m c \Delta\theta$$
- **Where:** 
  - $m$ is the mass in kilograms ($\text{kg}$).
  - $c$ is the specific heat capacity.
  - $\Delta\theta$ is the temperature change ($\text{K}$ or $^\circ\text{C}$).
- **Unit:** Joules per kilogram per Kelvin ($\text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$).

Specific heat capacity is a fixed physical property of a material. Every pure piece of copper, regardless of its size, has a specific heat capacity of about $400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$. 

### Common Specific Heat Capacities
JAMB will usually provide the specific heat capacity values in the question, but being familiar with them helps spot unrealistic answers:
- **Water (liquid):** $4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- **Ice (solid water):** $2100\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- **Aluminium:** $900\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- **Iron:** $460\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- **Copper:** $400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- **Lead:** $130\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$

*Notice how high the specific heat capacity of water is.* It takes a massive amount of energy ($4200\ \text{J}$) to raise just $1\ \text{kg}$ of water by one degree. 

**Applications of water's high specific heat capacity:**
1. **Engine Coolants:** Water is used in car radiators because it can absorb a massive amount of heat from the engine without its temperature rising too dangerously high.
2. **Climate (Land and Sea Breezes):** Coastal regions (like Lagos) have milder climates than inland regions (like Kano). Because the sea (water) has a high specific heat capacity, it takes a long time to heat up during the day and a long time to cool down at night. The land (rocks, sand) has a low specific heat capacity, so it heats up and cools down very quickly, driving convection currents (breezes).

## Differentiate Between Heat Capacity and Specific Heat Capacity

JAMB tests the distinction between these two concepts frequently. Memorise this comparison table:

| Feature | Heat Capacity ($C$) | Specific Heat Capacity ($c$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Heat required to raise the temperature of an *entire specific object* by $1\ \text{K}$. | Heat required to raise the temperature of exactly *$1\ \text{kg}$* of a material by $1\ \text{K}$. |
| **Dependence on Mass** | Yes. It increases if the mass of the object increases. | No. It is a constant property of the specific material regardless of size. |
| **Formulas** | $C = \frac{Q}{\Delta\theta}$ | $c = \frac{Q}{m \Delta\theta}$ |
| **Mathematical Relationship** | $C = m \times c$ | $c = \frac{C}{m}$ |
| **SI Unit** | $\text{J K}^{-1}$ | $\text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ |
| **Example** | "The heat capacity of this particular iron pot is $4600\ \text{J K}^{-1}$." | "The specific heat capacity of iron is $460\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$." |

## Principles of Simple Methods for Heat Determination

To determine the heat capacity or specific heat capacity of a substance, we rely on the **Law of Conservation of Energy**. We assume that in a closed, insulated system, thermal energy is simply transferred, not destroyed. 

### The Calorimeter
In all these experiments, we use a device called a **calorimeter**. A standard calorimeter is a small cylindrical vessel typically made of **copper** or **aluminium** because they are excellent conductors (they quickly reach thermal equilibrium with the liquid inside them) and they have a relatively low specific heat capacity (they don't "steal" too much heat from the experiment).

**Precautions to prevent heat loss in a calorimeter:**
1. **Lagging (Insulation):** The calorimeter is placed inside a larger container and surrounded by insulating materials like cotton wool, felt, or polystyrene to prevent heat loss by conduction and convection.
2. **Polishing:** The outside of the calorimeter is highly polished (shiny) to reduce heat loss by radiation.
3. **Lid:** An insulating lid covers the top to stop evaporation and hot air currents (convection) from escaping.
4. **Stirrer:** A copper stirrer is used to mix the liquid continuously so that the temperature is uniform throughout.

### 1. Method of Mixtures
This method involves mixing a hot substance with a cold substance. By the principle of conservation of thermal energy, if no heat escapes to the surroundings:
**Heat lost by the hot body = Heat gained by the cold body (and the calorimeter).**

### 2. Electrical Method
Here, an electrical immersion heater is used to heat a liquid or a solid. The electrical energy supplied by the heater is converted entirely into heat energy.
**Electrical energy supplied = Heat energy gained by the substance (and the calorimeter).**

### 3. Newton's Law of Cooling
**Newton's Law of Cooling** states that the rate at which a body loses heat to its surroundings is directly proportional to the temperature difference (excess temperature) between the body and the surroundings, provided this temperature difference is small. 
- **Mathematical expression:** $\frac{dQ}{dt} \propto -(\theta - \theta_s)$
- Where $\theta$ is the temperature of the body and $\theta_s$ is the temperature of the surroundings.

This law is used in an experiment to compare the specific heat capacities of two liquids. If identical volumes of two different liquids are placed in two perfectly identical calorimeters (same mass, same material, same polished surface) and heated to the same high temperature, they will both lose heat at the *exact same rate* under identical conditions. 

By timing how long it takes liquid 1 ($t_1$) and liquid 2 ($t_2$) to cool through the *same temperature range* (e.g., from $70^\circ\text{C}$ to $60^\circ\text{C}$), we can equate their rates of heat loss:
$$\text{Rate of heat loss} = \frac{\text{Total Heat Lost}}{\text{Time taken}}$$
$$\frac{(m_1 c_1 + C_c)\Delta\theta}{t_1} = \frac{(m_2 c_2 + C_c)\Delta\theta}{t_2}$$
Since the temperature drop $\Delta\theta$ is the same, it cancels out, giving:
$$\frac{m_1 c_1 + C_c}{t_1} = \frac{m_2 c_2 + C_c}{t_2}$$
Where $C_c$ is the heat capacity of the identical calorimeters. 

## How to Determine Heat Capacity and Specific Heat Capacity

Below are the experimental steps and the exact equations you must use to "determine" the values in exam questions.

### Determining Specific Heat Capacity by the Method of Mixtures (Solid in Liquid)

[IMAGE: Diagram of the method of mixtures apparatus. A well-insulated copper calorimeter containing water and a thermometer, with a hot metal block being dropped into it from a thread. Labels: "insulating lid", "lagging (cotton wool)", "copper calorimeter", "water", "thermometer", "stirrer", "hot solid block". Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**Procedure:**
1. Weigh the solid block to find its mass ($m_s$).
2. Suspend the solid in a beaker of boiling water and heat it until it reaches a steady initial high temperature ($\theta_1$, usually $100^\circ\text{C}$).
3. Weigh an empty calorimeter together with its stirrer to find its mass ($m_c$).
4. Pour cold water into the calorimeter and reweigh it to find the mass of the water ($m_w$). 
5. Record the initial steady temperature of the water and calorimeter ($\theta_2$).
6. **Quickly** transfer the hot solid from the boiling water into the calorimeter. (The transfer must be fast to minimize heat lost to the air while moving it).
7. Stir the mixture continuously to ensure even heat distribution and record the highest final steady temperature reached ($\theta_3$).

**The Equation:**
Assuming no heat is lost to the surroundings:
$$\text{Heat lost by solid} = \text{Heat gained by water} + \text{Heat gained by calorimeter}$$
$$m_s c_s (\theta_1 - \theta_3) = m_w c_w (\theta_3 - \theta_2) + m_c c_c (\theta_3 - \theta_2)$$
Where $c_w$ and $c_c$ are the specific heat capacities of water and the calorimeter material (e.g., copper). From this, $c_s$ can be calculated.

### Determining Specific Heat Capacity by the Electrical Method (For a Liquid)

[IMAGE: Diagram of the electrical method for determining specific heat capacity of a liquid. An insulated calorimeter containing a liquid. Inside the liquid is an electrical immersion heater connected to a DC power supply, a switch, an ammeter in series, and a voltmeter in parallel. A thermometer and a stirrer are also in the liquid. Labels: "thermometer", "stirrer", "immersion heater", "liquid", "lagged calorimeter", "V", "A". Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

**Procedure:**
1. Measure the mass of the liquid ($m$) and place it in a lagged calorimeter.
2. Insert an electrical heating coil, a thermometer, and a stirrer.
3. Record the initial temperature of the liquid ($\theta_1$).
4. Switch on the heater and simultaneously start a stopwatch. Record the current ($I$) on the ammeter and voltage ($V$) on the voltmeter.
5. Let the heater run for a measured time $t$ (in seconds), stirring continuously.
6. Switch off the heater and record the highest final temperature ($\theta_2$).

**The Equation:**
$$\text{Electrical Energy Supplied} = \text{Heat gained by liquid} + \text{Heat gained by calorimeter}$$
Electrical energy $E = \text{Power} \times \text{time} = P t = I V t$.
$$I V t = m c (\theta_2 - \theta_1) + C_c (\theta_2 - \theta_1)$$
*(Note: $C_c$ is the heat capacity of the calorimeter. If a question says the container has "negligible heat capacity", you set $C_c$ to zero, leaving just $I V t = m c \Delta\theta$).*

*Modification for a solid block:* To find the specific heat capacity of a solid electrically, a solid cylindrical block of the material is drilled with two holes. The heater is placed in one hole and a thermometer in the other. A few drops of oil are added to the holes to improve thermal contact. 

## Solving Numerical Problems on Quantity of Heat

### Common Mistakes to Avoid in Calculations
- **Wrong Time Unit:** In the electrical method ($E = Pt$), time $t$ MUST be in seconds. Multiplying Power by minutes will give an answer that is 60 times too small.
- **Wrong Mass Unit:** Masses must be in kilograms ($\text{kg}$). If given $50\ \text{g}$, convert it to $0.05\ \text{kg}$ by dividing by $1000$.
- **Ignoring the Container:** In mixtures, heat is gained by both the cold liquid *and* the container (calorimeter). Always check if the question gives you the mass or heat capacity of the container. If it doesn't, it will usually state "neglect heat capacity of the vessel."
- **Unnecessary Temperature Conversions:** The symbol $\Delta\theta$ means *change* in temperature. Because the size of 1 degree Celsius is identical to the size of 1 Kelvin, a difference of $20^\circ\text{C}$ is exactly a difference of $20\ \text{K}$. You do **not** need to add 273 to the temperature *difference*.

**Example 1: Basic Heat Capacity Calculation**
A block of copper has a mass of $4.0\ \text{kg}$. If the specific heat capacity of copper is $400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, calculate its heat capacity.

**Working:**
1. Identify the given values: mass $m = 4.0\ \text{kg}$, specific heat capacity $c = 400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
2. State the formula linking heat capacity and specific heat capacity: $C = m \times c$.
3. Substitute the values: $C = 4.0\ \text{kg} \times 400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1} = 1600\ \text{J K}^{-1}$.

---

**Example 2: Specific Heat Capacity ($Q = mc\Delta\theta$)**
How much heat energy is required to raise the temperature of $2.5\ \text{kg}$ of water from $25^\circ\text{C}$ to boiling point? (Specific heat capacity of water = $4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$).

**Working:**
1. List the values: $m = 2.5\ \text{kg}$, $c = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
2. Identify initial and final temperatures: $\theta_1 = 25^\circ\text{C}$, $\theta_2 = 100^\circ\text{C}$ (boiling point of water).
3. Find $\Delta\theta$: $\Delta\theta = 100^\circ\text{C} - 25^\circ\text{C} = 75\ \text{K}$.
4. Use the formula: $Q = m c \Delta\theta$.
5. Calculate: $Q = 2.5\ \text{kg} \times 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1} \times 75\ \text{K} = 787,500\ \text{J}$.

---

**Example 3: Method of Mixtures (Solid in Liquid, with calorimeter)**
A hot metal of mass $0.5\ \text{kg}$ at $100^\circ\text{C}$ is dropped into $0.15\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ contained in a copper calorimeter of mass $0.175\ \text{kg}$. The final steady temperature is $30^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal. (Specific heat capacity of water = $4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, specific heat capacity of copper = $400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$).

**Working:**
1. Identify and list the given values:
   - Metal: $m_m = 0.5\ \text{kg}$, initial temp $\theta_m = 100^\circ\text{C}$.
   - Water: $m_w = 0.15\ \text{kg}$, initial temp $\theta_1 = 20^\circ\text{C}$, $c_w = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
   - Calorimeter: $m_c = 0.175\ \text{kg}$, $c_c = 400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
   - Final mixture temp: $\theta_2 = 30^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Calculate the heat gained by the water:
   $Q_w = m_w \times c_w \times (\theta_2 - \theta_1)$
   $Q_w = 0.15\ \text{kg} \times 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1} \times (30 - 20)\ \text{K} = 0.15 \times 4200 \times 10 = 6300\ \text{J}$.
3. Calculate the heat gained by the copper calorimeter:
   $Q_c = m_c \times c_c \times (\theta_2 - \theta_1)$
   $Q_c = 0.175\ \text{kg} \times 400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1} \times (30 - 20)\ \text{K} = 0.175 \times 400 \times 10 = 700\ \text{J}$.
4. Find the total heat gained by the cold system: 
   Total gained = $6300\ \text{J} + 700\ \text{J} = 7000\ \text{J}$.
5. Express the heat lost by the hot metal:
   $Q_m = m_m \times c_m \times (\theta_m - \theta_2) = 0.5 \times c_m \times (100 - 30)$
   $Q_m = 0.5 \times c_m \times 70 = 35 c_m$.
6. Equate heat lost to heat gained and solve for $c_m$:
   $35 c_m = 7000$
   $c_m = \frac{7000}{35} = 200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.

**Example 4: Electrical Method (Heater Efficiency)**
A student uses an electric boiling ring rated $2000\ \text{W}$ to heat $2.0\ \text{kg}$ of water in a plastic bucket (negligible heat capacity). The temperature rises from $30^\circ\text{C}$ to $80^\circ\text{C}$ in $5$ minutes. What is the efficiency of the boiling ring? ($c_w = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$).

**Working:**
1. List the values: $P = 2000\ \text{W}$, $m = 2.0\ \text{kg}$, $\Delta\theta = 80^\circ\text{C} - 30^\circ\text{C} = 50\ \text{K}$, $t = 5\ \text{mins}$.
2. Convert time to seconds: $t = 5 \times 60 = 300\ \text{s}$.
3. Calculate the total electrical energy supplied (Input Energy):
   $E = P \times t = 2000\ \text{W} \times 300\ \text{s} = 600,000\ \text{J}$.
4. Calculate the actual heat energy gained by the water (Output Energy):
   $Q = m \times c_w \times \Delta\theta = 2.0\ \text{kg} \times 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1} \times 50\ \text{K} = 8400 \times 50 = 420,000\ \text{J}$.
5. Calculate efficiency using $\text{Efficiency} = (\frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}}) \times 100\%$:
   $\text{Efficiency} = \frac{420,000}{600,000} \times 100\% = 0.7 \times 100\% = 70\%$.
*(This means 30% of the electrical energy was lost to the environment).*

**Example 5: Mixing Two Quantities of the Same Substance (The JAMB Shortcut)**
During harmattan, a student prepares bathwater by mixing $0.4\ \text{kg}$ of hot water at $90^\circ\text{C}$ with $0.6\ \text{kg}$ of cold water at $40^\circ\text{C}$ in a plastic bowl of negligible heat capacity. What is the final steady temperature of the bathwater?

**Standard Algebraic Method:**
1. Let the final temperature be $\theta$.
2. Heat lost by hot water = $0.4 \times 4200 \times (90 - \theta)$.
3. Heat gained by cold water = $0.6 \times 4200 \times (\theta - 40)$.
4. Equate them: $0.4 \times 4200 \times (90 - \theta) = 0.6 \times 4200 \times (\theta - 40)$.
5. Cancel out $4200$ from both sides: 
   $0.4(90 - \theta) = 0.6(\theta - 40)$
   $36 - 0.4\theta = 0.6\theta - 24$
   $36 + 24 = 0.6\theta + 0.4\theta$
   $60 = 1.0\theta \implies \theta = 60^\circ\text{C}$.

**JAMB Shortcut for Mixing the Same Substance:**
When you mix two amounts of the *exact same* substance and ignore the container, the final temperature is just the weighted average of the initial temperatures. 
This shortcut is **safe only** when $c$ is the same for both parts!
$$T_{\text{final}} = \frac{m_1 \theta_1 + m_2 \theta_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$
1. Substitute the values directly:
   $T_{\text{final}} = \frac{(0.4 \times 90) + (0.6 \times 40)}{0.4 + 0.6}$
2. Calculate:
   $T_{\text{final}} = \frac{36 + 24}{1.0} = \frac{60}{1} = 60^\circ\text{C}$.
*(Using this shortcut saves you precious time in the exam hall!)*

**Example 6: Using Newton's Law of Cooling to Compare Specific Heat Capacities**
Two identical calorimeters are placed in a room. One contains $0.05\ \text{kg}$ of water and the other contains $0.04\ \text{kg}$ of an unknown liquid X, such that the volumes are equal. Both are heated to $60^\circ\text{C}$. The water takes $10\ \text{minutes}$ to cool down to $50^\circ\text{C}$, while liquid X takes $5\ \text{minutes}$ to cool through the same temperature range. If the heat capacity of each calorimeter is $20\ \text{J K}^{-1}$ and the specific heat capacity of water is $4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, find the specific heat capacity of liquid X.

**Working:**
1. List the values:
   - Water: $m_w = 0.05\ \text{kg}$, $c_w = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, $t_1 = 10\ \text{min}$.
   - Liquid X: $m_x = 0.04\ \text{kg}$, $c_x = ?$, $t_2 = 5\ \text{min}$.
   - Calorimeter heat capacity: $C_c = 20\ \text{J K}^{-1}$.
2. State the Newton's Law of Cooling formula for comparing rates:
   $$\frac{m_x c_x + C_c}{t_2} = \frac{m_w c_w + C_c}{t_1}$$
3. Substitute the values:
   $$\frac{0.04 c_x + 20}{5} = \frac{(0.05 \times 4200) + 20}{10}$$
4. Simplify the right side: 
   $(0.05 \times 4200) = 210$. 
   $210 + 20 = 230$.
   Right side $= \frac{230}{10} = 23$.
5. Solve for $c_x$:
   $\frac{0.04 c_x + 20}{5} = 23$
   $0.04 c_x + 20 = 23 \times 5 = 115$
   $0.04 c_x = 115 - 20 = 95$
   $c_x = \frac{95}{0.04} = 2375\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Heat Energy ($Q$)** | A form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder body due to a temperature difference. |
| **Heat Capacity ($C$)** | The heat energy required to raise the temperature of a *whole body* by $1\ \text{K}$ (or $1^\circ\text{C}$). |
| **Specific Heat Capacity ($c$)** | The heat energy required to raise the temperature of exactly $1\ \text{kg}$ of a substance by $1\ \text{K}$. |
| **Calorimeter** | A well-insulated metal vessel (often copper) used in experiments to measure quantities of heat. |
| **Method of Mixtures** | An experimental technique relying on conservation of energy where a hot body is mixed with a cold body. |
| **Newton's Law of Cooling** | States that the rate of heat loss of a body is directly proportional to its excess temperature over the surroundings. |
| **Lagging** | Insulating material (like felt or cotton wool) wrapped around a calorimeter to prevent heat loss to the environment. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Mixing up $C$ and $c$:** Capital $C$ is for Heat Capacity (depends on mass), and small $c$ is for specific heat capacity (a constant for the material).
- **Time conversions:** In the formula $Pt = mc\Delta\theta$, time $t$ MUST be in seconds. Multiplying power by minutes is a guaranteed way to pick a wrong "distractor" JAMB option.
- **Forgetting the calorimeter:** In the method of mixtures, the cold liquid *and* the container both gain heat. Do not leave the container out of your equation unless the question explicitly says "negligible heat capacity".
- **Transfer speed:** When describing precautions for the method of mixtures, transferring the hot solid *quickly* is necessary to stop it from losing heat to the air. Transferring it "slowly to avoid splashing" is incorrect and is a common trick option.
- **Cooling rate comparison:** Newton's Law of cooling compares two liquids of *equal volumes*, not equal masses. This ensures they expose the exact same surface area inside identical calorimeters.
- **Temperature differences:** You do not need to convert degrees Celsius to Kelvin when finding a difference, because a difference of $20^\circ\text{C}$ is exactly a difference of $20\ \text{K}$. 

## Quick summary

1. Heat is a form of internal kinetic and potential energy measured in Joules. It flows from a region of high temperature to a region of low temperature.
2. Heat capacity ($C$) is the heat required to raise the temperature of an entire object by $1\ \text{K}$. Formula: $C = m \times c$. Unit: $\text{J K}^{-1}$.
3. Specific heat capacity ($c$) is the heat required to raise the temperature of $1\ \text{kg}$ of a substance by $1\ \text{K}$. Formula: $Q = m c \Delta\theta$. Unit: $\text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
4. Water has a very high specific heat capacity ($4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$), making it excellent for cooling engines and moderating coastal climates.
5. The method of mixtures relies on the principle that Heat Lost = Heat Gained, provided the system is lagged (insulated) to stop heat escaping.
6. In the electrical method, electrical energy supplied ($Pt$ or $IVt$) equals the heat gained by the substance ($m c \Delta\theta$) plus any heat gained by the calorimeter.
7. Newton's law of cooling states the rate of heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference between a body and its surroundings. It can be used to compare specific heat capacities by timing how long identical calorimeters take to cool.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following statements best describes heat?**
- A. It is a measure of the average kinetic energy of molecules.
- B. It is a form of energy that flows from a higher temperature to a lower temperature.
- C. It is the amount of work a body can do when expanded.
- D. It is a measure of the total volume of a body.

**Answer: B.** Heat is thermal energy in transit, transferring from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature. Option A defines temperature.

**2. Which of the following is true concerning heat capacity and specific heat capacity?**
- A. Heat capacity depends on mass, while specific heat capacity does not.
- B. Both depend heavily on the mass of the substance.
- C. Specific heat capacity depends on mass, while heat capacity does not.
- D. Heat capacity is measured in $\text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.

**Answer: A.** Heat capacity ($C$) is the total heat required for the whole body, so it varies with mass. Specific heat capacity ($c$) is fixed for $1\ \text{kg}$ of a specific material, so it is independent of the body's total mass.

**3. A $100\ \text{W}$ electrical heater is used to heat $0.5\ \text{kg}$ of a substance. The temperature rises by $20\ \text{K}$ in $2\ \text{minutes}$. Assuming no heat loss, calculate the specific heat capacity of the substance.**
- A. $10\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- B. $20\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- C. $1200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- D. $24000\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Identify the values: $P = 100\ \text{W}$, $m = 0.5\ \text{kg}$, $\Delta\theta = 20\ \text{K}$, $t = 2\ \text{minutes}$.
2. Convert time to seconds: $t = 2 \times 60 = 120\ \text{s}$.
3. Calculate electrical energy supplied: $E = P \times t = 100 \times 120 = 12000\ \text{J}$.
4. Equate to heat gained ($Q = m c \Delta\theta$): $12000 = 0.5 \times c \times 20$.
5. Solve for $c$: $12000 = 10 \times c \implies c = 1200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
Mistake B occurs if you forget to convert $2\ \text{minutes}$ into seconds (using $t=2$).

**4. $0.4\ \text{kg}$ of a liquid at $90^\circ\text{C}$ is mixed with $0.6\ \text{kg}$ of the identical liquid at $40^\circ\text{C}$ in a vessel of negligible heat capacity. What is the final temperature of the mixture?**
- A. $30^\circ\text{C}$
- B. $60^\circ\text{C}$
- C. $65^\circ\text{C}$
- D. $100^\circ\text{C}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Since the liquids are identical and the vessel is ignored, use the shortcut: $T_{\text{final}} = \frac{m_1 \theta_1 + m_2 \theta_2}{m_1 + m_2}$.
2. Substitute the values: $T_{\text{final}} = \frac{(0.4 \times 90) + (0.6 \times 40)}{0.4 + 0.6}$.
3. Calculate: $T_{\text{final}} = \frac{36 + 24}{1.0} = 60^\circ\text{C}$.
Mistake C comes from just taking the simple average of the temperatures ($130 \div 2 = 65^\circ\text{C}$), which ignores the fact that there is more of the colder liquid.

**5. Newton's law of cooling states that the rate of heat loss of a body is directly proportional to the**
- A. mass of the body
- B. temperature of the surroundings
- C. temperature difference between the body and its surroundings
- D. specific heat capacity of the body

**Answer: C.** Newton's law of cooling explicitly links the cooling rate to the excess temperature (the temperature difference) between the hot body and its cooler environment.

**6. The SI unit of specific heat capacity is**
- A. $\text{J K}^{-1}$
- B. $\text{J kg}^{-1}$
- C. $\text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- D. $\text{kg K}^{-1}$

**Answer: C.** It is defined as the heat ($\text{J}$) per unit mass ($\text{kg}$) per unit temperature change ($\text{K}$), resulting in $\text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$. Option A is the unit for heat capacity.

**7. Calculate the heat capacity of a copper block of mass $4.0\ \text{kg}$ if the specific heat capacity of copper is $400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.**
- A. $0.01\ \text{J K}^{-1}$
- B. $100\ \text{J K}^{-1}$
- C. $404\ \text{J K}^{-1}$
- D. $1600\ \text{J K}^{-1}$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Use the formula $C = m \times c$.
2. Multiply the mass and specific heat capacity: $C = 4.0\ \text{kg} \times 400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1} = 1600\ \text{J K}^{-1}$.

**8. When determining specific heat capacity using the method of mixtures, which of the following precautions is NOT correct?**
- A. The calorimeter should be well lagged.
- B. The mixture should be stirred continuously.
- C. The hot solid should be transferred slowly to avoid splashing water.
- D. The hot solid should be transferred as quickly as possible.

**Answer: C.** The hot solid must be transferred *quickly* from the heat source into the calorimeter to minimize the amount of heat it loses to the surrounding air during the transfer. Transferring it slowly leads to a large heat loss and an inaccurate result.

**9. $0.1\ \text{kg}$ of a hot metal at $100^\circ\text{C}$ is dropped into $0.1\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ contained in a calorimeter of heat capacity $180\ \text{J K}^{-1}$. If the final steady temperature is $25^\circ\text{C}$, find the specific heat capacity of the metal. (Specific heat capacity of water = $4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$).**
- A. $280\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- B. $304\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- C. $375\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
- D. $400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$

**Answer: D.** 
1. Heat gained by water = $m_w \times c_w \times \Delta\theta = 0.1 \times 4200 \times (25 - 20) = 2100\ \text{J}$.
2. Heat gained by calorimeter = $C \times \Delta\theta = 180 \times (25 - 20) = 900\ \text{J}$.
3. Total heat gained = $2100 + 900 = 3000\ \text{J}$.
4. Heat lost by metal = $m_m \times c_m \times \Delta\theta = 0.1 \times c_m \times (100 - 25) = 7.5 \times c_m$.
5. Equate: $7.5 c_m = 3000 \implies c_m = \frac{3000}{7.5} = 400\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
Mistake A comes from completely ignoring the $900\ \text{J}$ heat gained by the calorimeter.

**10. An electric kettle rated $2000\ \text{W}$ operates for $5\ \text{minutes}$ to heat $2.0\ \text{kg}$ of water from $30^\circ\text{C}$ to $80^\circ\text{C}$. What is the efficiency of the kettle? (Specific heat capacity of water = $4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$).**
- A. $42\%$
- B. $70\%$
- C. $112\%$
- D. $4200\%$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Input energy = $P \times t = 2000 \times (5 \times 60) = 600,000\ \text{J}$.
2. Output heat = $m \times c_w \times \Delta\theta = 2.0 \times 4200 \times (80 - 30) = 8400 \times 50 = 420,000\ \text{J}$.
3. Efficiency = $(\frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}}) \times 100\% = (\frac{420,000}{600,000}) \times 100\% = 70\%$.
Mistake C comes from incorrectly using the final temperature ($80^\circ\text{C}$) instead of the temperature change ($50^\circ\text{C}$) to find the output energy.
