## Saturated and Unsaturated Vapours

**Objective i: distinguish between saturated and unsaturated vapours.**
**Content i: unsaturated and saturated vapours.**

To understand vapours, we must first look at **evaporation**. According to the kinetic theory of matter, molecules in a liquid are in constant, random motion but have different kinetic energies. Even at room temperature, some molecules near the surface possess enough kinetic energy to overcome the attractive forces of their neighbours and escape into the air. This process is called evaporation, and the gaseous state of a substance that is normally a liquid or solid at room temperature is called a **vapour**. 

When a liquid, such as petrol in a jerrycan or water in a bucket, is left open to the atmosphere, the escaping molecules drift away, and the liquid eventually dries up completely.

However, if you place the liquid in a **closed container**, the vapour molecules cannot escape. They bounce around in the space above the liquid. As they move, some of them strike the surface of the liquid and are recaptured. This returning process is called **condensation**. 

As evaporation continues, the concentration of vapour molecules in the space increases, which in turn increases the rate of condensation. Eventually, a state of **dynamic equilibrium** is reached. At this point, the number of molecules leaving the liquid per second (rate of evaporation) exactly equals the number of molecules returning to the liquid per second (rate of condensation). 

Based on this, we classify vapours into two main types:

| Feature | Unsaturated Vapour | Saturated Vapour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | A vapour that is not in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid. It can still accommodate more vapour molecules at that temperature. | A vapour that is in dynamic equilibrium with its own liquid. It holds the maximum possible amount of vapour at that temperature. |
| **Presence of liquid** | It is usually not in contact with its own liquid (all liquid has evaporated). | It is always in contact with some of its own unvaporized liquid. |
| **Gas laws** | It approximately obeys the standard gas laws (Boyle’s Law, Charles’s Law). | It does **NOT** obey the gas laws. |
| **Effect of volume change (at constant temperature)** | If you compress the space, its pressure increases (similar to Boyle's law). | If you compress the space, its pressure remains **constant**. The excess vapour molecules simply condense back into the liquid phase. |
| **Pressure exerted** | It exerts a pressure less than the maximum possible pressure at that temperature. | It exerts the maximum possible pressure for that temperature, known as the **Saturated Vapour Pressure (S.V.P)**. |

## Saturated Vapour Pressure (S.V.P) and Boiling

**Objective ii: relate saturated vapour pressure to boiling point.**
**Content ii: relationship between saturated vapour pressure (S.V.P) and boiling.**

The **Saturated Vapour Pressure (S.V.P)** of a liquid is the maximum pressure exerted by its vapour when the vapour is in dynamic equilibrium with its own liquid in a closed system at a given temperature. (Note: Some textbooks might refer to S.V.P as "vapour tension," but S.V.P is the standard JAMB terminology).

Two vital properties of S.V.P that frequently appear in JAMB exams are:
1. **It increases with temperature:** Heating a liquid gives its molecules more kinetic energy. More molecules escape into the vapour phase, increasing the concentration of the vapour and the pressure it exerts.
2. **It is entirely independent of volume:** Changing the volume of the container does not change the S.V.P. If you halve the volume at a constant temperature, the pressure does not double; instead, half of the vapour condenses, and the pressure stays exactly the same.

### How S.V.P relates to Boiling

Evaporation happens only at the surface of a liquid and at any temperature. **Boiling**, on the other hand, is a violent process that occurs throughout the entire volume of the liquid, but only at a specific temperature. 

**A liquid begins to boil exactly when its Saturated Vapour Pressure becomes equal to the external atmospheric pressure.**

When you heat water, its S.V.P rises. At room temperature, the S.V.P of water is low (around $17\ \text{mmHg}$). The external atmospheric pressure pressing down on the liquid (usually around $760\ \text{mmHg}$) crushes any vapour bubbles that try to form inside the liquid. 
As the temperature reaches $100^\circ\text{C}$, the S.V.P of the water reaches $760\ \text{mmHg}$. Now, the pressure of the vapour inside the liquid is strong enough to push against the atmosphere. Bubbles of vapour form freely throughout the liquid and rise to the top. The liquid is boiling.

This relationship explains why boiling points change depending on your environment:
- **At high altitudes (e.g., the Mambilla Plateau or Jos in Nigeria):** The atmospheric pressure is lower than at sea level. Because the external pressure is lower, the water's S.V.P reaches this lower target at a lower temperature. Water might boil at $95^\circ\text{C}$ instead of $100^\circ\text{C}$. This means food cooks slower because the boiling water is not as hot.
- **In a pressure cooker:** The steam generated is trapped inside the sealed pot, vastly increasing the pressure above the liquid. The water must reach a much higher temperature (e.g., $120^\circ\text{C}$) before its S.V.P can match the high internal pressure to boil. This higher boiling temperature cooks tough meat (like cow leg) much faster.

## Determination of S.V.P by the Barometer Tube Method

**Objective iii: determine S.V.P by barometer tube method.**
**Content iii: determination of S.V.P by barometer tube method.**

To measure the S.V.P of a volatile liquid (like ether, ethanol, or water) at room temperature, physicists use a straightforward laboratory setup involving a mercury barometer.

**Apparatus and Procedure:**
1. A thick glass tube (about $1\ \text{m}$ long) is filled completely with mercury, closed with a thumb, and inverted into a heavy dish (trough) of mercury. When the thumb is removed, the mercury column drops to a height $H$ (usually around $760\ \text{mmHg}$), leaving a vacuum at the top known as the **Torricellian vacuum**.
2. Using a specially curved pipette, a few drops of the test liquid (e.g., ether) are introduced into the bottom of the tube, under the mercury in the trough.
3. Because the liquid is far less dense than mercury, it floats swiftly up the tube and enters the Torricellian vacuum.
4. Once in the vacuum, the liquid evaporates instantly. The vapour molecules exert a downward pressure on the mercury, forcing the mercury column down to a new, lower height, $h_1$. Since all the liquid has evaporated, this vapour is **unsaturated**.
5. More drops of the liquid are introduced until a thin, visible layer of unvaporized liquid remains resting on top of the mercury column. 
6. Because liquid is now present and in equilibrium with the vapour, the space is completely saturated. The mercury level drops to a final steady height, $h$. Adding more liquid will not push the mercury down any further; it will just add to the liquid layer.

**Calculation:**
The S.V.P is the pressure the saturated vapour exerts to push the mercury column down from its original atmospheric height. It is calculated as the difference between the initial barometric height and the final barometric height:

$$ \text{S.V.P} = H - h $$

*(Where $H$ is the initial atmospheric pressure head and $h$ is the final mercury height. The unit is typically $\text{mmHg}$ or $\text{cmHg}$).*

## Formation of Dew, Mist, Fog, Cloud and Rain

**Content iv: formation of dew, mist, fog, cloud and rain.**

The atmosphere constantly holds water vapour. When warm, moist air cools, it eventually reaches a temperature where it can no longer hold all its moisture. The excess vapour condenses back into liquid water. Depending on where and how this condensation happens, different weather phenomena occur:

- **Dew:** During clear nights, the ground, grass, and parked cars radiate their heat and cool down rapidly. The layer of air directly touching these cold surfaces is chilled. If it cools enough, it becomes saturated, and water vapour condenses directly onto the surfaces as water droplets. This is very common in Nigeria during the early mornings of the rainy season or Harmattan.
- **Mist and Fog:** Sometimes, a large mass of air close to the ground cools down. The water vapour condenses on tiny suspended dust particles or smoke in the air, creating a low-hanging cloud. 
  - **Mist** contains slightly larger droplets and is less dense, slightly blurring vision.
  - **Fog** is much thicker, denser, and severely reduces visibility. Thick Harmattan fog in northern cities like Kano or Sokoto often causes flight cancellations.
- **Cloud:** When warm, moist air rises high into the upper atmosphere, the pressure drops, causing the air to expand and cool. Once it cools past its saturation point, the vapour condenses on atmospheric dust particles to form tiny water droplets or ice crystals high in the sky. A cloud is essentially fog at a high altitude.
- **Rain:** Inside a cloud, tiny water droplets move around and collide, merging to form larger, heavier drops. Once these drops become too heavy to be supported by rising air currents, gravity pulls them down to the earth as rain.

## Dew Point, Humidity and Relative Humidity

**Objective iv: differentiate between dew point, humidity and relative humidity.**
**Content v: study of dew point, humidity and relative humidity.**

To describe how much water vapour is in the air, physicists and meteorologists use three related but distinct terms. It is essential to differentiate between them clearly.

| Feature | Humidity (Absolute) | Relative Humidity (R.H.) | Dew Point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | The actual mass of water vapour present in a unit volume of the air. | The ratio of the mass of water vapour present in a given volume of air to the maximum mass required to completely saturate it at the same temperature. | The specific temperature to which air must be cooled for the water vapour currently present to just saturate it. |
| **Type of Measurement** | An absolute quantity (measured in $\text{kg m}^{-3}$ or $\text{g m}^{-3}$). | A ratio expressed as a percentage ($\%$). It has no physical unit. | A temperature (measured in $^\circ\text{C}$ or $\text{K}$). |
| **Everyday Context** | Tells us exactly how much water is in the air. | High R.H. (e.g., $90\%$ in Lagos) means sweat evaporates slowly, making you feel sticky and hot. Low R.H. (e.g., $15\%$ in Kano during Harmattan) causes quick evaporation, drying the skin and cracking lips, which is why people use shea butter. | If the air temperature drops to the dew point, Relative Humidity hits $100\%$. If it drops *below* the dew point, condensation (dew, fog) begins. |

**Formula for Relative Humidity (R.H.):**
By applying the gas laws to the unsaturated water vapour in the air, it can be proven that the mass of water vapour is proportional to the pressure it exerts. Therefore, Relative Humidity can be calculated using pressures:

$$ \text{R.H.} = \frac{\text{Partial pressure of water vapour in air at temperature } T}{\text{S.V.P of water at temperature } T} \times 100\% $$

The "partial pressure of water vapour currently in the air" is, by definition, exactly equal to the S.V.P of water at the dew point. Thus, the most useful and commonly tested formula in JAMB is:

$$ \text{R.H.} = \frac{\text{S.V.P of water at the dew point}}{\text{S.V.P of water at the room temperature}} \times 100\% $$

## Estimating Humidity: The Wet and Dry Bulb Hygrometer

**Objective v (Syllabus vi): estimate the humidity of the atmosphere using wet and dry bulb hygrometers.**
**Content vi: hygrometry; estimation of the humidity of the atmosphere using wet and dry bulb hygrometers.**

**Hygrometry** is the scientific measurement of atmospheric humidity. While there are several types of hygrometers (like the hair hygrometer or Regnault's hygrometer), the most common instrument used in laboratories and weather stations is the **wet and dry bulb hygrometer** (also called a psychrometer).

**How it works:**
1. The instrument consists of two identical standard thermometers mounted side by side on a board.
2. The **dry bulb thermometer** has its mercury or alcohol bulb exposed directly to the air. It measures the actual room temperature.
3. The **wet bulb thermometer** has its bulb wrapped in a piece of fine muslin cloth. The tail of this cloth dips into a small reservoir of distilled water, drawing water up by capillary action to keep the bulb constantly wet.
4. The water on the wet muslin evaporates into the surrounding air. Because evaporation requires energy (latent heat of vaporization), it draws this heat from the thermometer bulb itself. This causes the bulb to cool down. 
5. Consequently, **the wet bulb thermometer always records a lower temperature than the dry bulb thermometer** (unless the air is fully saturated).
6. The difference in reading between the dry bulb and the wet bulb is called the **temperature depression**.
7. The rate of evaporation—and thus the amount of cooling—depends entirely on how dry the surrounding air is:
   - **Dry air (Low Relative Humidity):** Evaporation happens very fast. The wet bulb cools significantly, resulting in a **large** temperature depression.
   - **Humid air (High Relative Humidity):** The air is already full of moisture, so evaporation is slow. Cooling is minimal, resulting in a **small** temperature depression.
   - **Saturated air ($100\%$ R.H.):** The air can hold no more moisture. Evaporation stops entirely, and both thermometers show the **exact same reading**.
8. To estimate the actual relative humidity, a user records the dry bulb temperature and the temperature depression, and then cross-references these two numbers on a standard chart known as **hygrometric tables**.

## Solving Numerical Problems on Vapours

**Objective vi (Syllabus vii): solve numerical problems.**

JAMB tests specific calculation skills in this topic. The three most common mathematical scenarios involve the barometer tube, relative humidity, and gases collected over water.

### 1. The Barometer Tube Calculation
This requires finding the S.V.P by observing how much a saturated vapour pushes down a mercury column.

**Worked Example 1:**
A standard mercury barometer reads $760\ \text{mmHg}$. When a few drops of ether are introduced into the Torricellian vacuum above the mercury, the liquid completely vaporizes. More ether is steadily added until a thin layer of liquid is observed resting on top of the mercury column, and the mercury level settles at $320\ \text{mmHg}$. Calculate the saturated vapour pressure of ether at the room temperature.

**Working:**
1. Identify the formula for the barometer method: $\text{S.V.P} = H - h$.
2. Substitute the values with their units: The initial atmospheric height $H = 760\ \text{mmHg}$, and the final height when the vapour is saturated $h = 320\ \text{mmHg}$.
3. Calculate: $\text{S.V.P} = 760 - 320 = 440$.
4. State the final answer: The S.V.P of the ether is $440\ \text{mmHg}$.

### 2. Relative Humidity Calculation
This applies the ratio of S.V.P at the dew point to S.V.P at room temperature.

**Worked Example 2:**
The saturated vapour pressure of water at $15^\circ\text{C}$ is $12.8\ \text{mmHg}$ and at $30^\circ\text{C}$ is $31.8\ \text{mmHg}$. If the temperature of a laboratory is $30^\circ\text{C}$ and its dew point is determined to be $15^\circ\text{C}$, calculate the relative humidity of the air in the laboratory.

**Working:**
1. Identify the relative humidity formula: 
   $$ \text{R.H.} = \left( \frac{\text{S.V.P at dew point}}{\text{S.V.P at room temperature}} \right) \times 100\% $$
2. Substitute the correct values: The room temperature is $30^\circ\text{C}$, and the S.V.P there is $31.8\ \text{mmHg}$. The dew point is $15^\circ\text{C}$, and the S.V.P there is $12.8\ \text{mmHg}$.
3. Calculate: 
   $$ \text{R.H.} = \frac{12.8\ \text{mmHg}}{31.8\ \text{mmHg}} \times 100\% $$
   $$ \text{R.H.} = 0.4025 \times 100\% = 40.25\% $$
4. State the final answer: The relative humidity of the laboratory is $40.25\%$.

### 3. Gas Collected Over Water (Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures)
When a gas (like oxygen or hydrogen) is prepared in a laboratory and collected by displacing water, the gas bubbles through the water. It becomes mixed with water vapour. The total pressure of the wet gas mixture is the sum of the dry gas pressure and the saturated vapour pressure of the water. You **must** subtract the water's S.V.P to find the true pressure of the dry gas before applying the General Gas Law.

**Worked Example 3:**
$200\ \text{cm}^3$ of oxygen gas was collected over water at a temperature of $27^\circ\text{C}$ and a total atmospheric pressure of $800\ \text{mmHg}$. If the saturated vapour pressure of water at $27^\circ\text{C}$ is $26.7\ \text{mmHg}$, calculate the volume of the dry oxygen gas at standard temperature and pressure (s.t.p.). 
*(Standard pressure = $760\ \text{mmHg}$, Standard temperature = $273\ \text{K}$).*

**Working:**
1. Find the pressure of the **dry gas** at the initial conditions by subtracting the S.V.P from the total pressure: 
   $P_{\text{dry}} = P_{\text{total}} - \text{S.V.P} = 800\ \text{mmHg} - 26.7\ \text{mmHg} = 773.3\ \text{mmHg}$.
2. Write down the initial state variables (State 1): 
   $P_1 = 773.3\ \text{mmHg}$
   $V_1 = 200\ \text{cm}^3$
   $T_1 = 27^\circ\text{C} = 27 + 273 = 300\ \text{K}$ *(Temperature must always be converted to Kelvin!)*
3. Write down the final state variables at s.t.p. (State 2):
   $P_2 = 760\ \text{mmHg}$
   $T_2 = 273\ \text{K}$
   $V_2 = ?$
4. Apply the General Gas Law: 
   $$ \frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} $$
5. Rearrange the formula to make $V_2$ the subject:
   $$ V_2 = \frac{P_1 \times V_1 \times T_2}{P_2 \times T_1} $$
6. Substitute the values and calculate:
   $$ V_2 = \frac{773.3 \times 200 \times 273}{760 \times 300} = \frac{422221.8}{228000} \approx 185.19\ \text{cm}^3 $$
7. State the final answer: The volume of the dry oxygen gas at s.t.p. is $185.19\ \text{cm}^3$.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |

| Vapour | The gaseous state of a substance that is normally a liquid or solid at room temperature. |
| Saturated vapour | A vapour that is in dynamic equilibrium with its own liquid. It holds the maximum possible moisture at that temperature. |
| Unsaturated vapour | A vapour that is not in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid and can still accommodate more vapour molecules. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Do NOT apply standard gas laws to saturated vapours.** Saturated vapours do not obey Boyle's or Charles's laws. A very common JAMB trap is asking what happens to the pressure of a saturated vapour if its volume is halved at a constant temperature. The pressure remains **constant**, it does not double!
- **Volume does not affect S.V.P.** The saturated vapour pressure of a liquid relies solely on temperature. It is entirely independent of the volume of the container. 
- **Gas over water trap:** If a question says a gas is collected "over water", you are dealing with a wet mixture. Always subtract the S.V.P of water from the total pressure to find the correct dry gas pressure ($P_1$) before jumping into your $P_1V_1/T_1$ calculations. 
  - *Wrong working:* $V_2 = \frac{800 \times 200 \times 273}{760 \times 300}$ 
  - *Correct working:* $V_2 = \frac{(800 - 26.7) \times 200 \times 273}{760 \times 300}$
- **Kelvin conversion:** Always convert Celsius to Kelvin when using the gas laws ($T_K = T_C + 273$).
- **R.H. formula rule:** For Relative Humidity calculations, ensure you place the S.V.P at the **dew point** in the numerator (top) and the S.V.P at the **room temperature** in the denominator (bottom). Mixing them up will give an impossible percentage greater than $100\%$.

## Quick summary

1. A saturated vapour is in dynamic equilibrium with its unvaporized liquid and exerts a maximum pressure (S.V.P). It does not obey the standard gas laws.
2. An unsaturated vapour can still hold more vapour, is not in equilibrium, and approximately obeys the gas laws.
3. S.V.P increases continuously as temperature rises, but it is totally unaffected by changes in volume.
4. A liquid boils only when its S.V.P successfully equals the external atmospheric pressure. Lower atmospheric pressure (such as at high altitudes) causes the boiling point to drop.
5. In the barometer tube experiment, the S.V.P is calculated as the initial atmospheric pressure minus the final mercury height when liquid rests on the mercury ($\text{S.V.P} = H - h$).
6. Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated. Further cooling below this point causes condensation phenomena like dew, mist, fog, or clouds.
7. Relative humidity is defined as the ratio of the S.V.P at the dew point to the S.V.P at room temperature, expressed as a percentage.
8. The wet bulb in a hygrometer is cooled by the evaporation of water. The drier the surrounding air, the faster the evaporation, resulting in a larger temperature difference between the wet and dry bulbs.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following statements perfectly distinguishes a saturated vapour from an unsaturated vapour?**
- A. A saturated vapour obeys Boyle's law perfectly at all temperatures.
- B. A saturated vapour is in dynamic equilibrium with its own unvaporized liquid.
- C. A saturated vapour always has a lower pressure than an unsaturated vapour.
- D. A saturated vapour contains fewer molecules than an unsaturated vapour.

**Answer: B.** A saturated vapour is one that is in dynamic equilibrium with its own liquid, where the rate of evaporation exactly equals the rate of condensation. Furthermore, saturated vapours do not obey the gas laws.

**2. A standard mercury barometer reads $760\ \text{mmHg}$. When a few drops of a volatile liquid are introduced into the Torricellian vacuum, the liquid vaporizes completely, and the mercury level drops to $700\ \text{mmHg}$. More liquid is added until a thin layer of liquid remains on the mercury, and the level drops to $650\ \text{mmHg}$. What is the saturated vapour pressure of the liquid at that temperature?**
- A. $110\ \text{mmHg}$
- B. $60\ \text{mmHg}$
- C. $650\ \text{mmHg}$
- D. $50\ \text{mmHg}$

**Answer: A.** 
1. The vapour is saturated only when unvaporized liquid remains visible. The final mercury height at this point is $h = 650\ \text{mmHg}$. 
2. The S.V.P is the pressure pushing down on the mercury column, calculated as $H - h = 760\ \text{mmHg} - 650\ \text{mmHg} = 110\ \text{mmHg}$.
*(The mistake behind option B is using the intermediate height of $700\ \text{mmHg}$, which occurred when the vapour was still unsaturated).*

**3. A liquid begins to boil when its**
- A. internal temperature becomes equal to the room temperature
- B. saturated vapour pressure becomes exactly equal to the external atmospheric pressure
- C. saturated vapour pressure drops to zero
- D. volume expands rapidly to fill the container

**Answer: B.** Boiling occurs precisely at the temperature where the liquid's saturated vapour pressure manages to equal the external atmospheric pressure, allowing vapour bubbles to form within the liquid.

**4. Which of the following is formed when water vapour in a large mass of air condenses on dust particles suspended close to the ground, severely reducing visibility?**
- A. Rain
- B. Dew
- C. Cloud
- D. Fog

**Answer: D.** Fog (and mist) are formed by condensation on dust particles close to the ground. Dew forms on solid surfaces, and clouds form high up in the atmosphere.

**5. The saturated vapour pressure of water is $12.0\ \text{mmHg}$ at $14^\circ\text{C}$ and $24.0\ \text{mmHg}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$. If the dew point of a room at $25^\circ\text{C}$ is $14^\circ\text{C}$, calculate the relative humidity of the room.**
- A. $25\%$
- B. $50\%$
- C. $75\%$
- D. $100\%$

**Answer: B.** 
1. $\text{R.H.} = \frac{\text{S.V.P at dew point}}{\text{S.V.P at room temperature}} \times 100\%$
2. $\text{R.H.} = \frac{12.0\ \text{mmHg}}{24.0\ \text{mmHg}} \times 100\% = 0.5 \times 100\% = 50\%$.

**6. In a wet and dry bulb hygrometer, the wet bulb thermometer generally records a lower temperature than the dry bulb because**
- A. the wet bulb thermometer is slightly faulty by design
- B. evaporating water absorbs latent heat from the bulb, causing it to cool
- C. the water in the reservoir is originally colder than the air
- D. the dry bulb absorbs more heat from surrounding light sources

**Answer: B.** Evaporation requires energy and causes cooling. The evaporating water draws the latent heat of vaporization from the wet bulb itself, lowering its temperature compared to the dry bulb.

**7. A given mass of gas is collected over water at $30^\circ\text{C}$ and a total pressure of $750\ \text{mmHg}$. If the saturated vapour pressure of water at $30^\circ\text{C}$ is $31.8\ \text{mmHg}$, what is the actual pressure of the dry gas?**
- A. $781.8\ \text{mmHg}$
- B. $750.0\ \text{mmHg}$
- C. $718.2\ \text{mmHg}$
- D. $31.8\ \text{mmHg}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. The total pressure is a mixture of the dry gas and water vapour.
2. $P_{\text{dry}} = P_{\text{total}} - \text{S.V.P}$
3. $P_{\text{dry}} = 750\ \text{mmHg} - 31.8\ \text{mmHg} = 718.2\ \text{mmHg}$.
*(The mistake behind option A is blindly adding the S.V.P to the total pressure instead of subtracting it).*

**8. What effect does pushing a piston down to compress a saturated vapour in a closed container (kept at a strictly constant temperature) have on its pressure?**
- A. The pressure increases in accordance with Boyle's law.
- B. The pressure drops as the molecules are squeezed together.
- C. The pressure remains constant because excess vapour condenses into liquid.
- D. The pressure initially increases and then quickly drops to zero.

**Answer: C.** Saturated vapour pressure is entirely independent of volume. Compressing the space simply causes the excess vapour molecules to condense back into the liquid phase, thereby maintaining a constant pressure.

**9. At high altitudes like the Mambilla Plateau, water boils at a lower temperature because**
- A. the surrounding air is colder
- B. the atmospheric pressure is considerably lower
- C. the atmospheric pressure is considerably higher
- D. the density of the water is naturally lower

**Answer: B.** Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude. Since a liquid only boils when its S.V.P equals the atmospheric pressure, it reaches this lower pressure target at a lower temperature.

**10. The temperature to which air must be cooled for the water vapour currently present to just saturate it is known as the**
- A. boiling point
- B. freezing point
- C. dew point
- D. triple point

**Answer: C.** The dew point is the temperature at which air becomes $100\%$ saturated with water vapour. If the air is cooled any further below this point, condensation begins to occur.
