## The laws of reflection

**Objective i: interpret the laws of reflection; Content i: laws of reflection**

When light energy strikes a surface, it can be absorbed, transmitted, or reflected (bounced back). Reflection is the process that allows us to see objects that do not produce their own light. 

When light hits a smooth, highly polished surface (like a glass mirror), it undergoes **regular reflection**, where parallel rays of light bounce off in a single, parallel direction. When light hits a rough surface (like paper or a wall), it undergoes **diffuse reflection**, scattering the light rays in many different directions. 

To analyse how light reflects, physicists draw an imaginary straight line perpendicular (at an angle of $90^\circ$) to the mirror surface at the exact point where the light ray hits it. This line is called the **Normal**.

The **Laws of Reflection** state that:
1. **First Law:** The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
2. **Second Law:** The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.

**Formula relationships:**
$i = r$

Where:
- $i$ is the angle of incidence, measured in degrees ($^\circ$). It is the angle between the incident ray and the normal.
- $r$ is the angle of reflection, measured in degrees ($^\circ$). It is the angle between the reflected ray and the normal.

**Glancing angle ($g$):**
The angle between the incident ray and the surface of the mirror itself is called the glancing angle. Since the normal is at $90^\circ$ to the surface, the relationship is:
$g = 90^\circ - i$

Where:
- $g$ is the glancing angle, measured in degrees ($^\circ$).

### Deviation of light by a plane mirror
When a light ray strikes a plane mirror, it is forced to change its direction. The angle by which the ray is turned away from its original straight-line path is called the **angle of deviation**.

$d = 180^\circ - (i + r)$

Because $i = r$ (from the second law of reflection), this simplifies to:
$d = 180^\circ - 2i$

Alternatively, in terms of the glancing angle:
$d = 2g$

Where:
- $d$ is the angle of deviation, measured in degrees ($^\circ$).

### Rotation of a plane mirror
A very important principle frequently tested in JAMB UTME exams is what happens when a mirror is rotated while the incoming light ray is kept perfectly still. 
- If a plane mirror is rotated through an angle $\theta$, the reflected ray is rotated through an angle of **$2\theta$**.

**Common mistakes:**
- *Mistake:* Measuring the angle of incidence from the mirror surface instead of the normal. If a question says "a ray strikes a mirror at $30^\circ$ to the surface", a student might mistakenly use $i = 30^\circ$.
- *Correct working:* The angle given is the glancing angle ($g = 30^\circ$). The angle of incidence is $i = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$.

## Formation of images by plane, concave and convex mirrors

**Objective ii: illustrate the formation of images by plane, concave and convex mirrors; Content iii: formation of images by plane, concave and convex mirrors and ray diagrams**

### Images formed by a plane mirror
When you look at yourself in a standard flat looking-glass, you are seeing an image formed by a plane mirror. This image always has five strict characteristics:
1. It is **virtual**. (It cannot be caught on a screen because light rays do not actually pass through it; they only appear to diverge from it).
2. It is **erect** (upright).
3. It is the **same size** as the object.
4. It is **laterally inverted**. (Your right hand appears as the left hand in the mirror).
5. It is the **same distance behind the mirror** as the object is in front of the mirror.

### Curved mirrors: types and terminology
Curved mirrors are usually spherical, meaning they are cut from a hollow glass sphere. There are two main types:
- **Concave (converging) mirror:** The reflecting surface curves inwards (like the inside of a spoon). It brings parallel light rays together to a real focal point.
- **Convex (diverging) mirror:** The reflecting surface bulges outwards (like the back of a spoon). It spreads parallel light rays apart, making them appear to come from a virtual focal point behind the mirror.

| Feature | Concave Mirror | Convex Mirror |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Surface** | Curves inwards | Bulges outwards |
| **Effect on parallel rays** | Converges them to a real point | Diverges them from a virtual point |
| **Principal Focus** | Real (in front of the mirror) | Virtual (behind the mirror) |
| **Focal Length Sign** | Positive ($+$) | Negative ($-$) |
| **Types of images formed** | Can be real or virtual, magnified or diminished | Always virtual, erect, and diminished |

**Important geometric terms for curved mirrors:**
- **Pole ($P$):** The geometric centre of the mirror's reflecting surface.
- **Centre of Curvature ($C$):** The centre of the original sphere from which the mirror was cut.
- **Radius of Curvature ($r$):** The distance from the pole to the centre of curvature ($r = PC$).
- **Principal Axis:** The straight line passing through the Pole and the Centre of Curvature.
- **Principal Focus ($F$):** The point on the principal axis where rays parallel and close to the principal axis converge (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex) after reflection.
- **Focal Length ($f$):** The distance from the pole to the principal focus ($f = PF$).

**Key formula relationship:** 
For both types of spherical mirrors, the radius of curvature is exactly twice the focal length: 
$r = 2f$

Where:
- $r$ is the radius of curvature, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.
- $f$ is the focal length, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.

### Ray diagrams for curved mirrors
To locate where an image will form, physicists draw at least two standard "rays" from the top of the object. The point where these rays intersect (or appear to intersect) after reflection is where the image is formed.
**Step-by-step rules for drawing rays:**
1. A ray drawn parallel to the principal axis reflects through the principal focus ($F$).
2. A ray drawn passing through the centre of curvature ($C$) reflects back along its own exact path.
3. A ray drawn passing through the principal focus ($F$) reflects parallel to the principal axis.
4. A ray striking the pole ($P$) reflects symmetrically, obeying $i = r$.

#### Image formation by a Concave mirror
The nature of the image formed by a concave mirror completely changes depending on how close the object is to the mirror.

| Position of Object | Position of Image | Nature and Size of Image |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| At infinity | At principal focus ($F$) | Real, inverted, diminished to a point |
| Beyond $C$ | Between $C$ and $F$ | Real, inverted, diminished |
| At $C$ | At $C$ | Real, inverted, same size as object |
| Between $C$ and $F$ | Beyond $C$ | Real, inverted, magnified |
| At $F$ | At infinity | No clear image forms (reflected rays are parallel) |
| Between $F$ and Pole ($P$) | Behind the mirror | **Virtual**, erect, magnified |

Panel 2: Object beyond C, image between C and F (real, inverted, diminished). Panel 3: Object at C, image at C (real, inverted, same size). #### Image formation by a Convex mirror
A convex mirror is much simpler because it produces the exact same type of image no matter where the object is placed in front of it:
- The image is always **behind the mirror** (between $P$ and $F$).
- It is always **virtual, erect, and diminished** (smaller than the object).

[IMAGE: Ray diagram for a convex mirror. An upright arrow (object) is placed in front of a convex mirror. A ray parallel to the principal axis reflects outwards, diverging as if originating from the virtual focus (F) behind the mirror. Another ray directed towards the centre of curvature (C) reflects back on itself. The virtual dashed extensions of these rays intersect behind the mirror between the pole and F, forming a small, erect, virtual image arrow. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

**Common mistakes:**
- *Mistake:* Believing that a convex mirror can sometimes form a real image if the object is far away.
- *Correction:* A convex mirror *always* forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image for a real object. If you need a real image, you must use a concave mirror.

## The mirror formula

**Objective iii: apply the mirror formula to solve optical problems; Content iv: use of the mirror formula**

The mathematical relationship between the object distance, image distance, and the focal length is given by the **mirror formula**:

$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$

Where:
- $f$ is the focal length, measured in metres ($\text{m}$) or centimetres ($\text{cm}$).
- $u$ is the object distance from the pole, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.
- $v$ is the image distance from the pole, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.

### The Real-is-Positive Sign Convention
To use the mirror formula correctly without making severe errors, you must assign mathematical signs ($+$ or $-$) to the distances.
*Textbook variation:* Most Nigerian secondary school textbooks and JAMB use the **"Real-is-Positive"** sign convention. (Some foreign textbooks use the Cartesian convention where $u$ is always negative, but do not use that for JAMB). 

**Rules for Real-is-Positive:**
- Distances of **real** objects and **real** images are **positive** ($+$).
- Distances of **virtual** images are **negative** ($-$).
- The focal length ($f$) and radius of curvature ($r$) of a **concave mirror** are **positive** ($+$) because its focus is real.
- The focal length ($f$) and radius of curvature ($r$) of a **convex mirror** are **negative** ($-$) because its focus is virtual (behind the mirror).

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: A student in a physics lab in Lagos places a candle $30\ \text{cm}$ in front of a concave mirror of radius of curvature $40\ \text{cm}$. Where will the image be formed?**

**Working:**
1. State the given values with signs: Radius $r = +40\ \text{cm}$ (concave). Therefore, focal length $f = \frac{40\ \text{cm}}{2} = +20\ \text{cm}$. Object distance $u = +30\ \text{cm}$.
2. Write the mirror formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
3. Substitute the values with units: $\frac{1}{20\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Rearrange to solve for $\frac{1}{v}$: $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{20\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}}$.
5. Find a common denominator ($60\ \text{cm}$): $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{3}{60\ \text{cm}} - \frac{2}{60\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{60\ \text{cm}}$.
6. Invert both sides and state the final answer: $v = +60\ \text{cm}$.
The image is formed $60\ \text{cm}$ in front of the mirror (the positive sign means it is a real image).

**Example 2: A convex security mirror at an estate gate in Abuja has a focal length of $10\ \text{cm}$. A person stands $15\ \text{cm}$ from it. Find the position of the image.**

**Working:**
1. State the given values with signs: Object distance $u = +15\ \text{cm}$. Focal length $f = -10\ \text{cm}$ (the negative sign is crucial because it is a convex mirror).
2. Write the formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
3. Substitute the values with units: $-\frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{15\ \text{cm}} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Rearrange to solve for $\frac{1}{v}$: $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{15\ \text{cm}}$.
5. Find a common denominator ($30\ \text{cm}$): $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{3}{30\ \text{cm}} - \frac{2}{30\ \text{cm}} = -\frac{5}{30\ \text{cm}} = -\frac{1}{6\ \text{cm}}$.
6. Invert both sides and state the final answer: $v = -6\ \text{cm}$.
The negative sign means the image is **virtual** and is formed $6\ \text{cm}$ behind the mirror.

**Example 3: A solar cooker uses a large concave mirror. If sunlight (parallel rays from infinity) focuses at a point $50\ \text{cm}$ from the mirror pole, and a cooking pot is later placed $75\ \text{cm}$ from the pole, where will the image of the pot be formed?**

**Working:**
1. Deduce the focal length: Parallel rays focus at the principal focus, so the focal length $f = +50\ \text{cm}$ (concave mirror).
2. State the object distance: $u = +75\ \text{cm}$.
3. Write the formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Substitute the values with units: $\frac{1}{50\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{75\ \text{cm}} + \frac{1}{v}$.
5. Rearrange: $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{50\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{75\ \text{cm}}$.
6. Find a common denominator ($150\ \text{cm}$): $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{3}{150\ \text{cm}} - \frac{2}{150\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{150\ \text{cm}}$.
7. Invert and state the final answer: $v = +150\ \text{cm}$.
The real image forms $150\ \text{cm}$ in front of the mirror.

**Common mistakes:**
- *Mistake:* Forgetting the negative sign convention for convex mirrors.
- *Wrong working (from Example 2):* $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{15\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}} \implies v = +30\ \text{cm}$.
- *Correct working:* Convex mirrors have a negative focal length. $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{15\ \text{cm}} = -\frac{1}{6\ \text{cm}} \implies v = -6\ \text{cm}$.

## Linear and angular magnification

**Objective iv: determine the linear magnification; Content v: linear and angular magnification**

### Linear magnification ($m$)
Linear magnification is defined as the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object. By geometry, it is also equal to the ratio of the image distance to the object distance.

$m = \frac{\text{Height of image}}{\text{Height of object}} = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{v}{u}$

Where:
- $m$ is the linear magnification (it is a ratio, so it has **no unit**).
- $h_i$ is the height of the image, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.
- $h_o$ is the height of the object, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.
- $v$ is the image distance, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.
- $u$ is the object distance, measured in $\text{m}$ or $\text{cm}$.

*(Note: In the Real-is-Positive convention, we usually just take the absolute positive magnitude for magnification calculations, so $m = \left| \frac{v}{u} \right|$. A magnification greater than 1 means the image is enlarged; a magnification less than 1 means it is diminished).*

**JAMB Shortcut for magnification and mirror formula:**
By combining the mirror formula and $m = \frac{v}{u}$, you can directly relate magnification, object distance, and focal length without finding $v$ first. For an object forming a real image:
$m = \frac{f}{u - f}$
*(Warning: For virtual images, the absolute formula becomes $m = \frac{f}{f - u}$. It is highly recommended to substitute $v = \pm mu$ directly into the standard mirror formula instead, to avoid memorisation errors).*

### Worked Examples for Magnification

**Example 4: A tailor in Aba uses a mirror. A button $2\ \text{cm}$ tall is placed $12\ \text{cm}$ from the mirror, and its real image is formed $24\ \text{cm}$ away. What is the linear magnification and image height?**

**Working:**
1. State the given values: Object height $h_o = 2\ \text{cm}$. Object distance $u = +12\ \text{cm}$. Image distance $v = +24\ \text{cm}$ (real image).
2. Write the magnification formula: $m = \frac{v}{u}$.
3. Substitute the values: $m = \frac{24\ \text{cm}}{12\ \text{cm}} = 2$. (The magnification is 2, no unit).
4. Write the height formula: $m = \frac{h_i}{h_o}$.
5. Substitute to find image height: $2 = \frac{h_i}{2\ \text{cm}}$.
6. State the final answer: $h_i = 4\ \text{cm}$.

**Example 5: A dentist in Kano uses a small concave mirror of focal length $3\ \text{cm}$ to view a tooth. If the mirror produces an upright image that is magnified two times, how far is the mirror from the tooth?**

**Working:**
1. Identify the nature of the image: An "upright" image in a single mirror is always virtual.
2. Apply the sign convention to $v$: Since $m = 2$ and the image is virtual, $v$ must be negative. So, $v = -2u$.
3. Write the formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$. Focal length $f = +3\ \text{cm}$ (concave).
4. Substitute the values with units: $\frac{1}{3\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{u} - \frac{1}{2u}$.
5. Simplify the right side: $\frac{1}{3\ \text{cm}} = \frac{2}{2u} - \frac{1}{2u} = \frac{1}{2u}$.
6. Solve for $u$: $2u = 3\ \text{cm} \implies u = 1.5\ \text{cm}$.
The mirror is held $1.5\ \text{cm}$ from the tooth.

**Example 6: An okada (motorcycle) rider in Lagos has a convex side mirror with a focal length of $2.0\ \text{m}$. A danfo (bus) that is $3.0\ \text{m}$ tall is $6.0\ \text{m}$ behind the mirror. Determine the linear magnification and the height of the image in the mirror.**

**Working:**
1. State the given values with signs: Object height $h_o = 3.0\ \text{m}$. Object distance $u = +6.0\ \text{m}$. Focal length $f = -2.0\ \text{m}$ (convex mirror).
2. Write the mirror formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
3. Substitute the values with units: $-\frac{1}{2.0\ \text{m}} = \frac{1}{6.0\ \text{m}} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Rearrange to solve for $\frac{1}{v}$: $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{2.0\ \text{m}} - \frac{1}{6.0\ \text{m}}$.
5. Find a common denominator: $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{3}{6.0\ \text{m}} - \frac{1}{6.0\ \text{m}} = -\frac{4}{6.0\ \text{m}} = -\frac{2}{3.0\ \text{m}}$.
6. Invert to find $v$: $v = -1.5\ \text{m}$. (The image is virtual and $1.5\ \text{m}$ behind the mirror).
7. Write the linear magnification formula: $m = \left| \frac{v}{u} \right|$.
8. Substitute values: $m = \frac{1.5\ \text{m}}{6.0\ \text{m}} = 0.25$. (Magnification has no unit).
9. Calculate image height using $m = \frac{h_i}{h_o}$: $0.25 = \frac{h_i}{3.0\ \text{m}}$.
10. State final image height: $h_i = 0.25 \times 3.0\ \text{m} = 0.75\ \text{m}$.

### Angular magnification ($M$)
While linear magnification compares physical heights, **angular magnification** (or magnifying power) compares the angles the image and object make at the eye. It is heavily used in optical instruments (like telescopes and microscopes, covered in later sections).

$M = \frac{\theta_i}{\theta_o}$

Where:
- $M$ is the angular magnification (no unit).
- $\theta_i$ is the angle subtended by the image at the eye, measured in degrees ($^\circ$) or radians ($\text{rad}$).
- $\theta_o$ is the angle subtended by the object at the unaided eye, measured in degrees ($^\circ$) or radians ($\text{rad}$).

For simple plane mirrors, if the observer's eye is at the pole of the mirror, the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, meaning the angle subtended by the image equals the angle subtended by the object ($\theta_i = \theta_o$). In this specific position, the angular magnification is exactly $1$.

**Common mistakes:**
- *Mistake:* Ignoring the sign of $v$ when the mirror forms an upright (virtual) image.
- *Wrong working:* A concave mirror forms an erect image magnified 2 times. A student writes $v = +2u$, leading to $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{2u}$.
- *Correct working:* An erect image in a single mirror is always virtual, so $v$ must be negative. $v = -2u$, leading to $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} - \frac{1}{2u}$.

## Applications of reflection of light

**Objective v: apply the laws of reflection of light to the working of periscope, kaleidoscope and the sextant; Content ii: application of reflection of light**

### Applications of plane mirrors
1. **The Periscope:** Used in submarines or to see over a crowd. A simple periscope consists of a long tube with two plane mirrors placed parallel to each other. 
   - *How it works:* The mirrors are inclined at an angle of $45^\circ$ to the vertical axis of the tube. Light from an object strikes the top mirror at a $45^\circ$ angle of incidence, turns exactly $90^\circ$ downwards, travels down the tube, strikes the bottom mirror at $45^\circ$, and turns $90^\circ$ horizontally into the viewer's eye.
2. **The Kaleidoscope:** A toy that produces beautiful, symmetrical patterns. 
   - *How it works:* It consists of a tube containing two or more plane mirrors inclined at an angle (often $60^\circ$) to each other, with loose bits of coloured glass placed between them. As the tube is rotated, multiple reflections create complex changing patterns. The number of images $n$ formed by two mirrors inclined at an angle $\theta$ follows the formula $n = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} - 1$.
3. **The Sextant:** An optical instrument used in navigation by sailors to measure the angular altitude of celestial bodies (stars or the sun) above the horizon. 
   - *How it works:* It relies directly on the principle of the rotation of a mirror. An observer looks at the horizon through a fixed half-silvered mirror. A movable index mirror is then rotated until the reflection of a star aligns with the horizon. Because rotating a mirror by an angle $\theta$ deflects the reflected ray by $2\theta$, the graduated scale on the sextant is marked in $2\theta$ increments to allow the navigator to read the star's altitude directly.

Two plane mirrors are placed at the internal corners, parallel to each other and inclined at 45 degrees to the vertical walls. A horizontal light ray from an object enters the top window, strikes the top mirror, reflects 90 degrees straight down, strikes the bottom mirror, and reflects 90 degrees horizontally out to a drawn eye. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### Applications of curved mirrors
- **Concave Mirrors:** Because they can converge light to a point and can form magnified, erect images (when the object is placed between the pole and the focus), they are heavily used as **shaving mirrors**, **dentist's mirrors**, and **make-up mirrors**. They are also used as parabolic reflectors behind the bulbs in **car headlamps** and **searchlights** (placing the light bulb exactly at the principal focus $F$ reflects the light out as a strong, parallel beam). Solar cookers use large concave mirrors to converge sunlight onto a central cooking pot.
- **Convex Mirrors:** Because they always produce a virtual, erect, and diminished image, they capture a very wide **field of view**. They are used extensively as **car driving mirrors (side and rear-view mirrors)** and as **security mirrors** in supermarkets and at sharp, blind bends on roads, allowing a person to see a large area at a single glance.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Normal** | An imaginary straight line drawn perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to a mirror surface at the point of incidence. |
| **Regular reflection** | Reflection from a smooth surface where parallel rays bounce off parallel to each other. |
| **Real image** | An image formed by the actual intersection of light rays; it can be caught on a screen (always inverted in a single mirror). |
| **Virtual image** | An image formed by rays that only appear to intersect behind the mirror; it cannot be caught on a screen (always erect). |
| **Pole ($P$)** | The geometric centre of a spherical mirror's reflecting surface. |
| **Principal Focus ($F$)** | The point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the axis converge (or appear to diverge from) after reflection. |
| **Focal length ($f$)** | The distance from the pole to the principal focus ($f = \frac{r}{2}$). |
| **Linear magnification ($m$)** | The ratio of the image height to the object height, or image distance to object distance ($m = \frac{v}{u}$). |
| **Angular magnification ($M$)** | The ratio of the angle subtended by the image to the angle subtended by the object at the eye ($\frac{\theta_i}{\theta_o}$). |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Sign convention errors are the #1 mistake in mirror calculations.** Using the Real-is-Positive convention, permanently remember that a **convex mirror** always has a **negative** focal length ($f$), and a **virtual image** always has a **negative** image distance ($v$).
- When a JAMB question says "an erect image magnified 2 times," it **must** be a concave mirror forming a virtual image. You must assign $v = -2u$ before plugging it into the formula.
- Do not confuse the formula for the **angle of deviation** ($d = 180^\circ - 2i$) with the **rotation of a mirror** (where the reflected ray rotates by $2\theta$).
- Remember that the angle of incidence $i$ is strictly measured from the **normal**, not the mirror surface. If a ray strikes a mirror at $30^\circ$ to the surface, $i = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$.
- **"Real images are always inverted, and virtual images are always erect."** This is a golden rule for single mirrors. 
- Convex mirrors are chosen for car side mirrors not because they magnify (they actually diminish objects), but strictly because they provide a much wider field of view.

## Quick summary

1. The laws of reflection state that the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane, and the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection ($i=r$).
2. A plane mirror forms an image that is virtual, erect, laterally inverted, the exact same size as the object, and at the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front.
3. Rotating a mirror by an angle $\theta$ causes the reflected ray to deviate by an angle of $2\theta$. This optical lever principle is applied in the sextant for marine navigation.
4. Concave mirrors converge light. They can form real, inverted images (when the object is far) or virtual, erect, magnified images (when the object is close, making them useful for dentists and for shaving).
5. Convex mirrors always form virtual, erect, and diminished images. This provides a wide field of view, making them ideal for security mirrors and car side mirrors.
6. The mirror formula is $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$. Under the Real-is-Positive sign convention, distances for real objects/images and the focal length of a concave mirror are positive, while virtual image distances and the focal length of a convex mirror are negative.
7. Linear magnification is the ratio of image distance to object distance ($m = \frac{v}{u}$), while angular magnification compares the visual angle subtended by the image to the visual angle subtended by the object.

## Practice questions

**1. A ray of light strikes a plane mirror at an angle of $20^\circ$ to the mirror surface. What is the angle of deviation of the ray?**
- A. $20^\circ$
- B. $40^\circ$
- C. $70^\circ$
- D. $140^\circ$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Identify the glancing angle: The angle to the mirror surface is $g = 20^\circ$.
2. Write the deviation formula using the glancing angle: $d = 2g$.
3. Calculate the deviation: $d = 2 \times 20^\circ = 40^\circ$. 
4. Alternatively, find the angle of incidence: $i = 90^\circ - 20^\circ = 70^\circ$.
5. Use the standard deviation formula: $d = 180^\circ - 2i$.
6. Calculate: $d = 180^\circ - 2(70^\circ) = 180^\circ - 140^\circ = 40^\circ$.
The mistake in option D is calculating $2i$ ($140^\circ$) instead of evaluating $180^\circ - 2i$.

**2. A plane mirror is rotated through an angle of $15^\circ$ while the incident ray is kept fixed. By what angle does the reflected ray rotate?**
- A. $15^\circ$
- B. $30^\circ$
- C. $45^\circ$
- D. $60^\circ$

**Answer: B.**
1. Recall the principle of mirror rotation: When a mirror is rotated by an angle $\theta$, the reflected ray rotates by $2\theta$.
2. Identify the given mirror rotation angle: $\theta = 15^\circ$.
3. Calculate the ray rotation: $2\theta = 2 \times 15^\circ = 30^\circ$.
The mistake in option A is thinking the ray rotates by the exact same angle as the mirror.

**3. Two plane mirrors are inclined at an angle of $60^\circ$ to each other. How many images of a small object placed between them will be formed?**
- A. $4$
- B. $5$
- C. $6$
- D. $7$

**Answer: B.**
1. Recall the formula for the number of images formed by inclined mirrors: $n = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} - 1$.
2. Identify the angle between the mirrors: $\theta = 60^\circ$.
3. Substitute the value into the formula: $n = \frac{360^\circ}{60^\circ} - 1$.
4. Simplify the division: $n = 6 - 1$.
5. Calculate the final number of images: $n = 5$.
The mistake in option C is forgetting to subtract 1 from the division result.

**4. A boy stands $4\ \text{m}$ in front of a plane mirror. If he moves $1\ \text{m}$ closer to the mirror, what is the new total distance between the boy and his image?**
- A. $3\ \text{m}$
- B. $4\ \text{m}$
- C. $6\ \text{m}$
- D. $8\ \text{m}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Calculate the boy's new object distance: $u = 4\ \text{m} - 1\ \text{m} = 3\ \text{m}$.
2. Recall the property of plane mirrors: The image is formed at the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front.
3. Determine the image distance: $v = 3\ \text{m}$.
4. Calculate the total distance from the boy to his image: $\text{Total} = u + v = 3\ \text{m} + 3\ \text{m} = 6\ \text{m}$.
The mistake in option A is giving only the distance from the boy to the mirror, not all the way to his image.

**5. A real image formed by a concave mirror is twice the size of the object. If the focal length of the mirror is $10\ \text{cm}$, find the object distance.**
- A. $5.0\ \text{cm}$
- B. $10\ \text{cm}$
- C. $15\ \text{cm}$
- D. $30\ \text{cm}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Relate magnification to object and image distances: $m = 2$. Since the image is real, $v$ is positive. Therefore, $v = +2u$.
2. Identify the focal length for a concave mirror: $f = +10\ \text{cm}$.
3. Write the mirror formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Substitute the knowns: $\frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{2u}$.
5. Find a common denominator for the right side: $\frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}} = \frac{2}{2u} + \frac{1}{2u} = \frac{3}{2u}$.
6. Cross-multiply to solve for $u$: $2u = 30\ \text{cm}$.
7. Divide by 2: $u = 15\ \text{cm}$.
The mistake in option A is treating the real image as virtual (using $v = -2u$), which leads to $u = 5.0\ \text{cm}$.

**6. An object is placed $30\ \text{cm}$ in front of a convex mirror. If the image formed is exactly one-third the size of the object, what is the focal length of the mirror?**
- A. $7.5\ \text{cm}$
- B. $15\ \text{cm}$
- C. $20\ \text{cm}$
- D. $30\ \text{cm}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Identify the properties of a convex mirror image: The image is always virtual, so $v$ must be negative.
2. Apply the magnification formula: $m = \frac{1}{3}$. Since $u = +30\ \text{cm}$, $v = -\frac{1}{3} \times 30\ \text{cm} = -10\ \text{cm}$.
3. Write the mirror formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Substitute the values: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}} + \left(-\frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}}\right)$.
5. Find a common denominator: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}} - \frac{3}{30\ \text{cm}} = -\frac{2}{30\ \text{cm}}$.
6. Simplify and invert: $\frac{1}{f} = -\frac{1}{15\ \text{cm}} \implies f = -15\ \text{cm}$.
7. State the magnitude of the focal length: $15\ \text{cm}$.
The mistake in option A is forgetting to make $v$ negative for a virtual image, giving $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}} + \frac{1}{10\ \text{cm}} = \frac{4}{30\ \text{cm}}$, which wrongly yields $f = 7.5\ \text{cm}$.

**7. Which of the following optical instruments relies primarily on the principle that rotating a mirror by an angle $\theta$ turns the reflected ray by $2\theta$?**
- A. Periscope
- B. Kaleidoscope
- C. Sextant
- D. Telescope

**Answer: C.**
1. Recall the working principle of a periscope: it uses two fixed plane mirrors angled at $45^\circ$ to turn light by $90^\circ$ twice.
2. Recall the kaleidoscope: it uses multiple fixed mirrors angled to each other (usually $60^\circ$) to create multiple images.
3. Recall the sextant: it features a movable index mirror. When this mirror is rotated by an angle $\theta$ to align celestial bodies, the reflected ray deviates by $2\theta$, allowing the scale to measure the altitude directly.
The mistake in option A is confusing an instrument that uses fixed parallel mirrors (periscope) with one that relies on continuous mirror rotation (sextant).

**8. A dentist uses a small mirror to examine a tooth located $1.5\ \text{cm}$ away. If the mirror produces an upright image that is magnified two times, what is the focal length of the mirror?**
- A. $1.0\ \text{cm}$
- B. $1.5\ \text{cm}$
- C. $3.0\ \text{cm}$
- D. $4.5\ \text{cm}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Identify the nature of the image: An upright, magnified image implies a concave mirror forming a virtual image.
2. Relate magnification to distance: $u = +1.5\ \text{cm}$ and $m = 2$. Since the image is virtual, $v = -2 \times 1.5\ \text{cm} = -3.0\ \text{cm}$.
3. Write the mirror formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Substitute the values: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{1.5\ \text{cm}} + \left(-\frac{1}{3.0\ \text{cm}}\right)$.
5. Find a common denominator: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{2}{3.0\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{3.0\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{3.0\ \text{cm}}$.
6. Invert to find $f$: $f = 3.0\ \text{cm}$.
The mistake in option A happens if you treat the virtual image distance $v$ as positive, giving $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{1.5\ \text{cm}} + \frac{1}{3.0\ \text{cm}} = \frac{3}{3.0\ \text{cm}}$, so $f = 1.0\ \text{cm}$.

**9. A convex mirror has a focal length of $30\ \text{cm}$. If an object is placed $60\ \text{cm}$ in front of it, what is the linear magnification of the image?**
- A. $0.33$
- B. $0.50$
- C. $2.0$
- D. $3.0$

**Answer: A.**
1. State the given values with the correct sign convention: $u = +60\ \text{cm}$, and for a convex mirror, $f = -30\ \text{cm}$.
2. Write the mirror formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
3. Substitute the values: $-\frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{60\ \text{cm}} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Rearrange to find $\frac{1}{v}$: $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{30\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{60\ \text{cm}}$.
5. Find a common denominator: $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{2}{60\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{60\ \text{cm}} = -\frac{3}{60\ \text{cm}}$.
6. Simplify and invert: $\frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{20\ \text{cm}} \implies v = -20\ \text{cm}$.
7. Calculate linear magnification $m = \left| \frac{v}{u} \right|$: $m = \frac{20\ \text{cm}}{60\ \text{cm}} = 0.33$. (Magnification has no unit).
The mistake in option D is calculating $\frac{u}{v} = \frac{60\ \text{cm}}{20\ \text{cm}} = 3.0$ instead of $\frac{v}{u}$.

**10. An object is placed $20\ \text{cm}$ from a concave mirror with a radius of curvature of $30\ \text{cm}$. Find the distance of the image from the mirror.**
- A. $8.6\ \text{cm}$
- B. $15\ \text{cm}$
- C. $30\ \text{cm}$
- D. $60\ \text{cm}$

**Answer: D.**
1. Calculate the focal length from the radius of curvature: $r = 30\ \text{cm}$, so $f = \frac{30\ \text{cm}}{2} = 15\ \text{cm}$ (positive for a concave mirror).
2. State the object distance: $u = +20\ \text{cm}$.
3. Write the mirror formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
4. Rearrange to solve for $\frac{1}{v}$: $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u}$.
5. Substitute the values: $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{15\ \text{cm}} - \frac{1}{20\ \text{cm}}$.
6. Find a common denominator ($60\ \text{cm}$): $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{4}{60\ \text{cm}} - \frac{3}{60\ \text{cm}} = \frac{1}{60\ \text{cm}}$.
7. Invert both sides: $v = 60\ \text{cm}$.
The mistake in option A is incorrectly adding the fractions ($\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{15\ \text{cm}} + \frac{1}{20\ \text{cm}} = \frac{7}{60\ \text{cm}} \implies v \approx 8.6\ \text{cm}$) instead of subtracting them.
