# JAMB Physics Notes: Optical Instruments

## What JAMB expects

This topic is part of Section A of the JAMB Physics syllabus. It covers the physics of how we use lenses to enhance our vision, capture images, and correct eye problems. The syllabus includes the following content items:

* (i) general principles of microscopes, telescopes, projectors, cameras and the human eye (physiological details of the eye are not required);
* (ii) power of a lens;
* (iii) angular magnification;
* (iv) near and far points;
* (v) sight defects and their corrections.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. apply the principles of operation of optical instruments to solve problems;
2. distinguish between the human eye and the cameras;
3. calculate the power of a lens;
4. evaluate the angular magnification of optical instruments;
5. determine the near and far points;
6. detect sight defects and their corrections.

***

**A Note on Sign Conventions:** In these notes, we use the standard **"Real-is-Positive"** sign convention standard in Nigerian secondary schools. This means:
- Real objects and real images have positive distances ($u$ and $v$ are positive).
- Virtual images have negative distances ($v$ is negative).
- Converging (convex) lenses have a positive focal length ($f$ is positive).
- Diverging (concave) lenses have a negative focal length ($f$ is negative).
Some textbooks use the Cartesian sign convention. Do not worry; both conventions give the exact same answers for focal length and lens power.

## General Principles of Optical Instruments

Optical instruments use mirrors, prisms, and most commonly **lenses** to manipulate light. They help us see things that are too small (microscopes), too far away (telescopes), or they project and record images (projectors and cameras). 

### 1. The Simple Microscope (Magnifying Glass)
A simple microscope is just a single **convex lens**. When an object (like a tiny insect or a small word in a textbook) is placed between the optical centre and the principal focus ($F$) of the lens, the lens produces an image that is **virtual, erect, and magnified**. 

[IMAGE: A convex lens with a small upright object arrow placed between the focal point F and the lens. Rays from the top of the object diverge after passing through the lens. Dashed lines trace these rays backwards to meet and form a much larger, upright virtual image arrow behind the object. Labels: "Object", "Virtual, erect, magnified image", "Focal point F". Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### 2. The Compound Microscope
A compound microscope uses two convex lenses to achieve much higher magnification than a simple magnifying glass. 
- The **objective lens** (closest to the object) has a very short focal length. It forms a real, inverted, and magnified image of the object.
- The **eyepiece lens** (closest to the eye) also has a short focal length, but slightly longer than the objective. It acts as a simple microscope to magnify the first image even further, producing a final **virtual, inverted, and highly magnified image**.

### 3. The Astronomical Telescope
An astronomical telescope is used to view distant objects like stars and the moon. It also uses two convex lenses.
- The **objective lens** has a **long focal length** and a large aperture (to gather as much light as possible from faint stars). It forms a real, inverted, and diminished image of the distant object at its focal point.
- The **eyepiece lens** has a **short focal length** and magnifies this real image, producing a final virtual image. 

### 4. The Projector
A slide or film projector forms a real, inverted, and magnified image of a brightly lit slide onto a distant screen. It uses a strong light source, a **condenser** (two plano-convex lenses that concentrate the light evenly onto the slide), and a convex **projection lens**. Because the lens naturally inverts the image, the slide must be put into the projector **upside down** so that the final picture on the screen appears upright!

### 5. The Camera
A camera is a light-proof box that uses a convex lens system to focus a **real, inverted, and diminished** image onto a light-sensitive surface (photographic film or a digital image sensor). It has a diaphragm (aperture) to control the amount of light entering and a shutter to control how long the light falls on the sensor.

### 6. The Human Eye
The human eye works very much like a camera. Light enters through the transparent cornea and pupil, and is focused by the crystalline **convex lens** to form a **real, inverted, and diminished** image on the **retina** (the light-sensitive screen at the back of the eye). The brain then interprets this inverted image so we see the world upright.

### How Optical Instruments Work (Principles and Problem Solving)

JAMB often asks you to solve problems involving the arrangement of lenses in these instruments.

**Telescopes in Normal Adjustment**
When an astronomical telescope is in "normal adjustment", it means the final image is formed at **infinity**. This is the most relaxed viewing state for the human eye.
For this to happen, the focal point of the objective lens ($F_o$) must perfectly coincide with the focal point of the eyepiece lens ($F_e$). 
Therefore, the distance between the two lenses (the length of the telescope tube, $L$) is the sum of their focal lengths:
$$L = f_o + f_e$$

**Example 1: Telescope Tube Length**
An astronomical telescope has an objective lens of focal length $100\ \text{cm}$ and an eyepiece of focal length $5\ \text{cm}$. If the telescope is in normal adjustment, what is the distance between the lenses?

1. Identify the formula for normal adjustment: $L = f_o + f_e$.
2. Substitute the given values: $L = 100\ \text{cm} + 5\ \text{cm}$.
3. Calculate the answer: $L = 105\ \text{cm}$.
4. **Answer:** The distance between the lenses is $105\ \text{cm}$.

### Distinguishing Between the Human Eye and the Camera

Although the eye and the camera operate on the same basic optical principles, their biological and mechanical structures differ. JAMB expects you to know which part of the camera corresponds to which part of the eye, and how they achieve focus.

| Feature | The Human Eye | The Camera |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Light-sensitive screen** (Where the image forms) | **Retina** | **Photographic film** or **Digital sensor** |
| **Light control** (Adjusts amount of light) | **Iris** (changes the size of the pupil) | **Diaphragm** (changes the size of the aperture) |
| **Focusing lens** | Crystalline lens (flexible convex lens) | Glass lens system (rigid convex lens) |
| **Exposure time** | Eyelids (continuous exposure while open) | **Shutter** (opens for a split second) |
| **Focusing method** | **Accommodation:** Ciliary muscles change the shape (focal length) of the lens while the distance to the retina stays fixed. | **Mechanical movement:** The lens moves back and forth to change the distance to the film, while the focal length stays fixed. |
| **Nature of image** | Real, inverted, diminished | Real, inverted, diminished |

## Power of a Lens

The power of a lens is a measure of how strongly it converges or diverges light. A lens with a short focal length bends light very sharply, so it has a **high power**. A lens with a long focal length bends light gently, so it has a **low power**.

The power $P$ of a lens is defined as the reciprocal of its focal length $f$, **measured in metres**.
$$P = \frac{1}{f\ (\text{in metres})}$$

- **Unit:** The SI unit of power of a lens is the **Dioptre (D)**.
- **Sign:** By our sign convention, the focal length of a converging (convex) lens is positive, so its power is **positive** (e.g., $+2.0\ \text{D}$). The focal length of a diverging (concave) lens is negative, so its power is **negative** (e.g., $-5.0\ \text{D}$).

**Lenses in contact:** If two thin lenses of powers $P_1$ and $P_2$ are placed in contact, the total power $P$ of the combination is simply their algebraic sum:
$$P = P_1 + P_2$$

---

### Calculating the Power of a Lens

**Example 2: Finding Power from Focal Length**
Calculate the power of a convex lens of focal length $5\ \text{cm}$.

1. Write the formula: $P = \frac{1}{f}$.
2. Convert the focal length to metres (this is a common JAMB trap!): $f = 5\ \text{cm} = 0.05\ \text{m}$.
3. Substitute the value: $P = \frac{1}{0.05}$.
4. Calculate the result: $P = +20$.
5. **Answer:** The power of the lens is $+20.0\ \text{D}$ (positive because it is a convex lens).

**Example 3: Finding Focal Length from Power**
An optician prescribes a correcting lens of power $-4.0\ \text{D}$ for a student. What is the focal length and type of the lens?

1. Write the formula: $f = \frac{1}{P}$.
2. Substitute the power: $f = \frac{1}{-4.0}$.
3. Calculate the focal length in metres: $f = -0.25\ \text{m}$.
4. Convert to centimetres for convenience: $f = -25\ \text{cm}$.
5. **Answer:** The focal length is $-25\ \text{cm}$. The negative sign means it is a **concave (diverging)** lens.

**Example 4: Lenses in Combination**
Two thin lenses of focal lengths $20\ \text{cm}$ and $-10\ \text{cm}$ are placed in contact. What is the power of the combination?

1. Find the power of the first lens: $f_1 = 20\ \text{cm} = 0.2\ \text{m} \implies P_1 = \frac{1}{0.2} = +5.0\ \text{D}$.
2. Find the power of the second lens: $f_2 = -10\ \text{cm} = -0.1\ \text{m} \implies P_2 = \frac{1}{-0.1} = -10.0\ \text{D}$.
3. Add the powers: $P_{\text{total}} = P_1 + P_2 = (+5.0) + (-10.0) = -5.0\ \text{D}$.
4. **Answer:** The power of the combination is $-5.0\ \text{D}$.

## Angular Magnification

For optical instruments that we look directly through (like microscopes and telescopes), we measure their effectiveness using **angular magnification** (also called magnifying power, $M$). 

Linear magnification is the ratio of image height to object height ($v/u$). But when viewing a distant star, its height is massive, and its image in a telescope is actually much smaller than the star itself! What the telescope does is make the angle the image forms at your eye *larger* than the angle the star forms at your naked eye.

Angular magnification is defined as:
$$M = \frac{\text{Angle subtended by the image at the eye (\(\beta\))}}{\text{Angle subtended by the object at the unaided eye (\(\alpha\))}}$$
$$M = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$$

Because it is a ratio of two angles, angular magnification **has no unit**.

---

### Evaluating the Angular Magnification of Instruments

**1. Astronomical Telescope**
For an astronomical telescope in normal adjustment (viewing at infinity), the angular magnification depends only on the focal lengths of the two lenses:
$$M = \frac{f_o}{f_e}$$
where $f_o$ is the focal length of the objective lens, and $f_e$ is the focal length of the eyepiece. To get a high magnification, a telescope needs an objective lens with a very long focal length and an eyepiece with a very short focal length.

**Example 5:** An astronomical telescope has an objective lens of focal length $120\ \text{cm}$ and an eyepiece of focal length $4\ \text{cm}$. Evaluate its angular magnification in normal adjustment.
1. Write the formula: $M = \frac{f_o}{f_e}$.
2. Substitute the values: $M = \frac{120}{4}$.
3. Calculate the result: $M = 30$.
4. **Answer:** The angular magnification is $30$.

**2. Simple Microscope (Magnifying Glass)**
When using a magnifying glass, the highest angular magnification is achieved when the virtual image is formed at the eye's near point (the least distance of distinct vision, usually denoted as $D = 25\ \text{cm}$). The formula is:
$$M = 1 + \frac{D}{f}$$
*(Note: If the image is formed at infinity instead, the formula is simply $M = \frac{D}{f}$. JAMB questions will usually specify if the image is at the near point).*

## Near and Far Points

To understand human vision and its defects, we must define the limits of where the eye can see objects clearly.

- **Near Point:** The closest point to the eye at which an object can be seen clearly without straining. The distance to the near point is called the **least distance of distinct vision** ($D$). For a normal healthy eye, this distance is **$25\ \text{cm}$**.
- **Far Point:** The furthest point from the eye at which an object can be seen clearly. For a normal healthy eye, the far point is at **infinity ($\infty$)**. 

### Determining the Near and Far Points

The ability of the human eye to adjust its focal length to see objects anywhere between the near point ($25\ \text{cm}$) and the far point (infinity) is called **accommodation**. The ciliary muscles change the thickness of the eye lens to achieve this.

If a person's near point or far point shifts away from these normal values, we can determine that they have a sight defect.
- If a student cannot read a textbook placed $25\ \text{cm}$ from their face, but has to push it back to $60\ \text{cm}$ to see clearly, their near point has moved to $60\ \text{cm}$. They are long-sighted.
- If an Okada rider in Lagos cannot see a traffic light $20\ \text{m}$ away clearly, but can see his dashboard perfectly, his far point is no longer at infinity. He is short-sighted.

***

## Sight Defects and Their Corrections

When the eye loses its normal range of accommodation, or if the eyeball is the wrong shape, sight defects occur. JAMB tests your ability to identify these defects, know their causes, choose the correct lens to fix them, and calculate the exact power of that lens.

### 1. Myopia (Short-sightedness)
- **Symptom:** A person can see near objects clearly but cannot see distant objects. Their far point is less than infinity.
- **Cause:** The eyeball is too long, or the eye lens is too converging (too thick). As a result, parallel light rays from a distant object are focused **in front of the retina**.
- **Correction:** A **diverging (concave) lens**. The lens spreads out the incoming light rays just enough so that the eye lens focuses them exactly on the retina. 

[IMAGE: Two eyeball diagrams for Myopia. Top: Parallel rays from infinity enter the eye and cross (converge) before reaching the retina, leaving a blurred circle on the back of the eye. Bottom: A concave lens is placed in front of the eye. The parallel rays diverge slightly as they pass through it, so the eye lens now brings them to a sharp focus exactly on the retina. Clean flat textbook illustration, white background.]

### 2. Hypermetropia (Long-sightedness)
- **Symptom:** A person can see distant objects clearly but cannot focus on near objects (like reading a book). Their near point is further away than the normal $25\ \text{cm}$.
- **Cause:** The eyeball is too short, or the eye lens is too weak (too flat). Light rays from a near object are focused **behind the retina**.
- **Correction:** A **converging (convex) lens**. The lens bends the incoming light rays inward before they enter the eye, allowing the eye lens to focus them on the retina.

### 3. Presbyopia
- **Symptom:** Difficulty focusing on near objects, common in older people.
- **Cause:** As a person ages, the eye lens loses its elasticity (it becomes stiff), and the ciliary muscles weaken, so the eye can no longer accommodate fully.
- **Correction:** Often corrected with converging lenses for reading, or **bifocals** if the person also has myopia.

### 4. Astigmatism
- **Symptom:** Blurry vision where vertical lines might appear clear but horizontal lines appear blurred, or vice-versa.
- **Cause:** Uneven curvature of the cornea or the eye lens (it is shaped slightly like a rugby ball instead of a perfect football).
- **Correction:** Corrected using **cylindrical lenses** that provide different powers in different planes.

---

### Detecting Sight Defects and Their Corrections (Calculations)

To calculate the corrective lens, we use the standard lens formula:
$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$$

**The golden rule for correcting vision:** The purpose of the artificial lens is to take an object at the distance the person *wants* to see ($u$) and form a **virtual image** at the distance the person *can actually* see (their defective near or far point). Because the image is virtual, **$v$ must always be negative**.

**Example 6: Correcting Myopia**
A boy has a far point of $50\ \text{cm}$. He wishes to see distant stars clearly. What is the focal length and power of the corrective lens required?

1. Identify the defect and setup: He is short-sighted. He wants to see at infinity, so $u = \infty$. His actual far point is $50\ \text{cm}$, so the image must form there. Since it is a virtual image, $v = -50\ \text{cm} = -0.5\ \text{m}$.
2. Write the lens formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
3. Substitute the values: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{\infty} + \frac{1}{-0.5}$.
4. Note that $\frac{1}{\infty} = 0$. Therefore, $\frac{1}{f} = 0 - 2 = -2.0\ \text{m}^{-1}$.
5. Power $P = \frac{1}{f} = -2.0\ \text{D}$.
6. Focal length $f = \frac{1}{-2.0} = -0.5\ \text{m} = -50\ \text{cm}$.
7. **Answer:** The focal length is $-50\ \text{cm}$ and the power is $-2.0\ \text{D}$. (A concave lens).

**Example 7: Correcting Hypermetropia**
A woman has a near point of $100\ \text{cm}$. Calculate the focal length of the lens she needs to read a newspaper placed at the normal near point of $25\ \text{cm}$.

1. Identify the defect and setup: She is long-sighted. She wants to read at $25\ \text{cm}$, so $u = 25\ \text{cm}$. Her defective near point is $100\ \text{cm}$, so the virtual image must form there. Thus, $v = -100\ \text{cm}$.
2. Write the lens formula: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$.
3. Substitute the values: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{25} + \frac{1}{-100}$.
4. Find a common denominator: $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{4}{100} - \frac{1}{100} = \frac{3}{100}$.
5. Calculate the focal length: $f = \frac{100}{3} \approx +33.3\ \text{cm}$.
6. **Answer:** The focal length of the required convex lens is $+33.3\ \text{cm}$.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Optical centre** | The central point of a lens through which light rays pass without bending. |
| **Accommodation** | The ability of the eye lens to change its focal length to focus on objects at various distances. |
| **Near point** | The closest distance a normal eye can see clearly without strain (usually $25\ \text{cm}$). |
| **Far point** | The furthest distance a normal eye can see clearly (infinity for a normal eye). |
| **Dioptre (D)** | The SI unit of the power of a lens, equal to the reciprocal of the focal length in metres. |
| **Myopia** | Short-sightedness; the inability to see distant objects clearly. |
| **Hypermetropia** | Long-sightedness; the inability to see near objects clearly. |
| **Presbyopia** | Loss of accommodation due to ageing, making it hard to focus on near objects. |
| **Angular magnification** | The ratio of the angle subtended by an image at the eye to the angle subtended by the object at the unaided eye. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Wrong unit for Power:** The most common mistake in JAMB is forgetting to convert the focal length to **metres** before calculating power. If $f = 10\ \text{cm}$, $P \neq \frac{1}{10}$. You must do $P = \frac{1}{0.1} = +10\ \text{D}$.
- **Wrong sign for image distance ($v$):** When calculating correcting lenses for sight defects, the image formed by the glasses is **always virtual**, so $v$ must be negative! If you forget the negative sign, your answer will be entirely wrong.
- **Myopia object distance:** For short-sightedness, always set $u = \infty$ (infinity) unless the question specifically states they want to see a specific distant object (like a board at $5\ \text{m}$). The term $\frac{1}{\infty}$ becomes $0$.
- **Normal adjustment distance:** The distance between the lenses in an astronomical telescope in normal adjustment is $f_o + f_e$. Do not multiply them.
- **Eye vs Camera:** Remember that the camera focuses by **moving the lens**, while the eye focuses by **changing the shape (focal length) of the lens**.

## Quick summary

1. A simple microscope uses a single convex lens to produce a virtual, erect, and magnified image. A compound microscope and an astronomical telescope use two convex lenses (objective and eyepiece).
2. The power of a lens is $P = \frac{1}{f}$, where $f$ is measured in metres. The unit is the Dioptre (D). Converging lenses are positive; diverging lenses are negative.
3. The human eye and the camera share similarities: the retina corresponds to the film/sensor, the iris to the diaphragm, and the pupil to the aperture.
4. Angular magnification is the ratio of angles subtended by the image and the object at the eye. For a telescope, $M = \frac{f_o}{f_e}$.
5. A normal eye has a near point of $25\ \text{cm}$ and a far point at infinity. The ability to shift focus between these points is called accommodation.
6. Myopia (short-sightedness) is caused by a long eyeball and is corrected with a diverging (concave) lens. 
7. Hypermetropia (long-sightedness) is caused by a short eyeball and is corrected with a converging (convex) lens.

## Practice questions

**1. Calculate the power of a convex lens of focal length $10\ \text{cm}$.**
- A. $+1.0\ \text{D}$
- B. $+10.0\ \text{D}$
- C. $-10.0\ \text{D}$
- D. $+0.1\ \text{D}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Formula: $P = \frac{1}{f\ \text{(in metres)}}$.
2. $f = 10\ \text{cm} = 0.1\ \text{m}$.
3. $P = \frac{1}{0.1} = +10.0\ \text{D}$.
(Option D ($+0.1\ \text{D}$) comes from failing to convert centimetres to metres: $\frac{1}{10} = 0.1$. Option A ($+1.0\ \text{D}$) comes from converting $10\ \text{cm}$ to $1.0\ \text{m}$ by mistake.)

**2. An astronomical telescope has an objective lens of focal length $100\ \text{cm}$ and an eyepiece of focal length $5\ \text{cm}$. In normal adjustment, what is its angular magnification?**
- A. $105$
- B. $95$
- C. $20$
- D. $500$

**Answer: C.**
1. $M = \frac{f_o}{f_e}$.
2. $M = \frac{100}{5} = 20$.
(Option A is a mistake of adding the focal lengths instead of dividing them).

**3. Which part of the camera corresponds in function to the retina of the human eye?**
- A. Film or image sensor
- B. Diaphragm
- C. Shutter
- D. Aperture

**Answer: A.** The retina is the light-sensitive surface at the back of the eye where the image is formed. In a camera, this function is performed by the photographic film or digital image sensor.

**4. A short-sighted man cannot see objects clearly if they are beyond $50\ \text{cm}$. The power of the lens needed to correct his vision is:**
- A. $+2.0\ \text{D}$
- B. $-2.0\ \text{D}$
- C. $+0.5\ \text{D}$
- D. $-0.5\ \text{D}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Myopia means he wants to see at infinity: $u = \infty$.
2. His far point is $50\ \text{cm}$: $v = -50\ \text{cm} = -0.5\ \text{m}$.
3. $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{\infty} + \frac{1}{-0.5} = 0 - 2 = -2.0\ \text{D}$.
(Option A is a common mistake for students who forget that short-sightedness requires a diverging lens, which has a negative power. Option D comes from simply making the value $0.5$ negative without finding its reciprocal).

**5. A long-sighted woman has a near point of $100\ \text{cm}$. What focal length of lens is required to enable her to read a book placed at $25\ \text{cm}$?**
- A. $25.0\ \text{cm}$
- B. $33.3\ \text{cm}$
- C. $75.0\ \text{cm}$
- D. $125.0\ \text{cm}$

**Answer: B.**
1. Object distance $u = 25\ \text{cm}$.
2. Image distance $v = -100\ \text{cm}$ (virtual image).
3. $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{25} - \frac{1}{100} = \frac{4 - 1}{100} = \frac{3}{100}$.
4. $f = \frac{100}{3} \approx 33.3\ \text{cm}$.
(Option C is the common mistake of simply subtracting the distances: $100 - 25 = 75$).

**6. For the astronomical telescope described in Question 2 ($f_o = 100\ \text{cm}$, $f_e = 5\ \text{cm}$) operating in normal adjustment, what is the distance between the two lenses?**
- A. $105\ \text{cm}$
- B. $95\ \text{cm}$
- C. $20\ \text{cm}$
- D. $500\ \text{cm}$

**Answer: A.** In normal adjustment, the length of the telescope is $L = f_o + f_e = 100 + 5 = 105\ \text{cm}$.

**7. For a normal, healthy human eye, the near point and far point are respectively:**
- A. $0\ \text{cm}$ and $25\ \text{cm}$
- B. $25\ \text{cm}$ and $100\ \text{m}$
- C. $25\ \text{cm}$ and infinity
- D. $10\ \text{cm}$ and infinity

**Answer: C.** The closest distance of distinct vision is $25\ \text{cm}$, and a normal eye can focus on parallel rays from stars that are essentially at an infinite distance away.

**8. A simple microscope (magnifying glass) produces an image that is:**
- A. Real, inverted and magnified
- B. Virtual, erect and magnified
- C. Virtual, inverted and diminished
- D. Real, erect and magnified

**Answer: B.** When an object is placed between the optical centre and the principal focus of a convex lens, it forms a virtual, erect (upright), and magnified image.

**9. Presbyopia is a sight defect common in old age. It is primarily caused by:**
- A. Elongation of the eyeball
- B. Loss of elasticity of the eye lens and weakening of the ciliary muscles
- C. Uneven curvature of the cornea
- D. Clouding of the eye lens

**Answer: B.** As people age, the lens hardens and the ciliary muscles weaken, making accommodation (focusing on near objects) very difficult. (Option A causes myopia, Option C causes astigmatism, and Option D describes cataracts).

**10. Two thin lenses of focal lengths $20\ \text{cm}$ and $-10\ \text{cm}$ are placed in contact. What is the power of the combination?**
- A. $-5.0\ \text{D}$
- B. $+5.0\ \text{D}$
- C. $-10.0\ \text{D}$
- D. $+15.0\ \text{D}$

**Answer: A.**
1. $P_1 = \frac{1}{0.2\ \text{m}} = +5.0\ \text{D}$.
2. $P_2 = \frac{1}{-0.1\ \text{m}} = -10.0\ \text{D}$.
3. Total Power $P = P_1 + P_2 = +5.0 + (-10.0) = -5.0\ \text{D}$.
(Option D ($+15.0\ \text{D}$) is the mistake of subtracting the negative power ($+5.0 - (-10.0)$) or dropping the negative sign of the second lens. Option B ($+5.0\ \text{D}$) is the mistake of dropping the negative sign from the final answer.)
