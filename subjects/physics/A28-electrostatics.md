## The existence of positive and negative charges in matter

All matter in the universe is made up of atoms. An atom consists of a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by orbiting electrons. 
- **Protons** carry a positive electric charge.
- **Electrons** carry a negative electric charge.
- **Neutrons** carry no charge (they are neutral).

Normally, matter is electrically neutral because the number of positive protons exactly equals the number of negative electrons. However, electrons are loosely bound to the outside of atoms and can be transferred from one object to another.
- If a neutral object **gains electrons**, it acquires an excess of negative charge and becomes **negatively charged**.
- If a neutral object **loses electrons**, it has a deficit of electrons (meaning more protons than electrons) and becomes **positively charged**.

The SI unit of electric charge is the **Coulomb** ($\text{C}$). The fundamental charge (the smallest independent charge found in nature) is the charge of a single electron, which has a magnitude of $1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$.

The most fundamental rule of electrostatics is the **First Law of Electrostatics**: 
*Like charges repel each other, while unlike charges attract each other.* 

### How to identify charges

To identify the sign of an unknown charge, we must rely on the principle of repulsion. 

If you bring a known positively charged rod near an unknown suspended object and they attract, you **cannot** immediately conclude that the unknown object is negatively charged. Why? Because a charged object can also attract a completely neutral object through a process called induction (explained below). 

However, repulsion only ever occurs between two objects that carry the same type of charge. Therefore, **repulsion is the only sure test** for identifying the presence of a like electric charge.

## Charging a body by friction, contact and induction

An object can be electrically charged using three main methods.

### Steps to charge a body

**1. Charging by friction (Rubbing):**
When two different insulating materials are rubbed together, the friction provides enough energy to tear electrons away from the atoms of one material and transfer them to the other. Which material loses electrons depends on their respective electron affinities.
- **Glass and silk:** When a glass rod is rubbed with a piece of silk, electrons leave the glass and move to the silk. The glass becomes **positively charged**, and the silk becomes **negatively charged**.
- **Ebonite (or plastic) and fur:** When a hard plastic pen or ebonite rod is rubbed with fur or wool, electrons move from the fur to the ebonite. The ebonite becomes **negatively charged**, and the fur becomes **positively charged**. This is a common phenomenon during the dry Harmattan season in Nigeria; running a plastic comb through dry hair transfers electrons, allowing the negatively charged comb to attract small neutral pieces of paper.

**2. Charging by contact (Conduction):**
If you touch an uncharged metal conductor with a charged object, charge will flow between them. For example, if a negatively charged ebonite rod touches a neutral metal sphere, some of the excess electrons on the rod repel each other and flow onto the sphere. When the rod is removed, the sphere is left with a negative charge. In this method, the body acquires the **same** type of charge as the charging object.

**3. Charging by induction:**
Induction is a method used to charge a conductor without physically touching it with the charging object. The conductor acquires the **opposite** charge to the charging object. 

Here are the step-by-step instructions to charge a neutral metal sphere positively by induction:
1. Place a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand (so charge cannot escape to the ground).
2. Bring a negatively charged rod near (but not touching) the sphere. The rod repels the free electrons in the sphere to the far side, leaving the near side positively charged. (This separation of charge within the body is called induced charge).
3. While the charged rod is still held nearby, **earth (ground)** the sphere by briefly touching the far side with your finger or connecting it to the ground with a wire. The repelled electrons escape from the sphere and flow down into the earth.
4. Remove your finger (the earth connection) first.
5. Finally, remove the negatively charged rod. The sphere is now left with a net **positive charge**, which redistributes itself evenly over the surface.

## The electroscope

A gold-leaf electroscope is a sensitive laboratory instrument used in electrostatics to detect and analyse electric charges. 

**Structure:**
It consists of a brass cap connected to a vertical brass rod. The rod passes through an insulating rubber or sulphur stopper into a transparent glass jar (which protects the delicate parts from drafts of air). At the bottom of the rod inside the jar, there is a fixed brass plate and a very thin, lightweight gold (or aluminium) leaf hinged at the top.

### Uses of an electroscope

The electroscope has several important uses in the physics laboratory:

1. **To detect the presence of an electric charge:** 
   If a charged object is brought near the cap of an uncharged electroscope, it induces a charge on the cap and the opposite charge on the brass plate and gold leaf. Because the plate and the leaf receive the same type of charge, they repel each other, and the leaf diverges (spreads out).
2. **To test the sign of an unknown charge:** 
   To do this, the electroscope must first be given a known charge (for example, a positive charge, making the leaf diverge). If an unknown charged object is brought near the cap and the leaf diverges **further**, the unknown charge is also positive (like charges). If the leaf **collapses** (falls), the unknown charge is negative (or the object could be a neutral conductor).
3. **To test whether a material is a conductor or an insulator:** 
   If you touch the cap of a highly charged electroscope with an object held in your bare hand, the leaf will collapse instantly if the object is a good conductor (the charge flows through the object and your body to the ground). If the object is an insulator, the leaf remains diverged because charge cannot flow through it.

## Coulomb's inverse square law, electric field and potential

Electrostatic forces operate as "action-at-a-distance"; they do not require physical contact between the charges. The French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb formulated the exact mathematical relationship governing this force.

### Applying Coulomb's square law to solve problems

**Coulomb's Law** states that the electrostatic force of attraction or repulsion between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges, and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

The formula is:
$$F = \frac{k |q_1| |q_2|}{r^2}$$

Where:
- $F$ is the electrostatic force in Newtons ($\text{N}$).
- $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the amounts of charge in Coulombs ($\text{C}$).
- $r$ is the distance between the centres of the charges in meters ($\text{m}$).
- $k$ is Coulomb's constant, which depends on the medium separating the charges. In a vacuum or air, $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}$. (Here, $\varepsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space, measuring $8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F m}^{-1}$).

**Comparison with Gravitation:**
JAMB frequently asks you to compare Coulomb's law with Newton's law of gravitation. Both are inverse square laws (force varies as $1/r^2$). However, gravitational force is always attractive and does not depend on the medium, whereas electrostatic force can be attractive or repulsive and depends heavily on the medium between the charges. Furthermore, electrostatic forces are immensely stronger than gravitational forces.

**1. Worked Example: Calculating Electrostatic Force**
Calculate the force between a $+5\ \mu\text{C}$ charge and a $-4\ \mu\text{C}$ charge separated by a distance of $0.2\ \text{m}$ in a vacuum. (Take $k = 9 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}$).

**Solution:**
1. Identify and convert the given values: $q_1 = 5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$, $q_2 = 4 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$ (use absolute values for magnitude), $r = 0.2\ \text{m}$.
2. Write Coulomb's law: $F = \frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2}$.
3. Substitute the values with their units: $F = \frac{(9 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}) \times (5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}) \times (4 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C})}{(0.2\ \text{m})^2}$.
4. Calculate the numerator: $9 \times 5 \times 4 \times 10^{9 - 6 - 6} = 180 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N m}^2$.
5. Calculate the denominator: $(0.2)^2 = 0.04\ \text{m}^2$.
6. Divide: $F = \frac{180 \times 10^{-3}}{0.04} = 4.5\ \text{N}$.
The force is $4.5\ \text{N}$. Because the charges have opposite signs, this is an **attractive** force.

**2. Worked Example: Finding the Distance**
A repulsive force of $14.4\ \text{N}$ exists between two charges of $+2\ \mu\text{C}$ and $+8\ \mu\text{C}$. Find the distance between them.

**Solution:**
1. Identify given values: $F = 14.4\ \text{N}$, $q_1 = 2 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$, $q_2 = 8 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$.
2. Rearrange Coulomb's formula to make $r^2$ the subject: $r^2 = \frac{k q_1 q_2}{F}$.
3. Substitute: $r^2 = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (2 \times 10^{-6}) \times (8 \times 10^{-6})}{14.4}$.
4. Simplify numerator: $9 \times 2 \times 8 \times 10^{-3} = 144 \times 10^{-3}$.
5. Solve for $r^2$: $r^2 = \frac{144 \times 10^{-3}}{14.4} = 10 \times 10^{-3} = 0.01\ \text{m}^2$.
6. Take the square root: $r = \sqrt{0.01} = 0.1\ \text{m}$. (Which is $10\ \text{cm}$).

**3. Worked Example: Superposition of Forces**
Three point charges are placed on a straight line. A $+2\ \mu\text{C}$ charge is at the origin ($0\ \text{m}$). A $-3\ \mu\text{C}$ charge is at the $3\ \text{m}$ mark. A $+4\ \mu\text{C}$ charge is at the $5\ \text{m}$ mark. Calculate the net force on the $-3\ \mu\text{C}$ charge.

**Solution:**
When multiple charges are present, find the force from each charge independently, then combine them as vectors (considering direction).
1. Force from the $+2\ \mu\text{C}$ charge ($F_{12}$): $F_{12} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (2 \times 10^{-6}) \times (3 \times 10^{-6})}{3^2} = \frac{54 \times 10^{-3}}{9} = 0.006\ \text{N}$. This force is attractive, pulling the middle charge to the **left**.
2. Force from the $+4\ \mu\text{C}$ charge ($F_{32}$): The distance is $5\ \text{m} - 3\ \text{m} = 2\ \text{m}$.
   $F_{32} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (4 \times 10^{-6}) \times (3 \times 10^{-6})}{2^2} = \frac{108 \times 10^{-3}}{4} = 0.027\ \text{N}$. This force is attractive, pulling the middle charge to the **right**.
3. Net force = $0.027\ \text{N} \text{ (right)} - 0.006\ \text{N} \text{ (left)} = 0.021\ \text{N}$ towards the right.

## Electric field intensity, potential and potential difference

An **electric field** is a region of space around a charged object where another electric charge will experience an electrostatic force. 

### Deducing expressions for electric field intensity and potential difference

**Electric Field Intensity ($E$):**
The electric field intensity (or electric field strength) at any point is defined as the electrostatic force experienced per unit positive test charge placed at that point. 
$$E = \frac{F}{q}$$
By substituting Coulomb's law ($F = \frac{k q Q}{r^2}$) into this definition, we can deduce the mathematical expression for the electric field intensity produced by a central point charge $Q$ at a distance $r$:
$$E = \frac{\left(\frac{k q Q}{r^2}\right)}{q} = \frac{k Q}{r^2}$$
- Electric field intensity is a **vector** quantity. Its direction is the direction a positive test charge would move (away from positive, towards negative).
- Its SI unit is Newtons per Coulomb ($\text{N C}^{-1}$) or Volts per meter ($\text{V m}^{-1}$).

**Electric Potential ($V$):**
Electric potential is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing a small test charge from infinity down to a specific point against the electric field.
$$V = \frac{W}{q}$$
From advanced mechanics, doing work against an inverse-square force gives a $1/r$ relationship. Thus, the potential at a distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$ is deduced as:
$$V = \frac{k Q}{r}$$
- Electric potential is a **scalar** quantity. It has no direction. When calculating potential, you must include the negative sign for negative charges (a negative charge creates a negative potential well). 
- Its SI unit is Joules per Coulomb ($\text{J C}^{-1}$), which is called the Volt ($\text{V}$).

**Potential Difference (P.D.):**
The potential difference between two points, A and B, in an electric field is the work done per unit charge in moving a positive test charge from point A to point B.
$$V_B - V_A = \frac{W}{q}$$

**4. Worked Example: Electric Field Intensity**
Calculate the electric field intensity at a distance of $40\ \text{cm}$ from a point charge of $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ in a vacuum.

**Solution:**
1. Identify given values: $Q = 16 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$, $r = 40\ \text{cm} = 0.4\ \text{m}$.
2. Formula: $E = \frac{k Q}{r^2}$.
3. Substitute: $E = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times 16 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.4)^2}$.
4. Calculate: $E = \frac{144 \times 10^3}{0.16} = 900 \times 10^3 = 9.0 \times 10^5\ \text{N C}^{-1}$.
(Because the source charge is positive, the field is directed outwards away from it).

**5. Worked Example: Finding the Neutral Point**
Two point charges, $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ and $+4\ \mu\text{C}$, are separated by a distance of $3.0\ \text{m}$ in a vacuum. At what distance from the $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ charge is the net electric field zero (the neutral point)?

**Solution:**
1. At the neutral point, the electric field from the first charge ($E_1$) exactly balances the electric field from the second charge ($E_2$) in magnitude, pointing in opposite directions.
2. Let the distance from the $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ charge be $x$. The distance from the $+4\ \mu\text{C}$ charge is therefore $(3.0 - x)$.
3. Equate the fields: $\frac{k \times 16 \times 10^{-6}}{x^2} = \frac{k \times 4 \times 10^{-6}}{(3.0 - x)^2}$.
4. Cancel out the common terms ($k$ and $10^{-6}$): $\frac{16}{x^2} = \frac{4}{(3.0 - x)^2}$.
5. To avoid complicated quadratic equations, simply take the square root of both sides: $\frac{4}{x} = \frac{2}{3.0 - x}$.
6. Cross-multiply: $4(3.0 - x) = 2x \implies 12 - 4x = 2x$.
7. Solve for $x$: $6x = 12 \implies x = 2.0\ \text{m}$.
The neutral point is $2.0\ \text{m}$ away from the $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ charge.

**6. Worked Example: Potential Difference and Work**
The work done in moving a charge of $+5\ \text{C}$ from point A to point B in an electric field is $120\ \text{J}$. Find the potential difference between the two points.

**Solution:**
1. Formula: $V_B - V_A = \frac{W}{q}$.
2. Substitute: $V_B - V_A = \frac{120\ \text{J}}{5\ \text{C}} = 24\ \text{V}$.

### Electric field flux patterns of isolated and interacting charges

Electric field lines (or lines of force) are imaginary lines drawn to represent the direction and strength of an electric field. 

**Properties of electric field lines:**
1. They originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges.
2. They never cross or intersect each other.
3. The tangent to a field line at any given point gives the direction of the electric field intensity at that point.
4. The closeness (density) of the lines indicates the strength of the field; closer lines mean a stronger field.

**JAMB Flux Patterns to Identify:**
- **Isolated positive charge:** The field lines radiate straight outwards in all directions, as a positive test charge would be repelled away.
- **Isolated negative charge:** The field lines radiate straight inwards from all directions.
- **Unlike charges (+ and -):** Lines leave the positive charge and curve over to enter the negative charge. The lines act like stretched elastic bands, pulling the two charges together.
- **Like charges (+ and +):** Lines leave both charges but bend sharply away from each other to avoid crossing. This creates an empty region in the dead centre where there are no lines. This empty region is a **neutral point** where the net electric field is zero.

## Electric discharge and lightning

At normal pressure, air is a very good insulator. However, if a massive potential difference is applied across a gap, the intense electric field rapidly accelerates stray electrons. These electrons smash into neutral air molecules, knocking off more electrons in a process called ionization by collision. This creates an avalanche of charged particles, allowing the gas to suddenly conduct electricity. This sudden flow of charge is called an **electric discharge**, often accompanied by a spark, heat, and a flash of light.

### Distribution of charges on a conductor and lightning conductors

When a solid or hollow metal conductor is given an electric charge, the static charge resides entirely on its **outer surface**. There is strictly zero net charge inside the conductor.

Furthermore, the charge does not always spread out evenly:
- On a perfect spherical conductor, the surface charge density is uniform.
- On irregularly shaped conductors, the charge heavily concentrates wherever the surface is most sharply curved. Therefore, the surface charge density is highest at **sharp points** and thin edges.

**Corona Discharge (Point Action):**
Because electric charge concentrates heavily at sharp points, the electric field just outside the point becomes extraordinarily intense. This intense field ionises the surrounding air. Oppositely charged air ions are attracted to the metal point and neutralize some of its charge, while similarly charged ions are violently repelled away, creating a draft called an "electric wind". This rapid leaking away of charge from sharp points is called **point action** or corona discharge.

**Lightning and Lightning Conductors:**
During intense tropical thunderstorms (common at the onset and end of the rainy season in Nigeria), friction between updrafts of water droplets and falling ice particles causes storm clouds to become highly charged. The base of the cloud usually becomes strongly negative, which induces a massive positive charge on the Earth's surface, tall trees, and buildings below. When the potential difference grows to millions of volts, the air insulation breaks down, and a massive electric discharge leaps between the cloud and the Earth. This is **lightning**.

To protect tall buildings (and telecom masts) from destructive lightning strikes, a **lightning conductor** is installed.
- **Construction:** It consists of a thick copper strip running from the highest point of the building deep into the wet ground (earthing). At the very top, it ends in several sharp copper spikes pointing at the sky.
- **How it works:**
  1. **Preventive action (Point action):** The intense induced positive charge on the sharp spikes ionises the air. Positive ions are repelled upwards towards the negatively charged cloud. This quietly helps to neutralize the cloud's charge, reducing the likelihood of a strike occurring at all.
  2. **Safe path (Discharge):** If the cloud's charge builds up too fast and a lightning bolt does strike, it will preferentially hit the highly conductive copper spikes rather than the roof. The thick copper strip provides a low-resistance path, conducting the massive electric current safely into the ground, preventing fires and structural damage to the building.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Unit conversions are vital:** JAMB questions frequently give charges in microcoulombs ($\mu\text{C}$). You must always convert them to Coulombs by multiplying by $10^{-6}$ before plugging them into Coulomb's law. Also, distances given in $\text{cm}$ must be converted to meters ($\text{m}$).
- **Forgetting to square the distance:** The most common mathematical mistake in Coulomb's law ($F = \frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2}$) and electric field intensity calculations is dividing by $r$ instead of $r^2$.
- **Scalars vs. Vectors:** Electric Field Intensity ($E$) is a **vector**; when finding the net field from multiple charges, you must consider their directions (left/right). Electric Potential ($V$) is a **scalar**; you simply add the values algebraically.
- **Negative signs in potential:** Because potential is a scalar, a negative charge produces a negative potential. The formula is $V = \frac{k Q}{r}$. If $Q$ is negative, $V$ is negative. Do not ignore the sign!
- **Attraction is not a sure test:** Remember that a charged body can attract a neutral body via induction. Only **repulsion** guarantees that both bodies are charged and have the same sign.

## Quick summary

1. All matter contains protons (positive) and electrons (negative). A body becomes negatively charged by gaining electrons and positively charged by losing electrons.
2. Bodies can be charged by friction (rubbing), contact (conduction), and induction. Induction gives a conductor a charge opposite to the charging body without physical contact.
3. A gold-leaf electroscope is used to detect the presence of charge, determine its sign, and test whether materials are conductors or insulators.
4. Coulomb's inverse square law states that $F = \frac{k |q_1| |q_2|}{r^2}$, meaning the force drops rapidly as the distance between charges increases.
5. Electric field intensity ($E = \frac{F}{q} = \frac{k Q}{r^2}$) is a vector representing force per unit charge. Electric potential ($V = \frac{W}{q} = \frac{k Q}{r}$) is a scalar representing work per unit charge.
6. Electric field lines start on positive charges, end on negative charges, never cross, and are closest where the field is strongest.
7. Static charge resides entirely on the outer surface of conductors and concentrates heavily at sharp points, leading to point action (leaking of charge).
8. Lightning conductors protect buildings using sharp spikes that utilize point action to neutralize clouds, and provide a low-resistance path to the earth if a strike occurs.

## Practice questions

**1. A glass rod rubbed with silk becomes positively charged because it:**

- A. gains electrons from the silk
- B. loses electrons to the silk
- C. gains protons from the silk
- D. loses protons to the silk

**Answer: B.** In solid materials, only the lightweight electrons can move. The friction transfers electrons from the glass to the silk, leaving the glass with a deficit of electrons, hence a positive charge.

**2. Which of the following is NOT a practical use of a gold-leaf electroscope?**

- A. To detect the presence of an electric charge
- B. To determine the sign of an electric charge
- C. To measure the exact magnitude of the electrostatic force in Newtons
- D. To test whether a material is a conductor or an insulator

**Answer: C.** An electroscope is a qualitative tool used to detect charge, determine its sign (if previously charged), and test conductivity. It does not provide an exact quantitative measurement of force in Newtons.

**3. Two point charges, each $3\ \mu\text{C}$, are separated by a distance of $30\ \text{cm}$ in a vacuum. The force of repulsion between them is:** (Take $k = 9 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}$)

- A. $0.9\ \text{N}$
- B. $0.09\ \text{N}$
- C. $0.27\ \text{N}$
- D. $9.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{N}$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Convert units: $q_1 = q_2 = 3 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$, $r = 0.3\ \text{m}$.
2. Use Coulomb's law: $F = \frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (3 \times 10^{-6}) \times (3 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.3)^2}$
3. $F = \frac{81 \times 10^{-3}}{0.09} = 0.9\ \text{N}$. 
The mistake behind option C is forgetting to square the distance ($0.3\ \text{m}$), and the mistake behind option D is using $30$ directly without converting to meters.

**4. The electric field intensity at a distance of $5\ \text{cm}$ from a point charge is $1.44 \times 10^7\ \text{N C}^{-1}$. Calculate the magnitude of the charge.** (Take $k = 9 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}$)

- A. $4.0\ \mu\text{C}$
- B. $8.0\ \mu\text{C}$
- C. $80.0\ \mu\text{C}$
- D. $4.0 \times 10^4\ \mu\text{C}$

**Answer: A.**
1. $E = \frac{k Q}{r^2}$, therefore $Q = \frac{E r^2}{k}$.
2. Convert $r$ to meters: $r = 0.05\ \text{m}$.
3. $Q = \frac{1.44 \times 10^7 \times (0.05)^2}{9 \times 10^9} = \frac{1.44 \times 10^7 \times 0.0025}{9 \times 10^9}$.
4. $Q = \frac{36000}{9 \times 10^9} = 4 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C} = 4.0\ \mu\text{C}$.
The mistake behind option C is forgetting to square the $0.05\ \text{m}$ distance in the formula.

**5. The work done in moving a charge of $2\ \mu\text{C}$ from a point of potential $50\ \text{V}$ to another point P is $4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$. Find the potential at point P.**

- A. $150\ \text{V}$
- B. $200\ \text{V}$
- C. $250\ \text{V}$
- D. $300\ \text{V}$

**Answer: C.**
1. $W = q(V_P - V_{initial})$.
2. $4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J} = 2 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C} \times (V_P - 50\ \text{V})$.
3. Divide by charge: $200\ \text{V} = V_P - 50\ \text{V}$.
4. Add 50 to both sides: $V_P = 250\ \text{V}$.
The mistake behind option B is solving for the potential difference ($200\ \text{V}$) but forgetting to add the initial potential of $50\ \text{V}$.

**6. Which of the following statements about electric lines of force is NOT true?**

- A. They originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges.
- B. They never cross each other.
- C. They form continuous closed loops.
- D. The tangent to a line of force at any point gives the direction of the electric field at that point.

**Answer: C.** Electric field lines do not form closed loops; they start on a positive charge and end on a negative charge (or go to infinity). Magnetic field lines, on the other hand, form continuous closed loops.

**7. Why are the tips of lightning conductors made sharp?**

- A. To easily pierce the clouds during a storm.
- B. To create a highly intense electric field that allows charge to leak away (point action).
- C. To reduce the surface area and minimize wind resistance.
- D. To prevent the accumulation of moisture and rust on the copper.

**Answer: B.** Charge concentrates heavily at sharp points, creating an intense electric field that ionises the air. This point action helps to neutralize the cloud and provide a preferential path for lightning.

**8. An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of $1000\ \text{V}$. The kinetic energy gained by the electron is:** (Charge on an electron = $1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$)

- A. $1.6 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$
- B. $1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$
- C. $1.6 \times 10^{-22}\ \text{J}$
- D. $1.0 \times 10^3\ \text{J}$

**Answer: A.**
1. The work done on the electron converts entirely to kinetic energy: $W = qV$.
2. $W = (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}) \times 1000\ \text{V} = 1.6 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$.
The mistake behind option B is failing to multiply the charge by the potential difference (effectively multiplying by 1 instead of 1000).

**9. Calculate the electric potential at a point $50\ \text{cm}$ from a point charge of $-4\ \mu\text{C}$ in a vacuum.** (Take $k = 9 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}$)

- A. $7.2 \times 10^4\ \text{V}$
- B. $-7.2 \times 10^4\ \text{V}$
- C. $-1.44 \times 10^5\ \text{V}$
- D. $-7.2 \times 10^2\ \text{V}$

**Answer: B.**
1. $V = \frac{k Q}{r}$. (Electric potential is a scalar; retain the negative sign).
2. $r = 0.5\ \text{m}$.
3. $V = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (-4 \times 10^{-6})}{0.5} = \frac{-36000}{0.5} = -72000\ \text{V} = -7.2 \times 10^4\ \text{V}$.
The mistake behind option C is dividing by $r^2$ ($0.25$) instead of $r$, which is the formula for electric field intensity, not potential.

**10. Two point charges, $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ and $+4\ \mu\text{C}$, are separated by a distance of $3.0\ \text{m}$ in a vacuum. At what distance from the $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ charge is the net electric field zero?**

- A. $1.0\ \text{m}$
- B. $1.5\ \text{m}$
- C. $2.0\ \text{m}$
- D. $2.5\ \text{m}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Let the neutral point be $x$ meters from the $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ charge. The distance from the $+4\ \mu\text{C}$ charge is $(3 - x)$.
2. Equate the fields: $\frac{16}{x^2} = \frac{4}{(3-x)^2}$.
3. Take the square root of both sides: $\frac{4}{x} = \frac{2}{3-x}$.
4. Cross multiply: $12 - 4x = 2x \implies 6x = 12 \implies x = 2.0\ \text{m}$.
The mistake behind option A is calculating the distance from the $+4\ \mu\text{C}$ charge instead of the $+16\ \mu\text{C}$ charge.
