## What is an electric cell?

An **electric cell** is a device that converts chemical energy (or light energy, in the case of solar cells) into electrical energy. When connected in a closed circuit, a cell provides the **electromotive force (e.m.f)** needed to drive an electric current through the circuit. A combination of two or more cells is called a **battery**. 

Cells are broadly divided into two groups:
1. **Primary cells:** These cannot be recharged once their chemical energy is exhausted (e.g., standard torchlight batteries).
2. **Secondary cells (Accumulators):** These can be recharged by passing a direct current (d.c.) through them in the opposite direction (e.g., car batteries, phone batteries).

## Identify the defects of the simple voltaic cell and their correction

The **simple voltaic cell** is the most basic electric cell. It consists of two dissimilar metal plates (electrodes) placed in an electrolyte. Typically, it uses a **copper plate** (the positive electrode or anode) and a **zinc plate** (the negative electrode or cathode) dipped in **dilute tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid** ($H_2SO_4$).

When connected to an external circuit, current flows from the copper to the zinc. However, the simple cell is rarely used in practice because it suffers from two major defects: **polarization** and **local action**.

### 1. Polarization
**Cause:** As the cell works, hydrogen gas is produced by the chemical reaction. These hydrogen bubbles cling to the surface of the positive copper plate. Because hydrogen is an insulator, the layer of bubbles increases the internal resistance of the cell. Worse, it sets up a "back e.m.f." that opposes the main e.m.f. of the cell, causing the current to drop rapidly to zero.
**Correction:** Polarization is prevented by using a **depolarizer**. A depolarizer is an oxidizing agent (such as manganese (IV) oxide, $MnO_2$, or potassium heptaoxodichromate (VI), $K_2Cr_2O_7$) that reacts with the hydrogen gas to form water, keeping the copper plate clear. In some cells, a porous pot is used to separate fluids and manage this defect mechanically.

### 2. Local action
**Cause:** The commercial zinc used in the negative plate contains tiny impurities like iron and carbon. When placed in the acid, these impurities form microscopic "local cells" on the surface of the zinc plate. These local cells cause the zinc to dissolve and waste away, producing hydrogen bubbles even when the main cell circuit is completely disconnected.
**Correction:** Local action is prevented by **amalgamation**. The zinc plate is rubbed with mercury to form a uniform coating called a zinc amalgam. The mercury covers up the impurities, preventing them from coming into contact with the acid, but still allows the pure zinc to dissolve and produce electricity.

## Compare different types of cells including solar cell

Various practical primary and secondary cells have been developed to overcome the defects of the simple cell and provide reliable power for different uses.

### Daniell and Leclanché cells

These are standard primary cells.

1. **Daniell Cell:** 
   This cell uses a two-fluid design to prevent polarization. It consists of a copper vessel (positive electrode) containing a solution of copper (II) tetraoxosulphate (VI) ($CuSO_4$), which acts as the **depolarizer**. Inside this is a porous pot containing dilute $H_2SO_4$ and an amalgamated zinc rod (negative electrode). The porous pot allows ions to pass while keeping the two liquids separate. It supplies a steady e.m.f of about $1.1\ \text{V}$ and is mostly used in laboratories.
2. **Leclanché Cell (Wet):** 
   Consists of a glass jar filled with ammonium chloride ($NH_4Cl$) solution as the electrolyte. A zinc rod serves as the negative electrode. The positive electrode is a carbon rod placed inside a porous pot, packed tightly with a mixture of manganese (IV) oxide ($MnO_2$) and powdered carbon. The $MnO_2$ is the **depolarizer**. It gives an e.m.f of about $1.5\ \text{V}$. Because polarization is only cleared slowly, this cell is only used for intermittent power (e.g., old electric bells).
3. **Leclanché Cell (Dry):** 
   This is the standard "dry cell" used in torchlights, radios, and remote controls. It works on the same principle as the wet Leclanché cell but uses a moist paste of ammonium chloride instead of a liquid. The zinc acts as both the negative electrode and the outer container. The e.m.f is about $1.5\ \text{V}$.

### Secondary accumulators and modern cells

Secondary cells can be recharged and are crucial for heavy-duty or long-term energy storage.

1. **Lead-Acid Accumulator:** 
   The most common car battery (and used with solar inverters in Nigeria). The positive plate is lead (IV) oxide ($PbO_2$) and the negative plate is spongy lead ($Pb$). The electrolyte is dilute tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid ($H_2SO_4$). A single cell provides an e.m.f of about $2.0\ \text{V}$.
2. **Nickel-Iron (NiFe) Accumulator:** 
   Also known as the Edison accumulator. The positive plate is nickel (IV) oxide and the negative plate is iron. The electrolyte is a solution of potassium hydroxide ($KOH$). It provides an e.m.f of about $1.2\ \text{V}$.
3. **Lithium-Ion (Li-ion) Cells:** 
   Modern, lightweight rechargeable batteries used in smartphones, laptops, and electric vehicles. They have a very high energy density (store a lot of energy for their size) and do not suffer from memory effects, but require careful electronic management to prevent overheating.
4. **Mercury-Cadmium Cells:** 
   Often shaped as small "button cells" used in wristwatches and hearing aids. They provide a very steady and precise voltage (about $1.35\ \text{V}$) over a long period.
5. **Solar Cells:** 
   Unlike chemical cells, a solar cell (photovoltaic cell) uses semiconductor materials (like silicon) to convert light energy directly into electrical energy. When sunlight strikes the cell, it dislodges electrons, creating a current. They are highly sustainable and widely used in Nigeria to power streetlights and boreholes.

| Feature | Primary Cells (Dry/Wet) | Lead-Acid Accumulator | NiFe Accumulator | Solar Cell |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rechargeable?** | No | Yes | Yes | N/A (generates continuously in light) |
| **Energy Source** | Chemical | Chemical | Chemical | Light (Sun) |
| **Typical e.m.f.** | $1.5\ \text{V}$ | $2.0\ \text{V}$ | $1.2\ \text{V}$ | $\approx 0.5\ \text{V}$ to $0.6\ \text{V}$ per cell |
| **Electrolyte** | $NH_4Cl$ paste/solution | Dilute $H_2SO_4$ (acidic) | $KOH$ solution (alkaline) | None (solid-state semiconductor) |

## Compare the advantages of lead-acid and Nickel-Iron accumulator

Both lead-acid and Nickel-Iron (NiFe) accumulators are popular secondary cells, but they have different strengths. In JAMB, you are frequently asked to contrast their practical advantages.

### Advantages of the NiFe accumulator over Lead-Acid
* **Robustness:** The NiFe cell is mechanically stronger and can withstand severe vibration and rough handling. 
* **Overcharge tolerance:** It is not easily damaged by overcharging or being left in a discharged state for a long time. 
* **Longer lifespan:** It generally lasts many more years than a lead-acid battery.
* **Lighter weight:** It is much lighter and highly portable.

### Advantages of the Lead-Acid accumulator over NiFe
* **Higher e.m.f.:** A lead-acid cell produces about $2.0\ \text{V}$ compared to the $1.2\ \text{V}$ of a NiFe cell, meaning fewer cells are needed to build a $12\ \text{V}$ battery.
* **Lower internal resistance:** Lead-acid batteries can supply very large currents for short periods, which is absolutely essential for starting heavy car engines. The NiFe cell cannot supply such high burst currents.
* **Cost:** Lead-acid batteries are significantly cheaper to manufacture.

## Maintenance of cells and batteries

Proper maintenance ensures a long lifespan for batteries, particularly the lead-acid accumulators commonly used for "keke NAPEPs", cars, and home inverters.
1. **Check the electrolyte level:** The acid level must cover the plates. When it drops due to evaporation during charging, it must be topped up **only with distilled water**, never with more acid.
2. **Check the state of charge:** Use a **hydrometer** to measure the specific gravity (relative density) of the acid. A fully charged lead-acid cell has a specific gravity of about $1.25$. If it drops below $1.15$, it must be recharged.
3. **Keep terminals clean:** Terminals should be cleaned of corrosion and smeared with petroleum jelly (Vaseline) to ensure good electrical contact.
4. **Avoid leaving it uncharged:** Leaving a lead-acid battery uncharged for a long time causes "sulphation" (the formation of hard white lead sulphate on the plates), which ruins the battery.
5. **Never short-circuit the terminals:** Connecting the positive and negative terminals directly with a wire draws a massive current that can overheat, warp the plates, and destroy the cell.

## Arrangement of cells

To obtain a desired electromotive force (e.m.f) or to supply a required current, multiple cells are grouped together to form a battery. Every cell has its own e.m.f, represented by the symbol $E$ (unit: Volts, $\text{V}$), and its own internal resistance, represented by $r$ (unit: ohms, $\Omega$). Cells can be arranged in series or in parallel.

### Series combination
In a series arrangement, the positive terminal of one cell is connected to the negative terminal of the next. 
* **Why use it?** To obtain a **higher total e.m.f.**
* **Total e.m.f:** For $n$ identical cells in series, $E_{\text{total}} = nE$.
* **Total internal resistance:** $r_{\text{total}} = nr$.

### Parallel combination
In a parallel arrangement, all the positive terminals are joined together to form one point, and all the negative terminals are joined to form another. **Note:** Only identical cells (same $E$) should be connected in parallel.
* **Why use it?** To obtain a **larger current capacity** without increasing the e.m.f, and to lower total internal resistance.
* **Total e.m.f:** For $n$ identical cells in parallel, the total e.m.f is just the e.m.f of ONE cell: $E_{\text{total}} = E$.
* **Total internal resistance:** Since the internal resistances are in parallel, $r_{\text{total}} = \frac{r}{n}$.

## Solve problems involving series and parallel combination of cells

When a battery is connected to an external resistor $R$ (unit: $\Omega$), the current $I$ (unit: Amperes, $\text{A}$) flowing through the circuit can be calculated using Ohm's law applied to the whole circuit:

$$I = \frac{\text{Total e.m.f}}{\text{Total external resistance } + \text{ Total internal resistance}}$$

**Formula for Series:**
$$I = \frac{nE}{R + nr}$$

**Formula for Parallel:**
$$I = \frac{E}{R + \frac{r}{n}}$$

### Worked Example 1: Cells in Series
Four identical cells, each of e.m.f $1.5\ \text{V}$ and internal resistance $0.5\ \Omega$, are connected in series. They are used to drive a current through an external resistor of $4.0\ \Omega$. Calculate the current flowing in the circuit.

**Steps:**
1. State the formula for a series combination: $I = \frac{nE}{R + nr}$.
2. Substitute the given values: $n = 4$, $E = 1.5\ \text{V}$, $r = 0.5\ \Omega$, $R = 4.0\ \Omega$.
3. Calculate the total e.m.f: $E_{\text{total}} = 4 \times 1.5\ \text{V} = 6.0\ \text{V}$.
4. Calculate the total internal resistance: $r_{\text{total}} = 4 \times 0.5\ \Omega = 2.0\ \Omega$.
5. Calculate the current: $I = \frac{6.0\ \text{V}}{4.0\ \Omega + 2.0\ \Omega} = \frac{6.0\ \text{V}}{6.0\ \Omega} = 1.0\ \text{A}$.
6. State the final answer with its unit: The current is $1.0\ \text{A}$.

### Worked Example 2: Cells in Parallel
Three identical cells, each of e.m.f $2.0\ \text{V}$ and internal resistance $1.5\ \Omega$, are connected in parallel across an external resistor of $3.5\ \Omega$. Find the current in the resistor.

**Steps:**
1. State the formula for a parallel combination: $I = \frac{E}{R + \frac{r}{n}}$.
2. Substitute the given values: $n = 3$, $E = 2.0\ \text{V}$, $r = 1.5\ \Omega$, $R = 3.5\ \Omega$.
3. Note the total e.m.f remains the same: $E_{\text{total}} = 2.0\ \text{V}$.
4. Calculate the total internal resistance: $r_{\text{total}} = \frac{1.5\ \Omega}{3} = 0.5\ \Omega$.
5. Calculate the current: $I = \frac{2.0\ \text{V}}{3.5\ \Omega + 0.5\ \Omega} = \frac{2.0\ \text{V}}{4.0\ \Omega} = 0.5\ \text{A}$.
6. State the final answer with its unit: The current is $0.5\ \text{A}$.

> **Common Mistakes:** A very common mistake JAMB candidates make is multiplying the e.m.f by the number of cells when they are in **parallel**. Remember, $4$ cells of $1.5\ \text{V}$ in parallel still only give a total e.m.f of $1.5\ \text{V}$, NOT $6.0\ \text{V}$.

## Efficiency of a cell

The efficiency of any device is the ratio of useful power output to the total power generated. 
For a cell driving a current $I$ through an external resistor $R$:
* **Useful power output** is the power dissipated in the external resistor: $P_{\text{out}} = I^2 R$
* **Total power generated** is the total power of the cell, which covers both the external circuit and the internal resistance $r$: $P_{\text{total}} = I^2 (R + r)$

The efficiency ($\eta$) is given by:
$$\eta = \frac{I^2 R}{I^2 (R + r)} \times 100\%$$
$$\eta = \frac{R}{R + r} \times 100\%$$

Alternatively, since the terminal potential difference (p.d) across the external resistor is $V = IR$ and the total e.m.f is $E = I(R + r)$, the efficiency can also be written as:
$$\eta = \frac{V}{E} \times 100\%$$

### Worked Example 3: Cell Efficiency
A cell of e.m.f $2.0\ \text{V}$ and internal resistance $1.0\ \Omega$ is connected to an external resistor of $4.0\ \Omega$. Calculate the efficiency of the cell.

**Steps:**
1. State the formula for cell efficiency: $\eta = \frac{R}{R + r} \times 100\%$.
2. Substitute the values: $R = 4.0\ \Omega$, $r = 1.0\ \Omega$.
3. Calculate the denominator (total resistance): $4.0\ \Omega + 1.0\ \Omega = 5.0\ \Omega$.
4. Calculate the fraction: $\frac{4.0\ \Omega}{5.0\ \Omega} = 0.8$.
5. Multiply by $100\%$ to find the final percentage: $0.8 \times 100\% = 80\%$.
6. State the final answer: The efficiency of the cell is $80\%$. (Note: Efficiency is a ratio and has no unit, only the percentage symbol).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Primary cell** | A cell that cannot be recharged after its chemical energy is depleted. |
| **Secondary cell** | An accumulator that can be recharged by passing a direct current through it backwards. |
| **Electromotive force (e.m.f)** | The total energy per unit charge supplied by a cell to drive current around a complete circuit. |
| **Polarization** | A defect in simple cells caused by the accumulation of hydrogen bubbles on the positive plate. |
| **Depolarizer** | An oxidizing chemical (like $MnO_2$) added to a cell to remove hydrogen bubbles and prevent polarization. |
| **Local action** | A defect where impurities in the zinc plate cause it to dissolve and waste away even when not in use. |
| **Amalgamation** | The process of rubbing mercury on a zinc plate to prevent local action. |
| **Internal resistance ($r$)** | The resistance to current flow found inside the cell itself, caused by its chemicals. |
| **Efficiency** | The ratio of the useful external power output to the total power generated by the cell, expressed as a percentage. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

* **Learn the defects and fixes directly:** Polarization = hydrogen bubbles = use a depolarizer. Local action = zinc impurities = use mercury (amalgamation). 
* **Know your cell components:** JAMB loves asking for the depolarizer in a Leclanché cell (it is Manganese (IV) oxide, $MnO_2$) or the plates in a lead-acid cell (Lead dioxide is positive, spongy Lead is negative).
* **Parallel e.m.f trap:** If $10$ identical cells of $2\ \text{V}$ each are in parallel, the total e.m.f is still $2\ \text{V}$. Do not multiply e.m.f in parallel configurations.
* **Distilled water only:** When asked what liquid is used to "top up" a lead-acid battery, always pick "distilled water", not acid. The acid does not evaporate; only the water does.
* **Efficiency formula logic:** Efficiency is simply $\frac{R}{R+r}$, which is the ratio of the external resistor to the total resistance. Do not accidentally flip it to $\frac{r}{R+r}$.

## Quick summary

1. Electric cells convert chemical or light energy into electrical energy. Primary cells are non-rechargeable, while secondary cells (accumulators) are rechargeable.
2. The simple voltaic cell suffers from polarization (hydrogen shielding the positive plate) and local action (impurities dissolving the zinc).
3. Polarization is corrected by using a depolarizer (like $MnO_2$); local action is corrected by amalgamating the zinc plate with mercury.
4. The lead-acid accumulator provides high current and higher e.m.f, making it ideal for starting cars, but the NiFe accumulator is more robust, lighter, and tolerant of overcharging.
5. Cells are arranged in series to maximize total e.m.f ($E_{\text{total}} = nE$), and in parallel to increase current capacity without increasing e.m.f ($E_{\text{total}} = E$).
6. To keep a lead-acid battery functioning, check its specific gravity with a hydrometer and top it up only with distilled water.
7. The efficiency of a cell is given by $\frac{R}{R+r} \times 100\%$ or $\frac{V}{E} \times 100\%$.

## Practice questions

**1. Polarization in a simple voltaic cell is caused by the accumulation of**
- A. oxygen bubbles on the zinc plate
- B. hydrogen bubbles on the zinc plate
- C. hydrogen bubbles on the copper plate
- D. oxygen bubbles on the copper plate

**Answer: C.** Polarization occurs when hydrogen gas bubbles form on and insulate the positive plate, which is the copper plate in a simple cell.

**2. Which of the following is used to correct local action in a simple cell?**
- A. Adding manganese (IV) oxide to the acid
- B. Rubbing the zinc plate with mercury
- C. Using a porous pot to separate the plates
- D. Replacing the copper plate with a carbon rod

**Answer: B.** Local action is caused by impurities in the zinc and is prevented by coating (amalgamating) the zinc plate with mercury. Option A corrects polarization.

**3. What is the function of manganese (IV) oxide in a Leclanché cell?**
- A. It acts as the positive electrode.
- B. It increases the internal resistance of the cell.
- C. It serves as a depolarizer.
- D. It acts as the main electrolyte.

**Answer: C.** Manganese (IV) oxide ($MnO_2$) acts as an oxidizing agent (depolarizer) to react with the hydrogen gas and turn it into water, preventing polarization.

**4. A major advantage of the Nickel-Iron (NiFe) accumulator over the lead-acid accumulator is that it**
- A. produces a higher electromotive force
- B. has a lower internal resistance
- C. can withstand severe vibration and rough handling
- D. is cheaper to manufacture

**Answer: C.** The NiFe cell is mechanically robust and withstands rough handling and overcharging better than the lead-acid cell. Options A, B, and D are advantages of the lead-acid accumulator.

**5. Five identical cells, each of e.m.f $2.0\ \text{V}$ and internal resistance $0.4\ \Omega$, are connected in series across an $8.0\ \Omega$ resistor. What is the current flowing in the circuit?**
- A. $0.24\ \text{A}$
- B. $1.0\ \text{A}$
- C. $1.23\ \text{A}$
- D. $1.25\ \text{A}$

**Answer: B.** 
1. Formula: $I = \frac{nE}{R + nr}$
2. $E_{\text{total}} = 5 \times 2.0\ \text{V} = 10.0\ \text{V}$
3. $r_{\text{total}} = 5 \times 0.4\ \Omega = 2.0\ \Omega$
4. $I = \frac{10.0\ \text{V}}{8.0\ \Omega + 2.0\ \Omega} = \frac{10.0}{10.0} = 1.0\ \text{A}$.
Mistake in A: Failing to multiply the e.m.f and internal resistance by $5$.

**6. Four identical cells, each of e.m.f $1.5\ \text{V}$ and internal resistance $2.0\ \Omega$, are connected in parallel to an external resistor of $1.5\ \Omega$. Calculate the current in the circuit.**
- A. $0.16\ \text{A}$
- B. $0.63\ \text{A}$
- C. $0.75\ \text{A}$
- D. $3.0\ \text{A}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Formula: $I = \frac{E}{R + \frac{r}{n}}$
2. Total e.m.f remains $1.5\ \text{V}$ (cells in parallel do not multiply e.m.f).
3. Total internal resistance $r_{\text{total}} = \frac{2.0\ \Omega}{4} = 0.5\ \Omega$.
4. $I = \frac{1.5\ \text{V}}{1.5\ \Omega + 0.5\ \Omega} = \frac{1.5}{2.0} = 0.75\ \text{A}$.
Mistake in D: Multiplying the e.m.f by $4$ to get $6.0\ \text{V}$, yielding $6.0 / 2.0 = 3.0\ \text{A}$. E.m.f is not added in parallel.

**7. When the acid level in a lead-acid accumulator drops, it should be topped up with**
- A. concentrated tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid
- B. dilute tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid
- C. tap water
- D. distilled water

**Answer: D.** During use and charging, only the water evaporates or breaks down; the acid remains. Topping up is done strictly with distilled water to restore the level without adding impurities.

**8. A cell of e.m.f $3.0\ \text{V}$ supplies a current through an external resistor. If the terminal potential difference across the resistor is $2.4\ \text{V}$, what is the efficiency of the cell?**
- A. $0.8\%$
- B. $20\%$
- C. $80\%$
- D. $125\%$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Efficiency $\eta = \frac{V}{E} \times 100\%$
2. $\eta = \frac{2.4\ \text{V}}{3.0\ \text{V}} \times 100\% = 0.8 \times 100\% = 80\%$.
Mistake in B: Calculating the percentage of the "lost" voltage, i.e., $\frac{3.0 - 2.4}{3.0} \times 100\% = 20\%$.

**9. Which of the following cells provides power for modern mobile phones and laptops?**
- A. Leclanché cell
- B. Lead-acid accumulator
- C. Lithium-ion cell
- D. Mercury-cadmium cell

**Answer: C.** Lithium-ion (Li-ion) cells are lightweight, have high energy density, and are fully rechargeable, making them the standard choice for portable modern electronics.

**10. Two cells, each with an e.m.f of $1.5\ \text{V}$ and internal resistance of $0.5\ \Omega$, are connected in series. They power a circuit containing two $2.0\ \Omega$ resistors connected in parallel. What is the total current flowing from the battery?**
- A. $0.6\ \text{A}$
- B. $0.75\ \text{A}$
- C. $1.5\ \text{A}$
- D. $2.4\ \text{A}$

**Answer: C.**
1. Battery is in series: $E_{\text{total}} = 1.5 + 1.5 = 3.0\ \text{V}$.
2. Total internal resistance: $r_{\text{total}} = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0\ \Omega$.
3. External resistors are in parallel: Total external $R = \frac{2.0 \times 2.0}{2.0 + 2.0} = \frac{4.0}{4.0} = 1.0\ \Omega$.
4. Current $I = \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{R + r_{\text{total}}} = \frac{3.0\ \text{V}}{1.0\ \Omega + 1.0\ \Omega} = \frac{3.0}{2.0} = 1.5\ \text{A}$.
Mistake in A: Adding the two external resistors in series ($2.0 + 2.0 = 4.0\ \Omega$), yielding $I = \frac{3.0}{4.0 + 1.0} = 0.6\ \text{A}$.
