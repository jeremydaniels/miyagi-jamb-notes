## 1. Introduction to Alternating Current (A.C.)

### Identify A.C. Current And D.C. Voltage

Electricity comes in two main forms: **Direct Current (d.c.)** and **Alternating Current (a.c.)**. 

**Direct Current (d.c.)** is an electric current that flows in **only one direction** and usually has a constant magnitude (size). The voltage from a dry cell (like the batteries used in wall clocks or flashlights) or a car battery is a d.c. voltage. 

**Alternating Current (a.c.)** is an electric current that **periodically reverses its direction** and continuously changes its magnitude with time. The electricity supplied to our homes by the national grid (e.g., NEPA/PHCN) or generated by standard fuel generators ("I pass my neighbour") is a.c.

Why do we use a.c. for the national power supply? The main reason is that a.c. voltages can easily be **stepped up** to a very high voltage for transmission over long distances (from power stations like Kainji Dam) using transformers. High voltage means low current, which heavily reduces power lost as heat in the transmission cables. It is then **stepped down** to a safer $240\ \text{V}$ for homes. D.c. cannot be stepped up or down this way because transformers only work with alternating current.

### Explanation Of A.C. Current And Voltage

A.c. voltage and current are generated by rotating a coil in a magnetic field (see electromagnetic induction). Because of this continuous rotation, the values of the voltage and current form a smooth **sine wave** when plotted against time. 

The equations for alternating current and voltage are:
$$I = I_0 \sin(\omega t)$$
$$V = V_0 \sin(\omega t)$$

where:
- $I$ is the **instantaneous current** (the current at any exact time $t$), in Amperes ($\text{A}$).
- $V$ is the **instantaneous voltage**, in Volts ($\text{V}$).
- $I_0$ is the **peak current** (maximum current), in Amperes ($\text{A}$).
- $V_0$ is the **peak voltage** (maximum voltage), in Volts ($\text{V}$).
- $\omega$ (omega) is the **angular frequency**, in radians per second ($\text{rad s}^{-1}$).
- $t$ is the time, in seconds ($\text{s}$).

The angular frequency $\omega$ is related to the ordinary frequency $f$ by the formula:
$$\omega = 2\pi f$$
where $f$ is the frequency in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$). In Nigeria, the standard frequency of the national a.c. supply is $50\ \text{Hz}$, which means the current completely reverses its direction and returns to its starting point 50 times every single second.

## 2. Measuring A.C. Values

### Peak And R.M.S. Values

Because an alternating current is constantly changing—from zero, up to a maximum peak, back to zero, and then down to a negative peak—we cannot just state one number to describe the actual current flowing at all times. We use two main ways to measure the size of an a.c. signal: the **peak value** and the **root mean square (r.m.s.) value**.

1. **Peak Value ($I_0$ or $V_0$):** This is the maximum absolute value reached by the current or voltage during one complete cycle. It is the height of the crest of the sine wave.
2. **Root Mean Square (r.m.s.) Value ($I_{rms}$ or $V_{rms}$):** This is defined as the steady direct current (d.c.) that would produce the **same heating effect per second** in a given resistor as the alternating current. 

Because the current is not at its peak all the time, the r.m.s. value is always lower than the peak value. Normal a.c. voltmeters and ammeters are designed to read r.m.s. values, not peak values. Whenever a value for a.c. is stated without specifying whether it is peak or r.m.s. (such as the Nigerian mains supply being $240\ \text{V}$), it is **always the r.m.s. value**.

### Differentiate Between The Peak And R.M.S. Values Of A.C.

For a sinusoidal wave, the r.m.s. value is obtained by dividing the peak value by the square root of 2. They are linked by the following formulas:
$$I_{rms} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$$
$$V_{rms} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$$

| Feature | Peak Value ($V_0$, $I_0$) | Root Mean Square (r.m.s.) Value ($V_{rms}$, $I_{rms}$) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | The absolute maximum magnitude reached during one complete cycle. | The steady d.c. value that produces the same heating effect per second. |
| **Magnitude** | Always the largest value in the a.c. cycle. | Always smaller than the peak value (for sine waves, $0.707 \times \text{Peak}$). |
| **Formula** | $V_0 = V_{rms} \times \sqrt{2}$ | $V_{rms} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$ |
| **Meter Readings** | Oscilloscopes can display it, but standard meters do not directly read it. | Standard a.c. ammeters and voltmeters read this value by default. |

**Worked Examples on Peak and R.M.S. Values**

**1. Calculate the peak voltage of the Nigerian mains supply, given that the r.m.s. voltage is $240\ \text{V}$.**
1. Write the formula connecting peak and r.m.s. voltage: $V_{rms} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$.
2. Rearrange the formula to make $V_0$ the subject: $V_0 = V_{rms} \times \sqrt{2}$.
3. Substitute the values: $V_0 = 240 \times \sqrt{2}$.
4. Calculate the final value (using $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$): $V_0 = 240 \times 1.414 = 339.4\ \text{V}$.
*(Note: The peak voltage is nearly $340\ \text{V}$, which is why an a.c. shock of $240\ \text{V}$ is more dangerous than a d.c. shock of $240\ \text{V}$.)*

**2. An alternating current is represented by the equation $I = 14.14 \sin(120\pi t)$. Find the r.m.s. current.**
1. Compare the given equation with the standard sine wave equation: $I = I_0 \sin(\omega t)$.
2. Identify the peak current ($I_0$) directly from the equation: $I_0 = 14.14\ \text{A}$.
3. Write the formula for r.m.s. current: $I_{rms} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$.
4. Substitute and solve (using $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$): $I_{rms} = \frac{14.14}{1.414} = 10\ \text{A}$.

**3. A Nigerian household generator is rated to output a peak current of $28.28\ \text{A}$. What is the r.m.s. current available to power the appliances?**
1. Identify the given peak current: $I_0 = 28.28\ \text{A}$.
2. Write the formula for r.m.s. current: $I_{rms} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$.
3. Substitute the values: $I_{rms} = \frac{28.28}{1.414}$.
4. Calculate the final value: $I_{rms} = 20\ \text{A}$.

**Common Mistake:** Students sometimes multiply by $\sqrt{2}$ when they should divide, or vice versa. **Rule of thumb:** The peak value is always the *larger* number. If you are finding the peak value, multiply by $\sqrt{2}$. If you are finding the r.m.s. value, divide by $\sqrt{2}$.

## 3. Pure Components in an A.C. Circuit

In d.c. circuits, the only property that opposes current is resistance. In a.c. circuits, components like capacitors and inductors also oppose current, but they do it differently. Let's examine how these three pure components behave when connected individually to an a.c. source.

### A.C. Source Connected To A Resistor

When an alternating voltage is applied across a pure resistor ($R$):
- The resistor opposes the flow of electrons by converting electrical energy into heat (just as it does with d.c.). The opposition to current is called **Resistance ($R$)**, measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
- Ohm's law applies directly: $V_{rms} = I_{rms} R$ or $V_0 = I_0 R$.
- The current and the voltage reach their peaks and cross zero at exactly the same time. We say that the current and voltage are **in phase**. 

[IMAGE: A circuit diagram of an a.c. source (a circle with a sine wave inside) connected in series with a resistor (a zig-zag line). Next to it, a graph showing two sine waves (Voltage and Current) that rise and fall exactly together in perfect synchronization. Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

### A.C. Source Connected To A Capacitor-Capacitive Reactance

A capacitor is essentially two metal plates separated by an insulator. A capacitor **blocks d.c.** completely once it is fully charged because electrons cannot pass through the insulator. However, when connected to an a.c. source, the constantly reversing voltage causes the capacitor to charge, discharge, charge in reverse, and discharge again. Thus, alternating current effectively flows back and forth in the wires, appearing to pass "through" the capacitor.

- A capacitor opposes the flow of a.c. This opposition is called **Capacitive Reactance ($X_C$)**, and it is measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
- The formula for capacitive reactance is:
  $$X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}$$
  where $C$ is the capacitance in Farads ($\text{F}$), and $f$ is the frequency in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$).
- Notice that if the frequency $f$ increases, the reactance $X_C$ decreases. High-frequency a.c. passes through a capacitor very easily.

### A.C. Source Connected To An Inductor-Inductive Reactance

An inductor is simply a coil of wire. When a.c. flows through it, the constantly changing current creates a changing magnetic field. This changing magnetic field induces a "back electromotive force (e.m.f.)" that opposes the change in current (due to Lenz's Law). 

- This opposition to the changing current is called **Inductive Reactance ($X_L$)**, measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
- The formula for inductive reactance is:
  $$X_L = \omega L = 2\pi f L$$
  where $L$ is the inductance in Henrys ($\text{H}$).
- Notice that if the frequency $f$ increases, the reactance $X_L$ increases. Inductors strongly oppose high-frequency a.c.

### Determine The Phase Difference Between Current And Voltage

When we plot the voltage and current of reactive components on a graph, they do not reach their maximum (peak) values at the same time. The delay between the current and the voltage reaching their peaks is called the **phase difference** or **phase angle**, measured in degrees ($^\circ$) or radians ($\text{rad}$).

- **In a pure Resistor:** Current and voltage are **in phase** (phase difference is $0^\circ$).
- **In a pure Capacitor:** The capacitor must first receive current to build up charge and voltage. Therefore, the **current LEADS the voltage by $90^\circ$** ($\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians). Conversely, the voltage lags the current by $90^\circ$.
- **In a pure Inductor:** The back e.m.f. opposes the buildup of current. Therefore, the **voltage LEADS the current by $90^\circ$** ($\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians). Conversely, the current lags the voltage by $90^\circ$.

**Exam tip:** To remember phase relationships, use the word **CIVIL**:
- In a **C**apacitor, **I** (current) leads **V** (voltage).
- **V** (voltage) leads **I** (current) in an **L** (inductor).

### Analyse Vector Diagrams

To easily analyze and add a.c. voltages that are out of phase, we draw vector (or phasor) diagrams. We use arrows to represent the magnitude and phase of the voltages and currents. 

- For a **resistor**, the current arrow and voltage arrow point in the **exact same direction**.
- For a **capacitor**, the current arrow points to the right (reference), while the voltage arrow ($V_C$) points **downwards** (lagging by $90^\circ$).
- For an **inductor**, the current arrow points to the right (reference), while the voltage arrow ($V_L$) points **upwards** (leading by $90^\circ$).

[IMAGE: A three-panel vector (phasor) diagram. Panel 1 (Resistor): Two horizontal arrows pointing right, labelled V_R and I. Panel 2 (Capacitor): A horizontal arrow pointing right labelled I, and a vertical arrow pointing down labelled V_C with a 90° angle. Panel 3 (Inductor): A horizontal arrow pointing right labelled I, and a vertical arrow pointing up labelled V_L with a 90° angle. Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

## 4. Series R-L-C Circuits

### R-L-C Circuits

When a resistor ($R$), an inductor ($L$), and a capacitor ($C$) are connected together in series across an a.c. source, we have an R-L-C series circuit. This is the foundation of many tuning and filtering circuits in electronics.

### Interpret R-L-C Circuits

Because the components are in series, the **same current ($I$) flows through all three components** at the same time. This is a very important rule. We use this common current as our reference line (the horizontal line pointing right) in our vector diagrams.

The voltages across each component are:
- $V_R = I R$ (in phase with the current)
- $V_L = I X_L$ (leads the current by $90^\circ$, points upwards)
- $V_C = I X_C$ (lags the current by $90^\circ$, points downwards)

### Effective Voltage In An R-L-C Circuits

Because the voltages $V_L$ and $V_C$ are $180^\circ$ completely out of phase with each other (one points directly up, the other directly down), they partially cancel each other out. The net reactive voltage is $(V_L - V_C)$, assuming $V_L > V_C$. 

Because $V_R$ is $90^\circ$ out of phase with this net reactive voltage, we cannot just add the numbers algebraically (e.g., $10\ \text{V} + 20\ \text{V}$ is WRONG). We must add them as vectors using Pythagoras' theorem. 
The **effective (total) voltage** of the a.c. source is given by:
$$V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2}$$

### Resistance And Impedance

Just as voltages add as vectors, the oppositions to current also add as vectors. 
- **Resistance ($R$)** is the opposition from the resistor only.
- **Total Reactance ($X$)** is the net opposition from the inductor and capacitor, given by $X = X_L - X_C$.
- **Impedance ($Z$)** is the total overall opposition to current flow in an a.c. circuit containing resistance and reactance. It is measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).

The impedance is calculated using Pythagoras' theorem:
$$Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$$

Ohm's law for the entire circuit becomes:
$$V = I Z$$

### Calculate The Effective Voltage, Reactance And Impedance

**Worked Examples**

**1. In a series R-L-C circuit, the voltage across the resistor is $30\ \text{V}$, the voltage across the inductor is $90\ \text{V}$, and the voltage across the capacitor is $50\ \text{V}$. Calculate the effective voltage of the source.**
1. Identify the given voltages: $V_R = 30\ \text{V}$, $V_L = 90\ \text{V}$, $V_C = 50\ \text{V}$.
2. Write the formula for effective voltage: $V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2}$.
3. Substitute the values: $V = \sqrt{30^2 + (90 - 50)^2}$.
4. Simplify the bracket first: $V = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2}$.
5. Square the numbers and add: $V = \sqrt{900 + 1600} = \sqrt{2500}$.
6. Find the square root: $V = 50\ \text{V}$.

**2. A circuit consists of a $12\ \Omega$ resistor, a coil of inductive reactance $25\ \Omega$, and a capacitor of capacitive reactance $9\ \Omega$ connected in series. Find the impedance of the circuit.**
1. Identify the given values: $R = 12\ \Omega$, $X_L = 25\ \Omega$, $X_C = 9\ \Omega$.
2. Write the formula for impedance: $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$.
3. Substitute the values: $Z = \sqrt{12^2 + (25 - 9)^2}$.
4. Simplify the bracket: $Z = \sqrt{12^2 + 16^2}$.
5. Square and add: $Z = \sqrt{144 + 256} = \sqrt{400}$.
6. Find the square root: $Z = 20\ \Omega$.

**3. An a.c. circuit consists of a $40\ \Omega$ resistor, a $0.1\ \text{H}$ inductor, and a $250\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor in series, connected to a $\frac{50}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$ supply. Calculate the total impedance.**
1. Identify values: $R = 40\ \Omega$, $L = 0.1\ \text{H}$, $C = 250 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F}$, $f = \frac{50}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$.
2. Calculate inductive reactance: $X_L = 2\pi f L = 2\pi \times \frac{50}{\pi} \times 0.1 = 100 \times 0.1 = 10\ \Omega$.
3. Calculate capacitive reactance: $X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times \frac{50}{\pi} \times 250 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{1}{100 \times 250 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{1}{0.02} = 50\ \Omega$.
4. Use the impedance formula: $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2} = \sqrt{40^2 + (10 - 50)^2}$.
5. Simplify: $Z = \sqrt{1600 + (-40)^2} = \sqrt{1600 + 1600} = \sqrt{3200} = 40\sqrt{2} \approx 56.57\ \Omega$.

**Common Mistake:** When given the voltages $V_R = 30\ \text{V}$, $V_L = 90\ \text{V}$, $V_C = 50\ \text{V}$, students often just add them up: $30 + 90 + 50 = 170\ \text{V}$. This is **wrong** because they do not reach their peaks at the same time. You must use the vector formula.

## 5. Power and Vectors in A.C. Circuits

### Vector Diagram, Phase Angle And Power Factor

By mapping $V_R$, $V_L$, and $V_C$ onto a single graph, we can find the **phase angle ($\theta$)** of the entire circuit. The source voltage $V$ and the source current $I$ are generally out of phase by this angle $\theta$. 

From the combined vector diagram (using trigonometry: $\tan \theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}$), the phase angle is:
$$\tan \theta = \frac{V_L - V_C}{V_R} = \frac{X_L - X_C}{R}$$

[IMAGE: A combined vector (phasor) diagram for an R-L-C series circuit. A horizontal axis pointing right represents the Current (I) and V_R. A vertical axis pointing up represents V_L, and a vertical axis pointing down represents V_C. An arrow representing the net reactive voltage (V_L - V_C) points upwards (assuming V_L > V_C). A diagonal resultant vector drawn from the origin represents the Effective Voltage (V). The angle between the horizontal axis (V_R) and the resultant V is labelled as the phase angle theta (θ). Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

### Determine The Instantaneous Power, Average Power And The Power Factor In A. C. Circuits

In a d.c. circuit, power is simply $P = I V$. In an a.c. circuit, things are slightly more complex because the current and voltage are constantly changing.

1. **Instantaneous Power ($p$):** This is the power at any exact split-second in time. It is found by multiplying the instantaneous current by the instantaneous voltage: $p = i v$. Because $i$ and $v$ fluctuate, the instantaneous power fluctuates as well.
2. **Average Power ($P$):** Over a complete cycle, the actual power consumed by the circuit is the average power. 
   
A very important fact in a.c. circuits is that **pure capacitors and pure inductors do not consume any average power**. They temporarily store energy and return it to the circuit. **Only resistors dissipate true power** (as heat). 

The formula for the average power dissipated in an a.c. circuit is:
$$P = I_{rms} V_{rms} \cos \theta$$
Alternatively, since all power is dissipated in the resistor, we can use:
$$P = I_{rms}^2 R$$

**The Power Factor**
In the power formula, the term **$\cos \theta$** is called the **power factor**. From our vector diagram, $\cos \theta = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}$. Therefore:
$$\text{Power Factor} = \cos \theta = \frac{R}{Z} = \frac{V_R}{V}$$

- A power factor of $1$ means the circuit is purely resistive (all energy is used).
- A power factor of $0$ means the circuit is purely reactive (no energy is consumed).

**Worked Examples on Power**

**1. An a.c. circuit has an impedance of $50\ \Omega$ and a resistance of $40\ \Omega$. Calculate the power factor.**
1. Write the formula for power factor: $\text{Power Factor} = \frac{R}{Z}$.
2. Substitute the given values: $\text{Power Factor} = \frac{40}{50}$.
3. Simplify the fraction: $\text{Power Factor} = 0.8$. (Note: power factor has no unit).

**2. An a.c. source has a peak voltage of $200\ \text{V}$ and supplies a peak current of $4\ \text{A}$ to a purely resistive circuit. Calculate the average power dissipated.**
1. Recognize that for a purely resistive circuit, current and voltage are in phase, so $\theta = 0^\circ$ and $\cos \theta = 1$.
2. The formula for average power uses r.m.s. values: $P = I_{rms} V_{rms} \cos \theta$.
3. Convert the given peak values to r.m.s. values: $V_{rms} = \frac{200}{\sqrt{2}}$, $I_{rms} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}$.
4. Substitute: $P = \left(\frac{200}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \times \left(\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \times 1$.
5. Multiply (note that $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2} = 2$): $P = \frac{800}{2} = 400\ \text{W}$.

**3. An R-L-C circuit draws an r.m.s. current of $5\ \text{A}$ from a $240\ \text{V}$ a.c. source. If the power factor of the circuit is $0.6$, calculate the average power dissipated.**
1. Write the average power formula: $P = I_{rms} V_{rms} \cos \theta$.
2. Note the given values: $I_{rms} = 5\ \text{A}$, $V_{rms} = 240\ \text{V}$, and the power factor ($\cos \theta$) is $0.6$.
3. Substitute the values: $P = 5 \times 240 \times 0.6$.
4. Calculate: $P = 1200 \times 0.6 = 720\ \text{W}$.

## 6. Resonance in R-L-C Circuits

### Resonance And Resonance Frequency

In an R-L-C series circuit, the inductive reactance $X_L$ increases as frequency goes up, while the capacitive reactance $X_C$ decreases as frequency goes up. 

**Resonance** occurs at the specific frequency where the **inductive reactance exactly equals the capacitive reactance**. When this happens, the effects of the inductor and the capacitor completely cancel each other out ($X_L - X_C = 0$).

We can find this specific frequency ($f_0$) by equating the formulas for $X_L$ and $X_C$:
$$2\pi f_0 L = \frac{1}{2\pi f_0 C}$$
Cross-multiply to get $f_0$ on one side:
$$(2\pi f_0)^2 LC = 1$$
$$2\pi f_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$
$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}$$

This is the **resonant frequency** formula. An everyday application of resonance is tuning a radio. By turning the tuning knob, you are changing the capacitance $C$ in the radio's circuit until its resonant frequency perfectly matches the frequency of the radio waves being broadcast by a station (e.g., $96.9\ \text{MHz}$). At that frequency, the current is maximum, and the radio picks up that station clearly.

[IMAGE: A graph showing the Resonance Curve. The vertical y-axis is labelled "Current (I)" and the horizontal x-axis is labelled "Frequency (f)". A bell-shaped curve starts low, rises to a high peak, and falls low again. A dotted vertical line drops from the very peak of the curve down to the x-axis, marking the point as "Resonant Frequency (f_0)". Clean flat textbook style, white background.]

### Recognize The Condition By Which The Circuit Is At Resonance

At resonance ($X_L = X_C$), several very important conditions occur in the circuit:
1. **Minimum Impedance:** Since $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (0)^2}$, the impedance drops to its lowest possible value, which is simply the resistance $R$ ($Z = R$).
2. **Maximum Current:** Because the impedance is at its absolute minimum, the current flowing through the circuit reaches its absolute maximum ($I = \frac{V}{R}$).
3. **Phase Angle is Zero:** The current and voltage are perfectly in phase ($\theta = 0^\circ$).
4. **Power Factor is $1$:** Since $\cos(0) = 1$, the true power dissipated is at its maximum.

### Determine The Resonant Frequency Of R-L-C Arrangement

**Worked Examples**

**1. A series circuit consists of an inductor of $0.2\ \text{H}$ and a capacitor of $5\ \mu\text{F}$. Calculate the resonant frequency of the circuit.**
1. Write the resonant frequency formula: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}$.
2. Convert capacitance to standard units: $C = 5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F}$.
3. Substitute $L$ and $C$: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{0.2 \times 5 \times 10^{-6}}}$.
4. Simplify the terms inside the square root ($0.2 \times 5 = 1$): $\sqrt{1 \times 10^{-6}} = 10^{-3}$.
5. Substitute back: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 10^{-3}}$.
6. Bring $10^{-3}$ to the top as $1000$: $f_0 = \frac{1000}{2\pi} = \frac{500}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$.

**2. An R-L-C circuit has an inductor of $4\ \text{mH}$ and a capacitor of $2.5\ \mu\text{F}$. What is its resonant frequency?**
1. Write the formula: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}$.
2. Convert to standard units: $L = 4 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{H}$, $C = 2.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F}$.
3. Multiply $L$ and $C$: $4 \times 10^{-3} \times 2.5 \times 10^{-6} = 10 \times 10^{-9} = 10^{-8}$.
4. Find the square root: $\sqrt{10^{-8}} = 10^{-4}$.
5. Calculate $f_0$: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 10^{-4}} = \frac{10000}{2\pi} = \frac{5000}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$.

**3. What capacitance is required to tune an R-L-C circuit to resonance at a frequency of $\frac{1000}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$ if the inductance is $0.5\ \text{H}$?**
1. Write the resonant frequency formula: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}$.
2. Rearrange to make $C$ the subject. Square both sides: $f_0^2 = \frac{1}{4\pi^2 L C} \implies C = \frac{1}{4\pi^2 f_0^2 L}$.
3. Substitute the values: $C = \frac{1}{4\pi^2 \left(\frac{1000}{\pi}\right)^2 \times 0.5}$.
4. Expand the squared term: $C = \frac{1}{4\pi^2 \times \frac{1000000}{\pi^2} \times 0.5}$.
5. The $\pi^2$ terms cancel out: $C = \frac{1}{4 \times 1000000 \times 0.5} = \frac{1}{2000000}$.
6. Calculate final value: $C = 0.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F} = 0.5\ \mu\text{F}$.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Alternating Current (a.c.)** | Electric current that periodically reverses its direction and continuously changes in magnitude. |
| **Peak Value ($V_0$ or $I_0$)** | The absolute maximum magnitude reached by the alternating voltage or current during one cycle. |
| **Root Mean Square (r.m.s.)** | The steady d.c. value that produces the same heating effect per second as the a.c. |
| **Reactance ($X$)** | The opposition to alternating current by a capacitor ($X_C$) or an inductor ($X_L$). Measured in Ohms ($\Omega$). |
| **Impedance ($Z$)** | The total overall opposition to alternating current in a circuit containing resistance and reactance. Measured in Ohms ($\Omega$). |
| **Phase angle ($\theta$)** | The angle by which the voltage leads or lags the current in an a.c. circuit. |
| **Power factor** | The cosine of the phase angle ($\cos \theta$); the ratio of true resistance to total impedance ($R/Z$). |
| **Resonance** | The condition in an R-L-C series circuit where inductive reactance equals capacitive reactance ($X_L = X_C$), resulting in minimum impedance and maximum current. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **R.m.s. vs Peak:** The value stated for mains supply (e.g., $240\ \text{V}$) is always the r.m.s. value. The formula is $V_{rms} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$. Do not multiply when you should divide!
- **Vector addition:** Never add alternating voltages algebraically. $V_{Total} = V_R + V_L + V_C$ is **WRONG**. Use the vector formula $V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2}$.
- **Average Power uses r.m.s.:** Average power $P = I_{rms} V_{rms} \cos \theta$. If you are given peak values, you must convert them to r.m.s. first, or your calculated power will be exactly double the correct answer.
- **Power in reactive components:** A pure capacitor and a pure inductor dissipate **zero** average power. All power is dissipated as heat in the resistor.
- **Units check:** $\omega L$, $\frac{1}{\omega C}$, $R$, and $Z$ all share the same unit: Ohms ($\Omega$). However, $LC$ has the unit of seconds squared ($\text{s}^2$), and $\sqrt{LC}$ has the unit of seconds ($\text{s}$).
- **Resonance facts to memorize:** At resonance, $X_L = X_C$, Impedance is minimum ($Z = R$), Current is maximum, and Power Factor is $1$.

## Quick summary

1. A.c. current periodically reverses direction, while d.c. flows continuously in one direction.
2. The r.m.s. value is the steady d.c. equivalent for heating effect; $I_{rms} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$.
3. In a pure resistor, current and voltage are in phase ($\theta = 0^\circ$).
4. In a pure capacitor, current leads voltage by $90^\circ$. Its reactance is $X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}$.
5. In a pure inductor, voltage leads current by $90^\circ$. Its reactance is $X_L = 2\pi f L$.
6. For an R-L-C series circuit, impedance is $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$ and effective voltage is $V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2}$.
7. Average power is calculated as $P = I_{rms} V_{rms} \cos \theta$. The power factor is $\cos \theta = \frac{R}{Z}$.
8. Resonance occurs when $X_L = X_C$, giving maximum current. The resonant frequency is $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}$.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following correctly describes alternating current (a.c.) compared to direct current (d.c.)?**

- A. A.c. flows continuously in one direction while d.c. periodically reverses direction.
- B. The magnitude of d.c. varies sinusoidally with time, but a.c. is constant.
- C. A.c. periodically reverses its direction of flow, whereas d.c. flows in only one direction.
- D. Both a.c. and d.c. voltages can be stepped up or down by a transformer.

**Answer: C.** 1. Direct current (d.c.) is defined as flowing continuously in one direction. 2. Alternating current (a.c.) is defined as periodically reversing its direction and changing its magnitude. 3. Transformers only work with a.c. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from completely swapping the definitions of a.c. and d.c.

**2. An alternating current is given by the equation $I = 10 \sin(100\pi t)$. What is the root mean square (r.m.s.) current?**

- A. $5.00\ \text{A}$
- B. $7.07\ \text{A}$
- C. $10.00\ \text{A}$
- D. $14.14\ \text{A}$

**Answer: B.** 1. From the equation $I = I_0 \sin(\omega t)$, identify the peak current $I_0 = 10\ \text{A}$. 2. Use the r.m.s. formula: $I_{rms} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$. 3. Substitute values: $I_{rms} = \frac{10}{1.414} = 7.07\ \text{A}$. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of dividing the peak current by 2 instead of dividing it by $\sqrt{2}$.

**3. In an a.c. circuit containing only a pure capacitor, what is the phase relationship between the voltage and the current?**

- A. The current and voltage are in phase.
- B. The current leads the voltage by $90^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{2}\ \text{rad}$).
- C. The voltage leads the current by $90^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{2}\ \text{rad}$).
- D. The current lags the voltage by $180^\circ$ ($\pi\ \text{rad}$).

**Answer: B.** 1. A capacitor must receive current to build up charge. 2. Because of this, the current peaks before the voltage peaks. 3. Therefore, current leads voltage by $90^\circ$ (remember CIVIL). The most tempting wrong option is C, which comes from the mistake of confusing the behaviour of a capacitor with that of an inductor (where voltage leads).

**4. A resistor, an inductor, and a capacitor are connected in series across an a.c. source. If the voltages across the resistor, inductor, and capacitor are $30\ \text{V}$, $90\ \text{V}$, and $50\ \text{V}$ respectively, calculate the effective voltage of the source.**

- A. $170\ \text{V}$
- B. $70\ \text{V}$
- C. $50\ \text{V}$
- D. $40\ \text{V}$

**Answer: C.** 1. Write the vector addition formula: $V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2}$. 2. Substitute the values: $V = \sqrt{30^2 + (90 - 50)^2}$. 3. Simplify: $V = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = \sqrt{900 + 1600} = \sqrt{2500} = 50\ \text{V}$. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of incorrectly adding the voltages algebraically ($30 + 90 + 50 = 170$) instead of vectorially.

**5. An R-L-C series circuit consists of a $12\ \Omega$ resistor, a coil with an inductive reactance of $25\ \Omega$, and a capacitor with a capacitive reactance of $9\ \Omega$. Calculate the impedance of the circuit.**

- A. $46\ \Omega$
- B. $28\ \Omega$
- C. $20\ \Omega$
- D. $16\ \Omega$

**Answer: C.** 1. Write the impedance formula: $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$. 2. Substitute the values: $Z = \sqrt{12^2 + (25 - 9)^2}$. 3. Simplify: $Z = \sqrt{12^2 + 16^2} = \sqrt{144 + 256} = \sqrt{400} = 20\ \Omega$. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of adding all the reactances algebraically ($12 + 25 + 9 = 46$) instead of vectorially.

**6. A series circuit consists of an inductor of $0.2\ \text{H}$ and a capacitor of $5\ \mu\text{F}$. Calculate the resonant frequency of the circuit.**

- A. $\frac{500}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$
- B. $\frac{1000}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$
- C. $500\pi\ \text{Hz}$
- D. $\frac{250}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$

**Answer: A.** 1. Write the formula: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}$. 2. Substitute values: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{0.2 \times 5 \times 10^{-6}}}$. 3. Simplify the square root: $\sqrt{1 \times 10^{-6}} = 10^{-3}$. 4. Solve: $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{1000}{2\pi} = \frac{500}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$. The most tempting wrong option is B, which comes from the mistake of forgetting the $2$ in the $2\pi$ part of the formula denominator.

**7. Which of the following is NOT true for an R-L-C series circuit at resonance?**

- A. The inductive reactance equals the capacitive reactance.
- B. The impedance of the circuit is maximum.
- C. The current in the circuit is maximum.
- D. The phase angle between voltage and current is zero.

**Answer: B.** 1. At resonance, the opposing reactances cancel each other out ($X_L = X_C$). 2. Because they cancel, the total impedance formula $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$ reduces to $Z = R$. 3. This means the impedance is at its **minimum**, not maximum, allowing the maximum current to flow. The most tempting wrong option is B, which comes from the mistaken assumption that "resonance" implies the highest level of opposition, when it actually means the exact opposite.

**8. In an a.c. circuit, the impedance is $50\ \Omega$ and the resistance is $40\ \Omega$. What is the power factor of the circuit?**

- A. $0.6$
- B. $0.8$
- C. $1.25$
- D. $0.75$

**Answer: B.** 1. Write the formula for power factor: $\text{Power factor} = \frac{R}{Z}$. 2. Substitute the given values: $\text{Power factor} = \frac{40}{50}$. 3. Calculate: $0.8$. The most tempting wrong option is C, which comes from the mistake of inverting the formula and dividing impedance by resistance ($50 / 40 = 1.25$).

**9. An a.c. source has a peak voltage of $200\ \text{V}$ and supplies a peak current of $4\ \text{A}$ to a purely resistive circuit. Calculate the average power dissipated.**

- A. $800\ \text{W}$
- B. $400\ \text{W}$
- C. $200\ \text{W}$
- D. $565\ \text{W}$

**Answer: B.** 1. Average power must be calculated using r.m.s. values: $P = I_{rms} V_{rms} \cos \theta$. 2. For a purely resistive circuit, $\cos \theta = 1$. 3. Convert peak to r.m.s.: $V_{rms} = \frac{200}{\sqrt{2}}$, $I_{rms} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}$. 4. Substitute: $P = \left(\frac{200}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \times \left(\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \frac{800}{2} = 400\ \text{W}$. The most tempting wrong option is A, which comes from the mistake of simply multiplying the peak values ($200 \times 4 = 800\ \text{W}$) without converting them to r.m.s. first.

**10. An inductor of inductance $0.5\ \text{H}$ is connected to a $240\ \text{V}$, $\frac{50}{\pi}\ \text{Hz}$ a.c. source. What is the r.m.s. current flowing through the inductor?**

- A. $4.8\ \text{A}$
- B. $2.4\ \text{A}$
- C. $9.6\ \text{A}$
- D. $48\ \text{A}$

**Answer: A.** 1. Calculate the inductive reactance using $X_L = 2\pi f L$. 2. Substitute values: $X_L = 2\pi \times \frac{50}{\pi} \times 0.5 = 100 \times 0.5 = 50\ \Omega$. 3. Use Ohm's law to find current: $I_{rms} = \frac{V_{rms}}{X_L}$. 4. Calculate: $I_{rms} = \frac{240}{50} = 4.8\ \text{A}$. The most tempting wrong option is C, which comes from the mistake of forgetting the $2$ in the formula $2\pi f L$, leading to a reactance of $25\ \Omega$ and an incorrect current of $9.6\ \text{A}$.
