## Conduction of Electricity Through Liquids

In solid conductors like copper wires or aluminium cables (such as those used by PHCN/NEPA), electricity is carried by free, mobile electrons. This is known as **metallic conduction**. During this process, the metal gets hot, but its chemical structure does not change.

However, liquids and gases behave very differently. When certain liquids conduct electricity, they do so using mobile, charged particles called **ions**, and the liquid itself is chemically decomposed (broken down) in the process. This is known as **electrolytic conduction**.

### Distinguishing between Electrolytes and Non-electrolytes

**Objective i: distinguish between electrolytes and non-electrolytes.**

To understand how liquids conduct, we must classify them into two main categories: electrolytes and non-electrolytes.

An **electrolyte** is a liquid or solution that conducts electricity and is chemically decomposed in the process. Electrolytes conduct electricity because they contain free-moving **ions** (positively and negatively charged particles).

A **non-electrolyte** is a liquid or solution that does not conduct electricity because it does not contain free mobile ions. Its particles exist only as neutral molecules.

| Feature | Strong Electrolytes | Weak Electrolytes | Non-electrolytes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | Completely split (dissociate) into free mobile ions when dissolved or melted. | Only partially split into ions. Most of the substance remains as neutral molecules. | Do not split into ions at all; exist entirely as neutral molecules. |
| **Conductivity** | Excellent conductors of electricity. | Poor conductors of electricity. | Do not conduct electricity at all (insulators). |
| **Examples in Physics** | - Dilute acids (e.g., $H_2SO_4$, $HCl$, $HNO_3$)<br>- Salt solutions (e.g., $NaCl$ / brine, $CuSO_4$)<br>- Alkalis (e.g., $NaOH$, $KOH$)<br>- Molten salts | - Ethanoic acid (vinegar)<br>- Ordinary tap water or well water<br>- Aqueous ammonia | - Kerosene, petrol, engine oil<br>- Pure (distilled) water<br>- Sugar solution<br>- Ethanol (alcohol)<br>- Benzene |

**Nigerian Context:** The battery acid used in car batteries and solar inverter batteries across Nigeria is dilute tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid ($H_2SO_4$). It is a very strong electrolyte. On the other hand, the kerosene used in stoves is a non-electrolyte. If you attempt to pass current through kerosene, nothing will happen.

### Analysing the Processes of Electrolysis

**Objective ii: analyse the processes of electrolysis.**

**Electrolysis** is defined as the chemical decomposition of a liquid or solution (the electrolyte) by the passage of a direct electric current (D.C.) through it.

#### The Electrolytic Cell (Voltameter)

The apparatus used to carry out electrolysis is called an **electrolytic cell** or a **voltameter**. It consists of a container holding the electrolyte and two solid conductors called **electrodes**, which are partially dipped into the liquid.

*   **Electrodes:** These are the solid plates or rods (usually made of metal or graphite/carbon) that allow current to enter and leave the electrolyte.
*   **Anode:** The **positive** electrode, connected to the positive terminal of the D.C. battery. Conventional current enters the electrolyte through the anode.
*   **Cathode:** The **negative** electrode, connected to the negative terminal of the battery. Conventional current leaves the electrolyte through the cathode.
    *(Exam tip: Use the memory aid **PANIC** — **P**ositive is **A**node, **N**egative **I**s **C**athode).*

#### How Electrolysis Works (The Arrhenius Theory)

The Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius proposed the theory of electrolytic dissociation, which explains the steps of electrolysis:

1.  **Ionisation (Dissociation):** When an electrolyte is melted or dissolved in water, it splits into independent, free-moving ions. Positively charged ions are called **cations**, and negatively charged ions are called **anions**.
2.  **Migration:** When the battery is switched on, a potential difference is established. Opposite charges attract. The positive **cations** migrate towards the negative **cathode**. The negative **anions** migrate towards the positive **anode**.
3.  **Discharge:** When the ions reach the electrodes, they transfer electrons to become neutral atoms or molecules (they are "discharged").
    *   **At the cathode:** Cations **gain** electrons (this is a reduction reaction). For example, copper ions become solid copper metal: $Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu$.
    *   **At the anode:** Anions **lose** electrons (this is an oxidation reaction) to the circuit, or the anode itself dissolves into the solution.

#### Preferential Discharge of Ions

Often, an electrolyte contains more than one type of cation or anion (especially in aqueous solutions, where water provides $H^+$ and $OH^-$ ions). When multiple ions migrate to the same electrode, only one type is selected for discharge. This is governed by three factors:

1.  **The Electrochemical Series:** Ions lower down in the series are discharged preferentially because they require less energy to lose/gain electrons.
    *   **Cations (from hardest to easiest to discharge):** $K^+ > Na^+ > Ca^{2+} > Mg^{2+} > Al^{3+} > Zn^{2+} > Fe^{2+} > Sn^{2+} > Pb^{2+} > H^+ > Cu^{2+} > Ag^+ > Au^+$
    *   **Anions (from hardest to easiest to discharge):** $F^- > SO_4^{2-} > NO_3^- > Cl^- > Br^- > I^- > OH^-$
2.  **Concentration of Ions:** A very high concentration of an ion can override the electrochemical series. For example, in concentrated sodium chloride (brine), $Cl^-$ is discharged at the anode instead of $OH^-$, even though $OH^-$ is lower in the series.
3.  **Nature of the Electrode:** If the anode is active (made of the same metal as the electrolyte's positive ion), it will dissolve into the solution rather than allowing anions to discharge. Inert electrodes (like carbon/graphite or platinum) do not participate in the reaction.

#### Classic Examples of Electrolysis

**1. Electrolysis of Acidified Water (Dilute $H_2SO_4$) using Platinum Electrodes:**
*   **Ions present:** $H^+$ (from acid and water), $OH^-$ (from water), $SO_4^{2-}$ (from acid).
*   **Cathode:** $H^+$ ions migrate here, gain electrons, and bubble off as **hydrogen gas ($H_2$)**.
*   **Anode:** Both $OH^-$ and $SO_4^{2-}$ migrate here. $OH^-$ is lower in the series, so it is discharged preferentially, bubbling off as **oxygen gas ($O_2$)**.
*   **Observation:** The volume of hydrogen gas produced is exactly twice the volume of oxygen gas (matching the formula $H_2O$).

**2. Electrolysis of Copper (II) tetraoxosulphate (VI) using INERT (Carbon/Platinum) Electrodes:**
*   **Ions present:** $Cu^{2+}$, $H^+$, $SO_4^{2-}$, $OH^-$.
*   **Cathode:** $Cu^{2+}$ is lower than $H^+$ in the series. It is discharged. **Solid pink/brown copper is deposited** on the cathode.
*   **Anode:** $OH^-$ is lower than $SO_4^{2-}$. It is discharged, producing **oxygen gas**.
*   **Observation:** As copper ions are removed from the solution, the blue colour of the electrolyte gradually fades, and the solution becomes more acidic.

**3. Electrolysis of Copper (II) tetraoxosulphate (VI) using ACTIVE Copper Electrodes:**
*   **Cathode:** $Cu^{2+}$ is discharged. **Copper is deposited**.
*   **Anode:** Because the anode is made of copper (an active electrode), neither $SO_4^{2-}$ nor $OH^-$ is discharged. Instead, the **copper anode dissolves** into the solution to form new $Cu^{2+}$ ions.
*   **Observation:** The anode shrinks in size, while the cathode grows heavier. The blue colour of the solution remains unchanged because the rate at which copper leaves the solution (at the cathode) equals the rate at which it enters (from the anode).

### Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis and Calculations

**Objective iii: apply Faraday’s laws of electrolysis to solve problems.**

In 1833, the English scientist Michael Faraday performed extensive experiments and formulated two quantitative laws describing the mass of substances liberated during electrolysis.

#### Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis
> **The mass ($m$) of an element deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electric charge ($Q$) passed through the electrolyte.**

Mathematically:
$$m \propto Q$$
Since electric charge $Q = It$ (where $I$ is steady current and $t$ is time), we can substitute $Q$ to get:
$$m \propto It$$
$$m = zIt$$
$$m = zQ$$

**Where:**
*   $m$ = mass of substance deposited (in grams, $\text{g}$).
*   $Q$ = total charge (in Coulombs, $\text{C}$).
*   $I$ = steady current (in Amperes, $\text{A}$).
*   $t$ = time the current flows (in seconds, $\text{s}$). *Note: You must always convert minutes or hours to seconds!*
*   $z$ = **Electrochemical equivalent (e.c.e.)** of the substance. It is defined as the mass of the substance deposited by exactly 1 Coulomb of electric charge. Its unit is $\text{g C}^{-1}$ or $\text{kg C}^{-1}$.

**Worked Example 1: Applying the First Law**
**Question:** Calculate the mass of silver deposited at the cathode when a steady current of $2.0\text{ A}$ is passed through a silver trioxonitrate (V) solution for $10\text{ minutes}$. (Electrochemical equivalent of silver = $1.118 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g C}^{-1}$).

**Working:**
1.  Identify the given values: $I = 2.0\text{ A}$, $t = 10\text{ minutes}$, $z = 1.118 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g C}^{-1}$.
2.  Convert time to seconds: $t = 10 \times 60 = 600\text{ s}$.
3.  Calculate the total charge: $Q = I \times t = 2.0 \times 600 = 1200\text{ C}$.
4.  Apply the first law formula: $m = z \times Q$.
5.  Substitute and calculate mass: $m = 1.118 \times 10^{-3} \times 1200 = 1.3416\text{ g}$.
**Answer:** The mass of silver deposited is **$1.34\text{ g}$**.

#### Faraday's Second Law of Electrolysis
> **When the same quantity of electricity is passed through different electrolytes, the relative masses of the elements deposited are directly proportional to their chemical equivalents.**

The **chemical equivalent** ($E$) of an element is its Molar Mass ($M$) divided by the charge on its ion/valency ($n$).
$$E = \frac{M}{n}$$

To deposit exactly one mole of a substance, you must supply $n$ moles of electrons. The total electric charge of one mole of electrons is a fundamental constant known as the **Faraday constant ($F$)**.
$F \approx 96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$.

By combining the two laws, we arrive at the most powerful formula for solving JAMB electrolysis calculations:
$$m = \frac{M \times I \times t}{n \times F}$$

**Where:**
*   $M$ = Molar mass of the element (in $\text{g mol}^{-1}$)
*   $n$ = charge on the ion/valency (e.g., $1$ for $Ag^+$, $2$ for $Cu^{2+}$, $3$ for $Al^{3+}$)
*   $F = 96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$

**Worked Example 2: Using Molar Mass and Faraday's Constant**
**Question:** How long will it take a current of $2.0\text{ A}$ to deposit $3.2\text{ g}$ of copper from a solution of copper (II) tetraoxosulphate (VI)? (Molar mass of Cu = $64\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, 1 Faraday = $96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$).

**Working:**
1.  Identify given values: $m = 3.2\text{ g}$, $I = 2.0\text{ A}$, $M = 64\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, $F = 96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$.
2.  Determine $n$: The electrolyte is Copper (II), meaning the ion is $Cu^{2+}$, so $n = 2$.
3.  Write the combined formula: $m = \frac{M \times I \times t}{n \times F}$.
4.  Substitute the values: $3.2 = \frac{64 \times 2.0 \times t}{2 \times 96500}$.
5.  Simplify the equation: $3.2 = \frac{128 \times t}{193000}$.
6.  Cross-multiply and solve for time: $t = \frac{3.2 \times 193000}{128} = 4825\text{ s}$.
**Answer:** It will take **$4825\text{ s}$**.

**Worked Example 3: Finding the Electrochemical Equivalent (Calibration)**
**Question:** In an experiment to calibrate an ammeter, a steady current of $1.5\text{ A}$ is passed through a copper voltameter for $30\text{ minutes}$. The actual mass of copper deposited is $0.891\text{ g}$. Calculate the electrochemical equivalent of copper.

**Working:**
1.  Identify given values: $I = 1.5\text{ A}$, $t = 30\text{ minutes}$, $m = 0.891\text{ g}$.
2.  Convert time to seconds: $t = 30 \times 60 = 1800\text{ s}$.
3.  Calculate charge: $Q = I \times t = 1.5 \times 1800 = 2700\text{ C}$.
4.  Write the first law formula ($m = zQ$) and rearrange to find $z$: $z = \frac{m}{Q}$.
5.  Calculate $z$: $z = \frac{0.891}{2700} = 0.00033\text{ g C}^{-1}$.
**Answer:** The electrochemical equivalent is **$3.3 \times 10^{-4}\text{ g C}^{-1}$**.

## Conduction of Electricity Through Gases

Under normal, everyday conditions (at atmospheric pressure), gases are excellent **insulators**—they do not conduct electricity. This is a very good thing; otherwise, electricity from high-tension power lines would flow through the air and electrocute people standing nearby!

However, gases *can* be made to conduct electricity under two very specific, extreme conditions:
1.  **Very low pressure** (a partial vacuum, achieved using a vacuum pump).
2.  **Very high potential difference** (high voltage, often between $10000\text{ V}$ and $50000\text{ V}$, supplied by an induction coil).

### Analysing Discharge Through Gases

**Objective iv: analyse discharge through gases.**

When a high voltage is applied across a gas at low pressure, the few stray electrons that naturally exist in the gas are accelerated to massive speeds. These fast electrons crash violently into neutral gas molecules. The impact knocks electrons out of the gas molecules, creating more free electrons and positive ions. These new electrons are accelerated to crash into more molecules. This chain reaction is called **ionisation by collision**, and it allows the gas to suddenly conduct a large current.

To study this phenomenon, physicists use a **discharge tube**: a long, sealed glass tube containing a gas, with metal electrodes at both ends. It is connected to a vacuum pump to suck out the gas and an induction coil for the high voltage.

As the vacuum pump gradually reduces the pressure inside the tube, a fascinating sequence of events occurs:

*   **At normal atmospheric pressure ($\sim 760\text{ mmHg}$):** Nothing happens. The gas acts as a perfect insulator.
*   **At $\sim 10\text{ mmHg}$:** The gas begins to conduct. A crackling sound is heard, and thin, wavy, irregular sparks jump across the tube (similar to miniature lightning).
*   **At $\sim 1\text{ mmHg}$:** The sparks broaden out into a continuous, glowing column of light called a **glow discharge** or positive column, which fills almost the entire tube. The colour depends purely on the gas inside (e.g., Neon glows bright red, Argon glows blue).
*   **At $\sim 0.1\text{ mmHg}$:** The glowing positive column breaks up into distinct bright and dark bands called **striations**. A dark space called the *Faraday dark space* appears near the cathode.
*   **At $\sim 0.01\text{ mmHg}$:** The positive column shrinks towards the anode. A new dark space, the *Crookes dark space*, expands from the cathode.
*   **Below $0.001\text{ mmHg}$ (Extreme Vacuum):** The glow inside the gas disappears completely. The tube looks dark. However, invisible streams of fast-moving electrons shoot straight out from the cathode. These are called **cathode rays**. When they hit the glass walls at the opposite end of the tube, they cause the glass to glow (fluoresce) with a bright yellow-green light.

#### Properties of Cathode Rays
Cathode rays (discovered by J.J. Thomson) were later identified as streams of electrons. Their properties include:
1.  They travel in straight lines (they will cast a sharp shadow of a solid object placed in their path).
2.  They possess kinetic energy and momentum (they can turn a small, light paddle wheel placed in the tube).
3.  They are negatively charged (they are deflected towards the positive plate of an electric field, and are deflected by magnetic fields).
4.  They cause certain materials (like zinc sulphide or glass) to fluoresce.
5.  If they strike a heavy metal target suddenly, their kinetic energy is converted into highly penetrating **X-rays**.

### Applications of Conduction of Electricity Through Gases

**Objective v: determine some applications/uses of conduction of electricity through gases.**

The scientific discoveries made from discharge tubes led to incredible modern technologies. JAMB expects you to identify the following applications:

1.  **Fluorescent Tubes:** These are widely used for lighting in homes, schools, and offices because they are more energy-efficient than traditional filament bulbs. A fluorescent tube contains a tiny amount of mercury vapour at low pressure. When electricity passes through, the mercury discharge produces invisible **ultraviolet (UV) radiation**. The inside of the glass tube is coated with a phosphor powder. This powder absorbs the UV radiation and fluoresces, emitting bright, visible white light.
2.  **Neon Signs:** Commonly seen in advertising boards at night. A glass tube is shaped into letters and filled with a specific gas at low pressure. Applying a high voltage makes the gas emit a brilliant glow. Neon gas gives a red light, sodium vapour gives yellow (often used in street lamps), and argon gives blue.
3.  **Cathode Ray Oscilloscopes (CRO) & Old Televisions:** Before flat screens were invented, televisions and computer monitors used huge vacuum tubes called Cathode Ray Tubes (CRT). A hot cathode emits a narrow beam of electrons (cathode rays). Because electrons are charged, magnetic and electric fields can deflect the beam rapidly to "draw" an image on a fluorescent screen. CROs are still used in physics and electronics labs to display electrical waveforms.
4.  **X-ray Tubes:** Used extensively in hospitals and airports. In an extreme vacuum tube, electrons are emitted by a hot filament (thermionic emission). They are accelerated by a massive voltage to hit a tungsten target. The sudden deceleration of the electrons produces X-rays, which can penetrate human tissue to photograph bones.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Electrolyte** | A liquid or solution that conducts electricity by means of mobile ions, and is chemically decomposed in the process. |
| **Electrolysis** | The chemical decomposition of an electrolyte by passing a direct current (D.C.) through it. |
| **Anode** | The positive electrode where current enters the electrolyte (attracts negative anions). |
| **Cathode** | The negative electrode where current leaves the electrolyte (attracts positive cations). |
| **Electrochemical equivalent ($z$)** | The mass of an element deposited by exactly 1 Coulomb of electricity (unit: $\text{g C}^{-1}$). |
| **Faraday constant ($F$)** | The total electric charge carried by exactly one mole of electrons ($96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$). |
| **Cathode Rays** | Streams of fast-moving electrons emitted from the cathode in a low-pressure discharge tube. |
| **Ionisation by Collision** | The process where fast-moving electrons collide with neutral gas molecules, knocking out electrons and creating ions, allowing the gas to conduct. |
| **Fluorescence** | The emission of visible light by a substance (like a glass wall or phosphor coating) when it is struck by electrons or UV light. |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

*   **Time unit failure:** The most common mistake students make in Faraday's Law calculations is forgetting to convert time to seconds. If a question gives "$20\text{ minutes}$", you **must** multiply by 60 to get $1200\text{ s}$ before using $Q = It$.
*   **Swapping Anode and Cathode rules:** Remember the memory aid **PANIC** (Positive is Anode, Negative is Cathode). In electroplating, if you connect the object you want to plate to the positive terminal by mistake, the object will dissolve into the solution instead of being coated! The object to be coated is *always* the cathode.
*   **Gas conduction conditions:** Exam questions often try to trick you by offering "high pressure and high voltage". Conduction in gases requires **LOW pressure** (a vacuum) and **HIGH voltage**.
*   **Ignoring valency ($n$):** When using the formula $m = \frac{MIt}{nF}$, remember that $n$ is not always 1. For Silver ($Ag^+$), $n=1$. For Copper ($Cu^{2+}$), $n=2$. For Aluminium ($Al^{3+}$), $n=3$.
*   **Mistaking the current:** Electrolysis strictly requires **Direct Current (D.C.)**. Alternating Current (A.C.) from a wall socket will not work because the rapidly switching polarity prevents a steady build-up of deposited metal on either electrode.

## Quick summary

1.  Electrolytes (like dilute acids and salt solutions) conduct electricity via free-moving ions and are chemically decomposed. Non-electrolytes (like pure water or kerosene) contain no ions and do not conduct.
2.  Electrolysis is the decomposition of an electrolyte by a direct electric current. Positively charged cations migrate to the negative cathode (where they gain electrons), and negatively charged anions migrate to the positive anode (where they lose electrons).
3.  The preferential discharge of ions depends on their position in the electrochemical series, their concentration, and the nature of the electrodes.
4.  Faraday’s First Law states $m = zIt$, linking mass deposited directly to the quantity of charge passed.
5.  Faraday’s Second Law allows calculating mass using molar mass and the Faraday constant ($F = 96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$): $m = \frac{MIt}{nF}$.
6.  Important applications of electrolysis include electroplating, metal purification, extraction of highly reactive metals (like aluminium), and calibrating ammeters.
7.  Gases are normally insulators but will conduct electricity via ionisation by collision under very low pressure and very high voltage.
8.  Gas discharge technology is applied in fluorescent tubes, neon signs, cathode ray oscilloscopes (CRO), and X-ray tubes.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following liquids is classified as a non-electrolyte?**
- A. Dilute tetraoxosulphate (VI) acid
- B. Sodium chloride solution
- C. Kerosene
- D. Copper (II) tetraoxosulphate (VI) solution

**Answer: C.** Kerosene is an organic compound made of covalent molecules. It does not dissociate into free mobile ions when in liquid form, so it cannot conduct electricity. The others are strong electrolytes.

**2. During the process of electrolysis, cations migrate towards the**
- A. anode
- B. cathode
- C. electrolyte
- D. salt bridge

**Answer: B.** Cations are positively charged ions. Since opposite charges attract, they migrate towards the negatively charged electrode, which is the cathode.

**3. A current of $3.0\text{ A}$ is passed through an electrolytic cell for $40\text{ minutes}$. If the electrochemical equivalent of the metal is $3.3 \times 10^{-4}\text{ g C}^{-1}$, what is the mass of the metal deposited?**
- A. $0.0396\text{ g}$
- B. $2.376\text{ g}$
- C. $3.96\text{ g}$
- D. $142.56\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** First, convert time to seconds: $t = 40 \times 60 = 2400\text{ s}$. Calculate total charge: $Q = I \times t = 3.0 \times 2400 = 7200\text{ C}$. Apply the first law: $m = zQ = 3.3 \times 10^{-4} \times 7200 = 2.376\text{ g}$. (Mistake A occurs if you fail to convert minutes to seconds: $3 \times 40 \times 3.3 \times 10^{-4} = 0.0396$).

**4. Which of the following sets of conditions is absolutely required for the conduction of electricity through a gas?**
- A. High pressure and high voltage
- B. Low pressure and high voltage
- C. Low pressure and low voltage
- D. High pressure and low voltage

**Answer: B.** Gases are normally excellent insulators. They only undergo ionisation by collision and conduct electricity when the pressure is made very low (using a vacuum pump) and the voltage applied across them is very high.

**5. In the electroplating of a cheap steel spoon with pure silver, the steel spoon must be made the**
- A. anode
- B. cathode
- C. electrolyte
- D. positive electrode

**Answer: B.** The object to be plated must always be placed at the cathode (the negative electrode). This is so that the positive silver ions ($Ag^+$) from the solution will migrate towards it and deposit as a solid metallic coating.

**6. What quantity of electricity is required to deposit exactly one mole of Aluminium ($Al^{3+}$) from its molten salt? (1 Faraday = $96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$)**
- A. $96500\text{ C}$
- B. $193000\text{ C}$
- C. $289500\text{ C}$
- D. $386000\text{ C}$

**Answer: C.** The valency/charge ($n$) of Aluminium is 3 ($Al^{3+}$). This means it requires 3 moles of electrons to deposit 1 mole of solid aluminium. Total charge $Q = n \times F = 3 \times 96500 = 289500\text{ C}$.

**7. What is the fundamental physics principle behind the operation of fluorescent tubes commonly used in Nigerian homes?**
- A. Electromagnetic induction
- B. Conduction of electricity through gases
- C. Heating effect of an electric current
- D. Electrolysis of a gas

**Answer: B.** Fluorescent tubes operate by passing an electric discharge through mercury vapour at a very low pressure. This gas conduction produces ultraviolet light, which in turn causes the phosphor coating inside the tube to fluoresce and emit visible white light.

**8. Calculate the electrochemical equivalent of a metal if a total electric charge of $96500\text{ C}$ deposits exactly $108\text{ g}$ of the metal.**
- A. $1.12 \times 10^{-4}\text{ g C}^{-1}$
- B. $1.12 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g C}^{-1}$
- C. $8.94 \times 10^{-4}\text{ g C}^{-1}$
- D. $8.94 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g C}^{-1}$

**Answer: B.** By rearranging Faraday's first law equation ($m = zQ$), we get $z = \frac{m}{Q}$. Substituting the given values yields $z = \frac{108}{96500} \approx 1.119 \times 10^{-3} \approx 1.12 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g C}^{-1}$.

**9. Two electrolytic cells containing solutions of Silver trioxonitrate (V) and Copper (II) tetraoxosulphate (VI) are connected in series. If $2.16\text{ g}$ of silver is deposited, what mass of copper will be deposited at the same time? (Molar mass of Ag = $108\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, Cu = $64\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, valency of Ag = 1, Cu = 2)**
- A. $0.32\text{ g}$
- B. $0.64\text{ g}$
- C. $1.28\text{ g}$
- D. $2.16\text{ g}$

**Answer: B.** By Faraday's second law, mass is proportional to the chemical equivalent ($E = \frac{M}{n}$). For Silver, $E_{Ag} = \frac{108}{1} = 108$. For Copper, $E_{Cu} = \frac{64}{2} = 32$. Since they are in series, the same charge flows through both, meaning $\frac{m_{Cu}}{E_{Cu}} = \frac{m_{Ag}}{E_{Ag}}$. Rearranging gives $m_{Cu} = \frac{2.16 \times 32}{108} = 0.64\text{ g}$. (Mistake C happens if you ignore the valency and use raw molar mass directly).

**10. How long will it take a steady current of $0.5\text{ A}$ to deposit $4.32\text{ g}$ of silver from a silver salt solution? (Given that the electrochemical equivalent of silver is $1.2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g C}^{-1}$)**
- A. $120\text{ minutes}$
- B. $360\text{ minutes}$
- C. $600\text{ minutes}$
- D. $7200\text{ minutes}$

**Answer: A.** From the first law, $m = zIt$. Rearranging to solve for time ($t$) gives $t = \frac{m}{zI} = \frac{4.32}{1.2 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.5} = 7200\text{ seconds}$. The options are in minutes. To convert seconds to minutes, divide by 60: $\frac{7200}{60} = 120\text{ minutes}$.
