## Analyse Elementary Radioactivity

**Objective x: analyse elementary radioactivity.**

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous disintegration (decay) of an unstable atomic nucleus, accompanied by the emission of ionizing radiation (such as alpha particles, beta particles, or gamma rays). 

It was discovered in 1896 by Henri Becquerel, who noticed that uranium salts emitted mysterious rays that could expose photographic plates in the dark. Later, Marie and Pierre Curie discovered other radioactive elements like polonium and radium.

Radioactivity is purely a **nuclear process**. It is a random and spontaneous event. This means you cannot predict exactly *when* a specific nucleus will decay, and the rate of decay is **unaffected by external physical or chemical conditions** like temperature, pressure, or chemical combination.

There are two main types:
- **Natural radioactivity:** The spontaneous decay of naturally occurring unstable nuclei (like Uranium-238 or Carbon-14).
- **Artificial radioactivity:** The decay of nuclei that have been made unstable in a laboratory by bombarding them with particles (like neutrons).

## Distinguish Between Stable and Unstable Nuclei

**Objective xi: distinguish between stable and unstable nuclei.**

The nucleus of an atom contains protons (positively charged) and neutrons (no charge). Since protons repel each other with strong electrostatic forces, the nucleus should theoretically fly apart. However, the **strong nuclear force** acts over very short distances to hold the protons and neutrons tightly together.

- **Stable nuclei** have a balance between the strong nuclear force and the electrostatic repulsion. For light elements (like Carbon or Oxygen), stability usually requires roughly equal numbers of protons and neutrons.
- **Unstable nuclei** lack this balance. If a nucleus has too many neutrons, too few neutrons, or is simply too large (atomic number greater than 82, e.g., Uranium or Radium), the strong nuclear force cannot overcome the electrostatic repulsion. 

Unstable nuclei "want" to become stable. They achieve this by undergoing radioactive decay—spitting out particles and energy until they turn into a stable nucleus (often ending up as an isotope of lead).

## Identify Isotopes of an Element

**Objective xii: identify isotopes of an element.**

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element that have the same atomic number (number of protons) but different mass numbers (number of neutrons).

We write isotopes in the form $^A_Z \text{X}$, where:
- **X** is the chemical symbol.
- **$Z$ (Atomic Number):** The number of protons (and electrons in a neutral atom).
- **$A$ (Mass Number):** The total number of protons + neutrons.

**Examples:**
- Carbon has three naturally occurring isotopes: $^{12}_6\text{C}$, $^{13}_6\text{C}$, and $^{14}_6\text{C}$. They all have 6 protons (so they are all carbon and share identical chemical properties), but they have 6, 7, and 8 neutrons, respectively. $^{14}_6\text{C}$ is an unstable isotope used for radioactive dating of ancient artifacts, like the Nok terracotta figures found in northern Nigeria.
- Uranium has isotopes like $^{235}_{92}\text{U}$ and $^{238}_{92}\text{U}$.

Because isotopes have the same number of electrons, their **chemical properties are exactly the same**. However, because they have different masses, their **physical properties** (like density and radioactivity) differ.

## Compare the Properties of Alpha, Beta and Gamma Rays

**Objective xiii: compare the properties of alpha, beta and gamma rays.**

When an unstable nucleus decays, it can emit three main types of radiation. 

| Property | Alpha ($\alpha$) | Beta ($\beta$) | Gamma ($\gamma$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nature** | A Helium nucleus ($^4_2\text{He}$) | A high-speed electron ($^0_{-1}\text{e}$) emitted from the nucleus | High-frequency electromagnetic wave (a photon) |
| **Charge** | $+2e$ (positive) | $-1e$ (negative) | Zero (neutral) |
| **Mass** | Heavy ($\approx 4\text{ u}$) | Very light ($\approx \frac{1}{1836}\text{ u}$) | Zero rest mass |
| **Speed** | Up to $10\%$ the speed of light | Up to $90\%$ the speed of light | Speed of light ($c = 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$) |
| **Ionizing power** | **Highest:** Knocks electrons off many air molecules as it passes. | **Moderate:** Less than alpha, more than gamma. | **Lowest:** Very weak ionizer. |
| **Penetrating power** | **Lowest:** Stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air. | **Moderate:** Stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. | **Highest:** Requires thick lead or thick concrete to be stopped. |
| **Deflection by fields** | Deflected towards the negative plate in an electric field. | Deflected strongly towards the positive plate in an electric field. | **Not deflected** by electric or magnetic fields. |

## Relate Half-Life and Decay Constant of a Radioactive Element

**Content x, Objective xiv: relate half-life and decay constant of a radioactive element.**

Because radioactive decay is a random process, we cannot predict when one atom will decay. But in a large lump of radioactive material (containing billions of atoms), the rate of decay follows a strict mathematical rule: **the rate of decay is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present.**

### The Decay Constant ($\lambda$)
The **decay constant ($\lambda$)** is defined as the fraction of the total number of radioactive nuclei in a sample that decays per unit time. Its unit is per second ($\text{s}^{-1}$), per day ($\text{day}^{-1}$), or per year ($\text{year}^{-1}$).

### Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$)
The **half-life ($T_{1/2}$)** is the time taken for exactly half the total number of radioactive nuclei in a given sample to decay.
If you start with $100\text{ g}$ of a radioactive substance with a half-life of 5 days, after 5 days you will have $50\text{ g}$ of the radioactive substance left. After another 5 days (10 days total), you will have $25\text{ g}$ left, and so on.

The relationship between the half-life and the decay constant is:
$$T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda} \quad \text{or} \quad T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

### Calculating Remaining Mass
The formula for the number of nuclei (or mass) remaining after a time $t$ is:
$$N_t = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$

For JAMB calculations without advanced calculators, a simpler formula is much safer:
$$N_t = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$$
where:
- $N_t$ = Amount remaining after time $t$
- $N_0$ = Initial amount
- $n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}}$ (The number of half-lives that have passed)

### Common mistakes in Half-Life calculations
- **Wrong fraction left:** Assuming that after two half-lives, $100\%$ of the substance has decayed ($50\% \times 2$).
  - *Wrong working:* Remaining mass = Initial mass $- (2 \times 50\%) = 0\text{ g}$.
  - *Corrected version:* The remaining fraction halves each time. After two half-lives, multiply by $\frac{1}{2}$ twice. Remaining mass = Initial mass $\times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 =$ Initial mass $\times \frac{1}{4}$.

**Worked Example 1: Finding the remaining mass**
A radioactive isotope of iodine used to trace groundwater in Nigerian boreholes has a half-life of $8.0\text{ days}$. If the initial mass of the sample is $100\text{ g}$, what mass will remain after $24\text{ days}$?
1. Identify the given values: Initial mass $N_0 = 100\text{ g}$, half-life $T_{1/2} = 8.0\text{ days}$, total time $t = 24\text{ days}$.
2. Find the number of half-lives ($n$): Divide the total time by the half-life.
   $n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{24\text{ days}}{8.0\text{ days}} = 3$
3. Write the radioactive decay formula:
   $N_t = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$
4. Substitute the values and calculate:
   $N_t = 100\text{ g} \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = 100\text{ g} \times \frac{1}{8} = 12.5\text{ g}$
5. State the final answer: The mass remaining after $24\text{ days}$ is $12.5\text{ g}$.

**Worked Example 2: Finding decay constant from half-life**
A radioactive isotope has a half-life of $20\text{ days}$. Calculate its decay constant. ($\ln 2 = 0.693$)
1. Identify the given values: $T_{1/2} = 20\text{ days}$.
2. Write the relationship formula: 
   $\lambda = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}}$
3. Substitute the values and calculate:
   $\lambda = \frac{0.693}{20\text{ days}} = 0.03465\text{ day}^{-1}$
4. State the final answer: The decay constant is approximately $0.0347\text{ day}^{-1}$.

## Production of Energy by Fusion and Fission

**Content xi: simple ideas of production of energy by fusion and fission.**

Both fusion and fission are nuclear reactions that release massive amounts of energy (millions of times more than chemical reactions like burning coal). They do this by converting a small amount of mass into energy.

### Nuclear Fission
**Nuclear Fission** is the splitting of a heavy, unstable nucleus (like Uranium-235) into two smaller, more stable nuclei of roughly equal mass.
- This process is usually triggered by bombarding the heavy nucleus with a slow-moving neutron.
- When it splits, it releases immense energy and 2 or 3 extra neutrons.
- These extra neutrons can hit other Uranium nuclei, causing them to split. This creates a **chain reaction**.
- **Applications:** Controlled chain reactions generate heat for electricity in nuclear power plants. Uncontrolled chain reactions are the basis of atomic bombs.

### Nuclear Fusion
**Nuclear Fusion** is the joining together of two light nuclei (like Hydrogen isotopes) to form a single, heavier, and more stable nucleus.
- **Example:** Fusing Deuterium ($^2_1\text{H}$) and Tritium ($^3_1\text{H}$) to form Helium ($^4_2\text{He}$).
- Fusion releases **more energy per unit mass** than fission and does not produce long-lived dangerous radioactive waste.
- **The catch:** Because both nuclei are positively charged, they repel each other strongly. To force them together, they must collide at tremendously high speeds. This requires astronomical temperatures (millions of degrees), which is why fusion is called a **thermonuclear reaction**.
- **Applications:** Fusion is the process that powers the Sun and all stars. It is also the mechanism behind the hydrogen bomb. Scientists are actively trying to build fusion reactors for clean energy on Earth.

## Determine Binding Energy, Mass Defect and Einstein's Equation

**Content xii, Objective xv: determine the binding energy, mass defect and Einstein’s energy equation $[\Delta E = \Delta Mc^2]$.**

### Einstein's Mass-Energy Equation
Albert Einstein theorised that mass and energy are interchangeable. Mass can be converted into energy, and vice versa, according to the famous equation:
$$\Delta E = \Delta m c^2$$
where:
- $\Delta E$ = Energy equivalent (in Joules, $\text{J}$)
- $\Delta m$ = Change in mass (in $\text{kg}$)
- $c$ = Speed of light in a vacuum ($3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$)

### Mass Defect ($\Delta m$)
If you measure the mass of a whole nucleus, it is always **slightly less** than the sum of the masses of its individual protons and neutrons (nucleons). This missing mass is called the **mass defect**.
$$\Delta m = (\text{Sum of masses of protons and neutrons}) - (\text{Actual mass of the nucleus})$$
Where did the missing mass go? When the protons and neutrons bound together to form the nucleus, the missing mass was converted into energy and radiated away.

### Binding Energy (BE)
The **binding energy** is the energy equivalent of the mass defect. It is the energy released when the nucleus was formed. Conversely, it is the exact amount of energy you would need to supply to break the nucleus apart into individual protons and neutrons.
- If the mass defect is in kilograms ($\text{kg}$), calculate BE in Joules using $BE = \Delta m c^2$.
- In nuclear physics, masses are usually tiny and are measured in **atomic mass units ($\text{u}$)**, where $1\text{ u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$.
- Einstein's equation shows that a mass defect of $1\text{ u}$ converts to an energy of roughly $931\text{ MeV}$ (Mega electron-Volts).
- Therefore, a faster shortcut formula used heavily in exams is:
  $BE \text{ (in MeV)} = \Delta m \text{ (in u)} \times 931$
*(Note: Some textbooks use $931.5\text{ MeV}$, but JAMB will state the constant to use).*

### Common mistakes in Mass-Energy calculations
- **Wrong units:** Using mass in atomic mass units ($\text{u}$) in the formula $E = m c^2$ to find energy in Joules.
  - *Wrong working:* $E = 0.0304\text{ u} \times (3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1})^2 = 2.7 \times 10^{15}\text{ J}$.
  - *Corrected version:* Mass MUST be in $\text{kg}$ to use $c^2$ for Joules. If mass is in $\text{u}$, simply multiply by $931$ to find the energy directly in $\text{MeV}$.

**Worked Example 3: Calculating Energy from Mass in kg**
Calculate the energy released if a mass of $1.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}$ is completely converted to energy. ($c = 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$)
1. Identify the given values: $\Delta m = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}$, $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
2. Write Einstein's formula: 
   $\Delta E = \Delta m c^2$
3. Substitute the values and calculate:
   $\Delta E = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg} \times (3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1})^2$
   $\Delta E = 1.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 13.5 \times 10^{13}\text{ J} = 1.35 \times 10^{14}\text{ J}$
4. State the final answer: The energy released is $1.35 \times 10^{14}\text{ J}$.

**Worked Example 4: Calculating Mass Defect and Binding Energy in MeV**
Calculate the mass defect and binding energy in $\text{MeV}$ of a Helium-4 nucleus ($^4_2\text{He}$). 
(Given: Mass of a proton $m_p = 1.0078\text{ u}$, Mass of a neutron $m_n = 1.0087\text{ u}$, Mass of Helium-4 nucleus $M = 4.0026\text{ u}$, $1\text{ u} = 931\text{ MeV}$)
1. Identify the composition of the nucleus: A Helium-4 nucleus has an atomic number $Z = 2$ (2 protons) and neutron number $N = 4 - 2 = 2$ (2 neutrons).
2. Calculate the sum of the masses of the individual nucleons:
   Mass of 2 protons = $2 \times 1.0078\text{ u} = 2.0156\text{ u}$
   Mass of 2 neutrons = $2 \times 1.0087\text{ u} = 2.0174\text{ u}$
   Total mass of nucleons = $2.0156\text{ u} + 2.0174\text{ u} = 4.0330\text{ u}$
3. Calculate the mass defect ($\Delta m$): Subtract the actual nucleus mass from the total nucleon mass.
   $\Delta m = 4.0330\text{ u} - 4.0026\text{ u} = 0.0304\text{ u}$
4. Write the formula for binding energy using the conversion factor:
   $BE\text{ (in MeV)} = \Delta m \times 931$
5. Substitute and calculate the binding energy:
   $BE = 0.0304 \times 931 = 28.3\text{ MeV}$
6. State the final answers: The mass defect is $0.0304\text{ u}$ and the binding energy is $28.3\text{ MeV}$.

## Analyse Wave-Particle Duality

**Content xiii, Objective xvi: analyse wave-particle duality.**

In classical physics, waves and particles were entirely different things. However, modern physics proves that nature has a dual character:
- Electromagnetic waves (like light) sometimes behave as waves (showing interference and diffraction) and sometimes behave as particles (photons, in the photoelectric effect).
- In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed that **matter** (particles like electrons, protons, and even everyday objects) also possesses **wave properties**. 

This concept is the **wave-particle duality of matter**.

De Broglie deduced an equation to calculate the wavelength of moving matter:
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{m v}$$
where:
- $\lambda$ = de Broglie wavelength (in $\text{m}$)
- $h$ = Planck's constant ($6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s}$)
- $p$ = Momentum of the particle ($p = m v$, in $\text{kg m s}^{-1}$)
- $m$ = Mass (in $\text{kg}$)
- $v$ = Velocity (in $\text{m s}^{-1}$)

Because Planck's constant is incredibly small, the wave nature of heavy objects (like a moving car) is entirely unnoticeable. However, for extremely light particles like electrons, the wavelength is large enough to be measured.

**Worked Example 5: De Broglie wavelength of a macroscopic object**
Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a ball of mass $0.20\text{ kg}$ moving at a velocity of $15\text{ m s}^{-1}$. (Take Planck's constant $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s}$)
1. Identify the given values: Mass $m = 0.20\text{ kg}$, velocity $v = 15\text{ m s}^{-1}$, $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s}$.
2. Calculate the momentum ($p$): Multiply mass by velocity.
   $p = m \times v = 0.20\text{ kg} \times 15\text{ m s}^{-1} = 3.0\text{ kg m s}^{-1}$
3. Write the de Broglie equation:
   $\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$
4. Substitute the values and calculate:
   $\lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s}}{3.0\text{ kg m s}^{-1}} = 2.21 \times 10^{-34}\text{ m}$
5. State the final answer: The wavelength is $2.21 \times 10^{-34}\text{ m}$. (This demonstrates that macroscopic objects have wavelengths too tiny to detect).

## Electron Diffraction

**Content xiv: electron diffraction.**

How do we prove that particles like electrons actually behave as waves? The definitive proof came from the phenomenon of **diffraction**, which is strictly a wave property (waves bending around obstacles or spreading out).

In 1927, physicists Davisson and Germer (and independently G.P. Thomson) fired a beam of fast-moving electrons through a very thin slice of a crystal (like graphite or nickel). Instead of scattering randomly like solid bullets, the electrons struck a fluorescent screen and formed a pattern of concentric bright and dark rings. 

This **diffraction pattern** is exactly what light waves do. It provided the ultimate experimental evidence for the wave nature of matter.

**Application:** The principles of electron diffraction led to the invention of the **Electron Microscope**. Because the de Broglie wavelength of moving electrons is much shorter than the wavelength of visible light, an electron microscope has an incredibly high resolving power, allowing scientists to see individual viruses and even atoms, which optical microscopes cannot resolve.

## Uncertainty Principle and Wave-Particle Problems

**Content xv, Objective xvii: the uncertainty principle; solve some numerical problems.**

In 1927, Werner Heisenberg formulated the **Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle**. It highlights a fundamental limit of quantum mechanics.

The principle states that **it is impossible to determine simultaneously and exactly both the position and the momentum of a quantum particle** (like an electron).

If you try to measure the exact position of an electron (e.g., by bouncing a high-energy photon off it), the impact of the photon drastically alters the electron's momentum. The more accurately you know the position, the more uncertain you become about its momentum, and vice versa.

The mathematical limit of this uncertainty is:
$$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$$
where:
- $\Delta x$ = Uncertainty in position (in $\text{m}$)
- $\Delta p$ = Uncertainty in momentum (in $\text{kg m s}^{-1}$)
- $h$ = Planck's constant ($6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s}$)

**Textbook Variation Note:** Some older textbooks and WAEC/JAMB past questions simplify the relationship to $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \approx h$ or $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{2\pi}$. For calculation questions, always look at the constant provided. If the question gives a value for $\frac{h}{4\pi}$, use exactly that in your formula.

### Common mistakes in Uncertainty calculations
- **Wrong formula variation:** Using the wrong constant variation for the uncertainty relationship because different books use different limits.
  - *Wrong working:* Always using $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge h$, even when the exam question explicitly provides the constant $\frac{h}{4\pi}$.
  - *Corrected version:* Always check the constants provided in the question bracket. If they give $h / 4\pi$, use $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p = \frac{h}{4\pi}$ for the minimum limit.

**Worked Example 6: Uncertainty Principle calculation**
The minimum uncertainty in the momentum of an electron is $5.27 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg m s}^{-1}$. Calculate the minimum uncertainty in its position.
(Take $\frac{h}{4\pi} = 5.27 \times 10^{-35}\text{ J s}$)
1. Identify the given values: Uncertainty in momentum $\Delta p = 5.27 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg m s}^{-1}$, constant $\frac{h}{4\pi} = 5.27 \times 10^{-35}\text{ J s}$.
2. Write the Heisenberg uncertainty principle equation: Use the exact limit for minimum uncertainty.
   $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p = \frac{h}{4\pi}$
3. Rearrange to make the uncertainty in position ($\Delta x$) the subject:
   $\Delta x = \frac{h / 4\pi}{\Delta p}$
4. Substitute the values and calculate:
   $\Delta x = \frac{5.27 \times 10^{-35}\text{ J s}}{5.27 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg m s}^{-1}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$
5. State the final answer: The minimum uncertainty in the electron's position is $1.0 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$.

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Alpha is heaviest; Gamma is massless:** Know the properties table. Alpha particles are heavy and doubly charged, so they are strongly ionizing but have low penetration. Gamma rays are neutral and massless, giving them the highest penetration power.
- **Isotopes share chemistry:** Isotopes have identical chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons.
- **Calculating half-lives:** Never divide the initial mass by the total time. First, find how many half-lives have passed, then halve the mass that many times.
- **Mass units for energy:** In $\Delta E = \Delta m c^2$, the mass MUST be in kilograms to get Joules. If mass defect is given in $\text{u}$, convert it to $\text{MeV}$ using the given multiplier (usually $931$).
- **Wave-particle duality proof:** If asked what experiment proves electrons act like waves, the answer is always **electron diffraction**. 

## Quick summary

1. Radioactivity is the random, spontaneous decay of an unstable nucleus with the emission of alpha, beta, or gamma rays.
2. Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same atomic number but a different mass number due to varying neutrons.
3. Half-life is the time taken for half the radioactive sample to decay. It is related to the decay constant by $T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$.
4. Nuclear fusion joins light nuclei into a heavier one at extreme temperatures, while nuclear fission splits a heavy nucleus with a neutron; both reactions release huge amounts of energy.
5. The mass of a nucleus is less than the mass of its individual protons and neutrons. This mass defect converts to binding energy according to Einstein's equation, $\Delta E = \Delta m c^2$.
6. Wave-particle duality suggests that matter (like electrons) exhibits both particle and wave behaviours. The de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda = \frac{h}{m v}$.
7. Electron diffraction provides the experimental proof of the wave nature of electrons, leading to technologies like the electron microscope.
8. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle asserts that we cannot exactly and simultaneously measure a quantum particle's position and momentum ($\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$).

## Practice questions

**1. A radioactive element has a half-life of $12\text{ years}$. If its original mass is $128\text{ g}$, what mass remains after $48\text{ years}$?**
- A. $16\text{ g}$
- B. $32\text{ g}$
- C. $8\text{ g}$
- D. $4\text{ g}$

**Answer: C.** 
1. Identify the given values: $N_0 = 128\text{ g}$, $T_{1/2} = 12\text{ years}$, $t = 48\text{ years}$.
2. Calculate the number of half-lives ($n$): $n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{48\text{ years}}{12\text{ years}} = 4$.
3. Write the decay formula: $N_t = N_0 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$.
4. Substitute the values: $N_t = 128\text{ g} \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4$.
5. Calculate the final answer: $N_t = 128\text{ g} \times \frac{1}{16} = 8\text{ g}$.
The mistake behind option A is miscounting the number of half-lives as $3$ instead of $4$, which leaves $128\text{ g} \times \frac{1}{8} = 16\text{ g}$.

**2. A radioactive substance has a half-life of $20\text{ days}$. Calculate its decay constant. ($\ln 2 = 0.693$)**
- A. $0.0347\text{ day}^{-1}$
- B. $0.0693\text{ day}^{-1}$
- C. $28.86\text{ day}^{-1}$
- D. $13.86\text{ day}^{-1}$

**Answer: A.** 
1. Identify the half-life: $T_{1/2} = 20\text{ days}$.
2. Write the relationship formula: $\lambda = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}}$.
3. Substitute the values: $\lambda = \frac{0.693}{20\text{ days}}$.
4. Calculate the answer: $0.03465\text{ day}^{-1} \approx 0.0347\text{ day}^{-1}$.
The mistake behind option C is dividing the half-life by $0.693$ instead of the other way around ($20\text{ days} / 0.693 = 28.86\text{ day}^{-1}$).

**3. The mass defect of an oxygen-16 nucleus is $0.137\text{ u}$. What is its binding energy? (Take $1\text{ u} = 931\text{ MeV}$)**
- A. $127.5\text{ MeV}$
- B. $6795\text{ MeV}$
- C. $1.37\text{ MeV}$

**7. A nucleus has a mass defect of $0.02\text{ u}$. Calculate its binding energy in Joules. (Take $1\text{ u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$ and $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$)**

- C. $9.96 \times 10^{-21}\text{ J}$
- D. $1.80 \times 10^{12}\text{ J}$

**Answer: B.**

2. Convert mass defect to $\text{kg}$: $\Delta m = 0.02 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg} = 3.32 \times 10^{-29}\text{ kg}$.
3. Write Einstein's formula: $\Delta E = \Delta m c^2$.
4. Substitute into the formula: $\Delta E = 3.32 \times 10^{-29}\text{ kg} \times (3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1})^2$.
5. Square the speed of light: $(3.0 \times 10^8)^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{16}\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-2}$.
