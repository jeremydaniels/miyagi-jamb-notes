# JAMB Use of English Notes: Words, Phrases, Clauses, Figures of Speech and Idioms in Passages

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Comprehension and Summary. It covers:

- **Test (b):** comprehension of words, phrases, clauses, sentences, figures of speech and idioms as used in the passages.
- **Objective iii:** identify the grammatical functions of words, phrases, clauses and figurative/idiomatic expressions.

These questions appear mainly with the **comprehension passage (5 questions)** and the **summary passage (5 questions)**, and the same skills help you in the **cloze passage (10 questions)**.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Work out the meaning of a word as it is used in a passage, even when you have never seen it before, using context clues.
2. Explain what a phrase, clause or whole sentence in a passage means.
3. Recognise the common figures of speech and explain what they mean in context.
4. Explain the meaning of idioms used in a passage.
5. Name the grammatical function of a word (subject, object, complement, modifier and so on).
6. Name a phrase or clause (noun phrase, adverbial clause of time, and so on) and say what job it does in the sentence.

Synonyms, antonyms, word classes, clause patterns and figurative and idiomatic usage are taught in more depth in Section B (B1, B2, B3a, B4a–B4d, B7a and B7b). This file focuses on **using those skills inside a passage**, which is how Section A tests them.

**About the passages in these notes:** every passage here is original, written for practice. The people, small communities and events in them are invented, even when a real state or city is named for a Nigerian setting. Do not treat them as real news or history.

## Part 1: The meaning of words as used in the passage

### Contextual meaning vs dictionary meaning

Many English words have **more than one meaning**. JAMB asks for the meaning "**as used in the passage**", which is the **contextual meaning**: the meaning the word has in that particular sentence.

| Word | Meaning 1 | Meaning 2 | Meaning 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *run* | to move fast on foot (*She runs every morning.*) | to manage (*He runs a pharmacy in Onitsha.*) | to operate (*The generator runs on diesel.*) |
| *settle* | to pay (*settle a bill*) | to make one's home (*They settled in Jos.*) | to end a disagreement (*settle a dispute*) |
| *sound* | a noise | healthy or reliable (*a sound mind; sound advice*) | to seem (*That sounds interesting.*) |
| *fair* | just, honest (*a fair judge*) | light in colour (*fair skin*) | a market or exhibition (*a trade fair*) |
| *check* | to examine | to stop or control (*check the spread of disease*) | a bill in a restaurant (mainly American) |

**JAMB trap:** the wrong options often include the **most common meaning** of the word, which is not the meaning in the passage.

**Example:**

> The new policy is meant to **check** the rising cost of fertiliser.

"Check" here means **control/restrain**, not "examine". An option saying "inspect" would be a trap.

### The substitution test (the most useful method)

1. Go back to the sentence in the passage.
2. Read the sentence before and after it.
3. **Replace** the word with each option in turn.
4. Choose the option that keeps the **meaning** of the sentence **and** fits the **grammar** (the same word class, tense and number).

**Example:**

> After weeks of drought, the farmers were **elated** when the first heavy rain fell.

| Option | Substituted sentence | Fits? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. surprised | "the farmers were surprised when the first heavy rain fell" | Possible, but rain after waiting is not mainly surprising |
| B. overjoyed | "the farmers were overjoyed when the first heavy rain fell" | Best: drought ends, rain helps crops |
| C. worried | "the farmers were worried..." | Contradicts the context |
| D. tired | "the farmers were tired..." | No support |

**Answer: B.** *Elated* means very happy and excited.

### Context clues: how to guess unfamiliar words

Writers often leave clues that help you work out a hard word.

| Type of clue | How it works | Signal words | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | The writer explains the word | is, means, refers to, that is, or, dashes, brackets | "The patient had **hypertension**, that is, high blood pressure." |
| **Synonym/restatement** | A word with a similar meaning appears nearby | or, in other words, also | "The chief was known to be **frugal**; he was careful with every kobo." |
| **Antonym/contrast** | A word with the opposite meaning appears nearby | but, however, unlike, whereas, instead of | "Unlike his **garrulous** brother, Tunde hardly said a word." (garrulous = talkative) |
| **Example** | Examples show what the word means | such as, for example, like, including | "**Legumes** such as beans, groundnuts and soya beans add nitrogen to the soil." |
| **General sense (inference)** | The whole situation suggests the meaning | (none) | "The **arduous** climb up Obudu's steep hills left the tourists exhausted." (arduous = very difficult, tiring) |
| **Cause and effect** | The cause or result explains the word | because, so, as a result | "Because the bridge was **dilapidated**, the government closed it." (dilapidated = in a bad, broken-down condition) |
| **Word parts** | Prefixes, roots and suffixes give clues | — | *un-* (not), *re-* (again), *mis-* (wrongly), *-less* (without), *bio* (life), *-cide* (killing) |

**Common prefixes and suffixes that help with meaning:**

| Part | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *un-, in-, im-, il-, ir-, dis-* | not, opposite | unkind, inaccurate, impossible, illegal, irregular, dishonest |
| *mis-* | wrongly | misuse, mislead |
| *re-* | again, back | rebuild, return |
| *pre-* | before | prepaid, predict |
| *post-* | after | postpone, post-war |
| *anti-* | against | antibiotic, anti-corruption |
| *inter-* | between | international, interstate |
| *-less* | without | careless, homeless |
| *-ful* | full of | hopeful, careful |
| *-able, -ible* | can be | readable, visible |
| *-cide* | killing | pesticide, homicide |

**Warning:** word parts are a clue, not a rule. *Inflammable* means "easily set on fire" (the same as *flammable*), not "not flammable". *Invaluable* means "extremely valuable", not "of no value". Always check against the sentence.

### Connotation: the feeling a word carries

Words with similar dictionary meanings can carry different feelings (**connotations**). Writers choose words to show their attitude.

| Neutral | Positive | Negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| thin | slim, slender | skinny, bony |
| old | experienced, mature | ancient, outdated |
| careful with money | thrifty, economical | stingy, miserly |
| determined | resolute, persistent | stubborn, obstinate |
| crowd | gathering, audience | mob, rabble |

**Example:** "The **mob** gathered outside the council office" suggests disorder and danger; "the **crowd**" is neutral. JAMB may ask what a word suggests about the writer's view (see A2c).

### Worked example: words in context

> Mrs Abiodun runs a small bakery in Osogbo. When flour prices **soared** last year, many bakers raised the price of bread or reduced the size of the loaf. Mrs Abiodun chose a third path: she **diversified**, adding cassava flour to her recipe and selling meat pies and chin-chin alongside bread. Her customers, who had feared the worst, were **relieved** to find their favourite loaf at the same price.

| Word | Clue | Meaning in context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *runs* | "a small bakery" | manages/owns and operates (not "moves fast") |
| *soared* | prices + bakers raised prices | rose very quickly and greatly |
| *diversified* | "adding cassava flour… selling meat pies and chin-chin alongside bread" | widened the range of her products and ingredients |
| *relieved* | "had feared the worst" → loaf at same price | freed from worry |

## Part 2: The meaning of phrases, clauses and sentences in the passage

JAMB may ask what a **group of words** means: a phrase ("at the eleventh hour"), a clause ("as soon as the ink was dry") or a whole sentence ("The writer means that…").

### Step by step

1. **Locate** the expression and read the whole sentence and the sentences around it.
2. **Break it down:** what does each important word contribute?
3. **Put it in your own words** (paraphrase) before you look at the options.
4. **Match** your paraphrase to the options. The correct option is usually a paraphrase, not a copy.
5. **Check the tone:** is the expression literal, figurative, ironic or idiomatic? (See Parts 3 and 4.)

### Worked examples

> The contract was signed **at the eleventh hour**, just before the deadline expired.

"At the eleventh hour" = **at the last possible moment**. The clue is "just before the deadline expired". This is an idiom.

> The minister's promise was **a promise written in sand**.

The sentence means the promise would **not last / would not be kept**, because writing in sand is easily washed away. This is a metaphor.

> **Had the driver slowed down**, the accident would not have happened.

"Had the driver slowed down" = **if the driver had slowed down**. The sentence implies that the driver did **not** slow down and that this caused the accident. (This inverted conditional structure is a favourite in sentence interpretation; see B8.)

## Part 3: Figures of speech in passages

A **figure of speech** is an expression that uses words in a non-literal way to create an effect: a picture, emphasis, humour or feeling. JAMB asks you to **name** the figure of speech and to **explain** what it means in the passage. B7a covers figurative usage in Section B.

### The main figures of speech

| Figure of speech | What it is | Example | What it means/does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Simile** | A direct comparison using *like* or *as* | "The old man was as stubborn as a mule." | He refused to change his mind |
| **Metaphor** | An implied comparison: one thing is said to *be* another | "The classroom was a furnace." | The classroom was extremely hot |
| **Personification** | Giving human qualities to non-human things | "The harmattan wind bit our faces." | The wind was very cold and dry, painful on the skin |
| **Hyperbole** | Deliberate exaggeration for effect | "I have told you a million times." | I have told you many times |
| **Litotes** | Understatement using a negative to express a positive | "The meal was not bad at all." | The meal was quite good |
| **Understatement** (meiosis) | Making something seem smaller than it is | "The flood was a bit of a problem." (after houses were destroyed) | Creates irony or calm |
| **Euphemism** | A mild word for something unpleasant | "Her grandfather passed on last year." | He died |
| **Irony** | Saying the opposite of what is meant, or a contrast between expectation and reality | "What lovely weather!" (during a storm) | The weather is terrible |
| **Sarcasm** | Bitter, mocking irony meant to hurt or criticise | "Well done, genius. You locked the keys inside the car." | Mocking the person's mistake |
| **Oxymoron** | Two opposite words placed together | "deafening silence", "bitter-sweet", "open secret" | A striking, contradictory idea |
| **Paradox** | A statement that seems to contradict itself but contains truth | "The more you learn, the more you realise how little you know." | Learning reveals how much is still unknown |
| **Antithesis** | Contrasting ideas placed in balanced phrases | "Man proposes, God disposes." | Plans contrast with outcomes |
| **Metonymy** | Using something closely associated with a thing to stand for it | "The pen is mightier than the sword." (pen = writing; sword = force) | Writing/ideas are more powerful than violence |
| **Synecdoche** | A part stands for the whole (or the whole for a part) | "All hands on deck." (hands = workers) | Everyone come and help |
| **Apostrophe** | Addressing an absent person, a dead person or a thing as if present | "O Death, where is your sting?" | Speaking directly to death |
| **Rhetorical question** | A question asked for effect, not for an answer | "Who does not want peace?" | Everyone wants peace |
| **Alliteration** | Repetition of the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words | "**P**eter **P**iper **p**icked a **p**eck…" | Sound effect, memorability |
| **Onomatopoeia** | A word that imitates the sound it describes | "The bees buzzed", "The oil sizzled" | Sound effect |
| **Climax** | Ideas arranged in rising order of importance | "He came, he saw, he conquered." | Builds to a strong ending |
| **Anticlimax** | A sudden drop from something serious to something trivial | "He lost his job, his house and his umbrella." | Humour or mockery |
| **Pun** | A play on words with two meanings | "A good tailor always suits his customers." | Humour |
| **Allusion** | A brief reference to a well-known person, story or event | "He is a real Judas." | He is a traitor (biblical reference) |

**Notes on textbook variation:**

- Some textbooks treat **personification** as a kind of metaphor, and some treat **litotes** as a kind of understatement. If both appear as options, choose the more specific name that fits.
- Some books do not separate **metonymy** and **synecdoche**; others separate them strictly (association vs part-whole).
- **Sarcasm** is often taught as a form of irony.
- Lists of figures of speech differ in length from book to book. The ones above are the most commonly tested.

### Comparing figures of speech that are often confused

| Pair | Difference | Example 1 | Example 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simile vs metaphor | Simile uses *like/as*; metaphor does not | "She sings like a nightingale." (simile) | "She is a nightingale." (metaphor) |
| Metaphor vs personification | Metaphor compares any two things; personification gives human qualities to non-human things | "Life is a journey." (metaphor) | "The sun smiled on the farm." (personification) |
| Hyperbole vs litotes | Hyperbole exaggerates up; litotes uses a negative to understate | "I'm so hungry I could eat a whole cow." | "He is not unfamiliar with trouble." |
| Irony vs sarcasm | Irony is a contrast of meaning; sarcasm is irony intended to mock or hurt | "The fire station burnt down." (situational irony) | "Oh, brilliant idea, setting fire to the kitchen." |
| Oxymoron vs paradox | Oxymoron is two contradictory words together; paradox is a whole statement that seems contradictory | "a wise fool" | "Less is more." |
| Metonymy vs synecdoche | Metonymy uses an associated thing; synecdoche uses a part for the whole | "The Villa has issued a statement." (Aso Villa = the Presidency) | "He has many mouths to feed." (mouths = people) |
| Euphemism vs litotes | Euphemism softens something unpleasant; litotes understates with a negative | "He was let go." (sacked) | "It was no small achievement." |

### How to answer figure-of-speech questions: step by step

1. **Ask: is the expression literal?** If it is literally true, it is not figurative (though it may still be alliteration or onomatopoeia, which are sound devices).
2. **Look for "like" or "as… as".** If present and comparing unlike things, it is probably a simile.
3. **Is something said to be something else?** Metaphor.
4. **Is a thing given human actions or feelings?** Personification.
5. **Is it an exaggeration?** Hyperbole. **An understatement with "not"?** Litotes.
6. **Does it mean the opposite?** Irony (or sarcasm, if mocking).
7. **Are two opposites joined?** Oxymoron. **Does the statement seem self-contradictory?** Paradox.
8. **Is a related thing or part standing for the whole?** Metonymy or synecdoche.
9. **Then explain the meaning** in the context of the passage.

**JAMB trap:** "as" alone does not make a simile. "She worked **as** a nurse" is literal. A simile compares two **unlike** things: "as busy as a bee".

### Worked example: figures of speech in a passage

> The hall was a sea of green and white as the students waited for the governor. When he finally arrived, two hours late, the principal smiled and said, "Thank you for coming so early, Your Excellency." The crowd laughed. The governor's speech, which promised new laboratories, was music to the teachers' ears, but the old roof above them groaned in the wind as if it had heard such promises before.

| Expression | Figure of speech | Meaning in context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "a sea of green and white" | Metaphor (and hyperbole) | A very large crowd dressed in green and white |
| "Thank you for coming so early" | Irony | He came very late; the principal is gently mocking his lateness |
| "music to the teachers' ears" | Metaphor (also a common idiom) | The promise pleased the teachers very much |
| "the old roof… groaned" | Personification | The roof creaked in the wind |
| "as if it had heard such promises before" | Personification within a comparison | Suggests doubt: such promises have been made before and not kept |

**Implied meaning:** the last sentence hints that the writer doubts the governor's promise will be kept. (Implied meanings are covered in A2a and the writer's attitude in A2c.)

## Part 4: Idioms in passages

An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning **cannot be worked out from the meanings of its individual words**. "To kick the bucket" means "to die", not to kick a bucket.

The syllabus says (in Section B) that idioms tested are **formal and expressed in Standard British English (SBE)**. In passages, idioms are used naturally, and you must explain their meaning from context. B7b gives a long list of idioms.

### Common idioms you may meet in passages

| Idiom | Meaning | Example sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| at the eleventh hour | at the last possible moment | The strike was called off at the eleventh hour. |
| a bolt from the blue | a complete surprise | The news of his resignation came as a bolt from the blue. |
| burn the midnight oil | study or work late into the night | Students burn the midnight oil before WAEC. |
| the lion's share | the largest part | The northern states received the lion's share of the grant. |
| turn over a new leaf | change one's behaviour for the better | After leaving prison, he turned over a new leaf. |
| beat about the bush | avoid the main point | Stop beating about the bush and tell me what happened. |
| bury the hatchet | end a quarrel and become friends | The two families finally buried the hatchet. |
| in the same boat | in the same difficult situation | With the fuel scarcity, all motorists were in the same boat. |
| take the bull by the horns | face a difficulty boldly | The new principal took the bull by the horns and tackled lateness. |
| throw in the towel | give up | After three failed attempts, he refused to throw in the towel. |
| a storm in a teacup | a lot of fuss about something unimportant | The quarrel over the seating plan was a storm in a teacup. |
| let the cat out of the bag | reveal a secret by mistake | She let the cat out of the bag about the surprise party. |
| a white elephant | something costly that brings no benefit | The abandoned stadium has become a white elephant. |
| at sixes and sevens | in confusion, disorganised | The office was at sixes and sevens after the manager left. |
| a red-letter day | a very important and happy day | Matriculation day was a red-letter day for the family. |
| to make ends meet | to manage on one's income | Many families struggle to make ends meet. |
| to see eye to eye | to agree | The two leaders do not see eye to eye on the matter. |
| to call a spade a spade | to speak plainly and honestly | The auditor called a spade a spade in her report. |

### How to work out an idiom from context: step by step

1. **Recognise it as an idiom:** the literal meaning makes no sense in the passage (nobody literally "burns oil").
2. **Look at the situation:** what is happening before and after?
3. **Predict the meaning** in plain words.
4. **Check the options** and choose the one that fits the situation and the grammar.
5. **Beware of the literal option:** JAMB often includes an option that explains the idiom word by word. It is almost always wrong.

**Example:**

> When the auditors arrived unannounced, the accountant knew the **game was up**.

Options: A. the football match had ended; B. his wrongdoing had been discovered; C. he would win; D. the auditors were playing.

**Answer: B.** "The game is up" means a secret plan or wrongdoing has been discovered and can no longer continue. A and D are literal traps.

## Part 5 (Objective iii): Grammatical functions of words

### Name vs function

JAMB asks two kinds of grammar question about expressions in a passage:

1. **The grammatical name** (what it **is**): noun, verb, noun phrase, adverbial clause of reason, etc.
2. **The grammatical function** (what job it **does**): subject of the verb, object of the verb, complement, qualifier of a noun, modifier of a verb, object of a preposition, etc.

| Name (class) | Function | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun | Subject of the verb | **Amina** won the prize. |
| Noun | Object of the verb | The teacher praised **Amina**. |
| Noun | Complement of the subject | Amina is the **captain**. |
| Noun | Object of a preposition | I sat beside **Amina**. |
| Adjective | Qualifier (modifier) of a noun | a **brilliant** student |
| Adjective | Complement of the subject | The student is **brilliant**. |
| Adverb | Modifier of a verb | She spoke **softly**. |
| Adverb | Modifier of an adjective | a **very** brilliant student |

**Notes on terms:** Nigerian textbooks often say an adjective **qualifies** a noun and an adverb **modifies** a verb, adjective or adverb. Many modern grammars use **modifier** for both. Both uses are found in JAMB options, so understand both.

### The main grammatical functions

| Function | What it does | Example (function shown in bold) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Subject** | Tells who or what does the action or is described; usually before the verb | **The rain** destroyed the bridge. |
| **Direct object** | Receives the action of a transitive verb (answers "what?" or "whom?" after the verb) | The rain destroyed **the bridge**. |
| **Indirect object** | Tells to whom or for whom something is done | She gave **her brother** a book. |
| **Subject complement** | Completes a linking verb (*be, become, seem, appear, look*) and describes the subject | Ngozi became **a lawyer**. The soup tastes **delicious**. |
| **Object complement** | Describes or renames the object | They elected him **chairman**. The news made her **sad**. |
| **Adjunct/adverbial** | Gives extra information about time, place, manner, reason, etc. | We met **in Kaduna** **last year**. |
| **Qualifier/modifier** | Describes a noun (adjectival function) or a verb/adjective/adverb (adverbial function) | the **red** car; ran **quickly** |
| **Object of a preposition** | Follows a preposition | under **the table** |
| **Apposition** | A noun or noun phrase that renames another noun next to it | Mr Bello, **our principal**, retired. |

### The same word, different classes and functions

A word's class depends on how it is used in the sentence. JAMB tests this often.

| Word | Sentence | Class | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *round* | The second **round** was difficult. | Noun | Subject (head of the noun phrase) |
| *round* | She drew a **round** shape. | Adjective | Qualifies "shape" |
| *round* | The children gathered **round** the fire. | Preposition | Introduces "the fire" |
| *round* | The wheel turned **round**. | Adverb | Modifies "turned" |
| *round* | The athletes **round** the final bend. | Verb | Predicator (main verb) |
| *swimming* | **Swimming** is good exercise. | Noun (gerund) | Subject |
| *swimming* | The **swimming** pool is closed. | Adjective (noun modifier) | Qualifies "pool" |
| *swimming* | The boys are **swimming**. | Verb (present participle) | Part of the verb phrase |
| *that* | **That** is my book. | Pronoun (demonstrative) | Subject |
| *that* | **That** book is mine. | Determiner/demonstrative adjective | Qualifies "book" |
| *that* | I know **that** he is honest. | Conjunction (subordinator) | Introduces a noun clause |
| *that* | The book **that** I bought is new. | Relative pronoun | Introduces an adjectival clause |

**Note on textbook variation:** some books call "that" in "that book" a **demonstrative adjective**; modern grammars call it a **determiner**. Some books call "swimming" in "swimming pool" an adjective; others call it a noun used as a modifier (a gerund). Read all the options: usually only one of the competing labels appears.

### How to identify the function of a word: step by step

1. **Find the main verb** of the clause.
2. **Ask "who or what + verb?"** → the answer is the **subject**.
3. **Ask "verb + what/whom?"** → the answer is the **object** (if the verb is transitive).
4. **Is the verb a linking verb (be, become, seem…)?** → what follows and describes the subject is the **complement**.
5. **Does the word describe a noun?** → it qualifies the noun (adjectival function).
6. **Does it tell how, when, where, why?** → it modifies the verb (adverbial function).
7. **Does it follow a preposition?** → object of the preposition.

## Part 6 (Objective iii): Grammatical names and functions of phrases

A **phrase** is a group of words **without a finite verb with its own subject**. It works as a single unit in a sentence.

### Kinds of phrases

| Phrase | Head word / structure | Example (phrase in bold) | Usual function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Noun phrase** | A noun (or pronoun) with its modifiers | **The tall man in the blue agbada** is my uncle. | Subject, object, complement |
| **Verb phrase** | Main verb with auxiliaries | She **has been reading** all day. | Predicator (the verb element) |
| **Adjectival phrase** | An adjective with its modifiers, or any phrase that describes a noun | The soup was **too hot to eat**. | Complement; qualifier of a noun |
| **Adverbial phrase** | An adverb with its modifiers, or any phrase that tells how, when, where, why | She spoke **very softly**. | Modifier of a verb |
| **Prepositional phrase** | A preposition + its object | The keys are **on the table**. | Adjectival or adverbial |
| **Infinitive phrase** | *to* + verb + other words | **To travel by road at night** is risky. | Subject, object, adverbial (purpose) |
| **Gerund phrase** | *-ing* verb used as a noun + other words | **Swimming in the lagoon** is dangerous. | Subject, object |
| **Participial phrase** | *-ing* or *-ed/-en* participle + other words, describing a noun | **Exhausted by the journey**, the traders slept. | Qualifies a noun (adjectival) |

### Prepositional phrases: adjectival or adverbial?

A prepositional phrase is named by its form, but JAMB often asks for its **function**:

| Sentence | Phrase | Function | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The man **in the blue agbada** is my uncle. | in the blue agbada | Adjectival (qualifies "man") | Answers "which man?" |
| The boys sat **under the mango tree**. | under the mango tree | Adverbial of place (modifies "sat") | Answers "where did they sit?" |
| She left **after the meeting**. | after the meeting | Adverbial of time | Answers "when?" |
| A house **with a red roof** stood alone. | with a red roof | Adjectival | Answers "which house?" |
| He solved the problem **with great care**. | with great care | Adverbial of manner | Answers "how?" |

**Note on textbook variation:** Nigerian school textbooks and JAMB options usually name phrases by their **function** ("adjectival phrase", "adverbial phrase of place"). Many modern grammars name them by their **head word** ("prepositional phrase") and then state the function separately. So "under the mango tree" may be called an **adverbial phrase of place** in one book and a **prepositional phrase functioning as an adverbial** in another. Read the options carefully and choose the one that matches the question's wording ("name" or "function").

### How to identify a phrase: step by step

1. **Check for a finite verb with its own subject.** If there is one, it is a **clause**, not a phrase.
2. **Find the head word:** noun → noun phrase; preposition → prepositional phrase; adverb → adverbial phrase; *to* + verb → infinitive phrase; *-ing* word as noun → gerund phrase; participle describing a noun → participial phrase.
3. **Ask what job the phrase does:** names something (noun function), describes a noun (adjectival), or tells how/when/where/why (adverbial).

## Part 7 (Objective iii): Grammatical names and functions of clauses

A **clause** is a group of words that contains a **subject and a finite verb**. The detailed patterns of clauses and sentences are taught in B3a and B3b; here we focus on naming clauses in a passage.

### Main and subordinate clauses

| | Main (independent) clause | Subordinate (dependent) clause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can stand alone? | Yes, it makes complete sense | No, it depends on a main clause |
| Introduced by | No subordinator needed | A subordinator: *that, who, which, when, because, if, although, where, whether, what* etc. |
| Example | **We stayed at home** because it rained. | We stayed at home **because it rained**. |

### The three kinds of subordinate clause

**1. Noun clauses** do the work of a noun.

| Function | Example (noun clause in bold) |
| --- | --- |
| Subject of a verb | **What he said** surprised everyone. |
| Object of a verb | I know **that she is honest**. |
| Subject complement | The problem is **that we have no money**. |
| Object of a preposition | She was worried about **what the doctor would say**. |
| In apposition to a noun | The fact **that he lied** upset us. |

Common introducers: *that, what, whatever, who, whoever, whether, if* (= whether), *how, why, when, where*.

**2. Adjectival (relative) clauses** do the work of an adjective: they **qualify a noun or pronoun**.

| Example (adjectival clause in bold) | Noun qualified |
| --- | --- |
| The boy **who won the prize** is my cousin. | boy |
| This is the house **where I was born**. | house |
| The book **(that/which) you lent me** was useful. | book |
| My mother, **who is a nurse**, works at night. | mother |

Introducers: *who, whom, whose, which, that, where, when, why* (and sometimes no introducer: "the book **you lent me**").

**Defining vs non-defining:** a defining clause tells us **which** one ("The students **who passed** will be admitted": only those students). A non-defining clause adds extra information and is set off by commas ("My mother, **who is a nurse**, works at night").

**3. Adverbial clauses** do the work of an adverb: they usually **modify the verb** of the main clause.

| Type | Answers | Introducers | Example (clause in bold) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time | When? | when, while, before, after, as soon as, until, since | **When the rain stopped**, we left. |
| Place | Where? | where, wherever | Sit **where you can see the board**. |
| Reason (cause) | Why? | because, since, as | She stayed home **because she was ill**. |
| Purpose | For what purpose? | so that, in order that, lest | He saved money **so that he could buy a phone**. |
| Result | With what result? | so… that, such… that | It rained **so heavily that the road flooded**. |
| Condition | On what condition? | if, unless, provided that | **If you study hard**, you will pass. |
| Concession (contrast) | In spite of what? | although, though, even though, whereas | **Although he was tired**, he finished the work. |
| Manner | How? | as, as if, as though | She spoke **as if she knew the answer**. |
| Comparison (degree) | How much? Compared with what? | than, as… as | Ada is taller **than her brother is**. |

**Note on textbook variation:** some books list adverbial clauses of **result** and **purpose** together, or call reason clauses "cause" clauses. Some treat comparison clauses separately from adverbial clauses. The meanings are the same.

### Words that introduce more than one kind of clause

| Introducer | Noun clause | Adjectival clause | Adverbial clause |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *when* | I don't know **when he will come**. (object) | That was the day **when we met**. (qualifies "day") | **When he comes**, call me. (time) |
| *where* | Tell me **where you live**. (object) | This is the town **where I grew up**. (qualifies "town") | Plant the tree **where there is sunlight**. (place) |
| *that* | She said **that she was tired**. (object) | The car **that he bought** is new. (qualifies "car") | He ran so fast **that he won**. (result, with *so*) |

**The test:** ask what job the clause does.
- Can you replace it with "it" or "something"? → **noun clause** ("I don't know **something**").
- Does it describe a noun just before it? → **adjectival clause**.
- Does it tell when, where, why, how or on what condition the main action happens? → **adverbial clause**.

### How to name a clause in a JAMB question: step by step

1. **Find the clause** (it has its own subject and finite verb).
2. **Identify the introducer.**
3. **Ask what job it does:**
   - Is it the subject, object or complement of something? → **noun clause** (then state the function).
   - Does it describe a noun? → **adjectival clause** qualifying that noun.
   - Does it answer when/where/why/how/on what condition? → **adverbial clause** (then state the type).
4. **Check the options:** JAMB often gives the right class with the wrong function, or the right function with the wrong class. Both parts must match.

### Worked example: grammatical names and functions in a passage

> **When the floodwaters rose**, the villagers carried their belongings to the school on the hill. The headmaster, **who had lived there for thirty years**, opened every classroom. **What they needed most** was clean water. **To help them**, a church in the next town sent tankers. The rescue workers, **soaked to the skin**, worked through the night.

| Expression | Grammatical name | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "When the floodwaters rose" | Adverbial clause of time | Modifies the verb "carried" |
| "who had lived there for thirty years" | Adjectival (relative) clause, non-defining | Qualifies "the headmaster" |
| "What they needed most" | Noun clause | Subject of the verb "was" |
| "clean water" | Noun phrase | Complement of the subject (after "was") |
| "To help them" | Infinitive phrase | Adverbial of purpose (modifies "sent") |
| "soaked to the skin" | Participial phrase (also an idiom meaning "completely wet") | Qualifies "the rescue workers" |
| "on the hill" | Prepositional phrase | Adjectival: qualifies "the school" |
| "through the night" | Prepositional phrase | Adverbial of time: modifies "worked" |

### Grammatical functions of figurative and idiomatic expressions

The syllabus also asks you to identify the grammatical function of **figurative and idiomatic** expressions. An idiom or figure of speech is still a phrase or clause in the sentence, so treat it in two steps:

1. **Meaning:** what does it mean in context?
2. **Grammar:** what kind of unit is it and what job does it do?

| Sentence | Expression | Meaning | Grammatical name and function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The new stadium has become **a white elephant**. | a white elephant | a costly thing with no benefit | Noun phrase; complement of the subject "stadium" |
| She finished the essay **at the eleventh hour**. | at the eleventh hour | at the last moment | Prepositional (adverbial) phrase of time; modifies "finished" |
| The students **burnt the midnight oil**. | burnt the midnight oil | studied late into the night | Verb phrase with its object; the predicate of the sentence |
| **Like a wounded lion**, the striker fought back. | like a wounded lion (simile) | fiercely | Adverbial phrase of manner; modifies "fought" |
| He has **a heart of gold**. | a heart of gold (metaphor) | a very kind nature | Noun phrase; object of "has" |

## Common traps in these questions

| Trap | Example | How to beat it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common meaning instead of contextual meaning | Choosing "examine" for *check* in "check inflation" | Use the substitution test |
| Literal meaning of an idiom | "Burn the midnight oil" = "light a lamp" | Ask what the whole expression means in the situation |
| Wrong word class in the option | Replacing an adjective with a noun option | The option must fit the grammar of the sentence |
| Simile/metaphor confusion | Calling "He is a lion in battle" a simile | No *like/as* → metaphor |
| "As" that is not a simile | "She worked as a nurse" | Similes compare unlike things |
| Name vs function | Answering "noun clause" when the question asks for the function ("object of the verb") | Read the question: name or function? |
| Clause vs phrase | Calling "after the meeting" a clause | No subject + finite verb → phrase |
| Introducer confusion | Assuming every *when*-clause is adverbial | Test the job the clause does |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Contextual meaning | The meaning a word has in a particular sentence or passage |
| Context clue | Information near a word that helps you work out its meaning |
| Connotation | The feeling or association a word carries in addition to its basic meaning |
| Figure of speech | A non-literal use of words for effect, e.g. simile, metaphor |
| Idiom | A fixed expression whose meaning cannot be worked out from its separate words |
| Phrase | A group of words without a subject and finite verb, acting as one unit |
| Clause | A group of words with a subject and a finite verb |
| Main clause | A clause that can stand alone as a sentence |
| Subordinate clause | A clause that depends on a main clause |
| Noun clause | A subordinate clause that functions as a noun (subject, object, complement, etc.) |
| Adjectival (relative) clause | A subordinate clause that qualifies a noun or pronoun |
| Adverbial clause | A subordinate clause that modifies a verb, telling time, place, reason, condition, etc. |
| Subject | The part of a clause that does the action or is described |
| Object | The part that receives the action of a transitive verb |
| Complement | The part that completes a linking verb and describes the subject (or describes the object) |
| Qualifier/modifier | A word or group of words that describes another |
| Apposition | A noun phrase placed beside another to rename it |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **"As used in the passage"** means the contextual meaning. Always go back to the sentence.
- **Use the substitution test:** the right option fits both the meaning and the grammar of the sentence.
- **The most common meaning is often the trap.** Think about the context first.
- **Idioms are never literal.** Cross out options that explain an idiom word by word.
- **Simile needs *like* or *as*; metaphor does not.** Not every "as" is a simile.
- **Read the grammar question carefully:** does it ask for the **name** (adverbial clause of time) or the **function** (modifies the verb)?
- **Phrase or clause?** Look for a subject plus a finite verb.
- **Noun clause test:** can you replace it with "it" or "something"?
- **Introducers like *when, where, that* can start different clause types.** Decide by function, not by the first word.
- **Labels differ between books** (qualifier/modifier, demonstrative adjective/determiner, adjectival phrase/prepositional phrase). Understand the idea and match it to the options given.

## Quick summary

1. JAMB tests the meaning of words, phrases, clauses, sentences, figures of speech and idioms **as used in the passage**, which is the contextual meaning.
2. Use the substitution test and context clues (definition, synonym, contrast, example, general sense, cause–effect, word parts) to work out unfamiliar words.
3. Figures of speech such as simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole, litotes, irony, oxymoron, paradox, metonymy and synecdoche must be named and explained in context.
4. Idioms have fixed, non-literal meanings; the literal option is almost always wrong.
5. Grammatical function means the job a word does: subject, object, complement, qualifier/modifier, object of a preposition or apposition.
6. Phrases (noun, verb, adjectival, adverbial, prepositional, infinitive, gerund, participial) have no subject and finite verb; clauses do.
7. Subordinate clauses are noun clauses (subject, object, complement, object of preposition, apposition), adjectival clauses (qualify nouns) and adverbial clauses (time, place, reason, purpose, result, condition, concession, manner, comparison).

## Practice questions

Read the passage below and answer questions 1 to 6.

> Before the market fire, Alhaji Garba's textile shop in Kano was **thriving**. Customers came from Niger and Chad to buy his cloth. Then, one dry night in January, the fire swept through the market **like a hungry beast**, and by morning his shop was a heap of ashes. Friends advised him to **throw in the towel** and retire to his village. Instead, he borrowed money, rented a small stall and started again. **What surprised everyone** was not that he rebuilt the business, but how quickly he did it. Within two years, the man **who had lost everything** owned two shops.

**1. The word "thriving", as used in the passage, means**

- A. growing slowly
- B. doing very well
- C. struggling
- D. newly opened

**Answer: B.** The next sentence shows customers came from other countries, so the shop was successful. "Thriving" means flourishing or doing very well.

**2. "like a hungry beast" is an example of**

- A. metaphor
- B. simile
- C. hyperbole
- D. litotes

**Answer: B.** The fire is compared to a hungry beast using "like".

**3. The expression "throw in the towel" means to**

- A. clean up after the fire
- B. give up
- C. start a new business
- D. borrow money

**Answer: B.** It is an idiom meaning to give up. Option A is a literal trap; the friends were advising him to retire.

**4. "What surprised everyone" is a**

- A. noun clause functioning as the subject of the verb "was"
- B. adjectival clause qualifying "everyone"
- C. adverbial clause of reason
- D. noun phrase functioning as an object

**Answer: A.** The clause has its own subject ("What") and verb ("surprised"), and it is the subject of "was". You can replace it with "Something": "Something was not that he rebuilt…".

**5. "who had lost everything" is an adjectival clause qualifying**

- A. everyone
- B. the business
- C. the man
- D. two shops

**Answer: C.** The relative clause comes directly after "the man" and tells us which man.

**6. In the sentence "Customers came from Niger and Chad to buy his cloth", the expression "to buy his cloth" functions as an**

- A. adverbial of purpose
- B. object of the verb "came"
- C. adjectival phrase qualifying "Chad"
- D. adverbial of place

**Answer: A.** It tells us **why** the customers came. "Came" is intransitive, so it takes no object.

**7. In "The harmattan wind whistled through the empty classrooms", the figure of speech is**

- A. personification
- B. oxymoron
- C. euphemism
- D. antithesis

**Answer: A.** Whistling is a human action given to the wind.

**8. In "The pen is mightier than the sword", "the pen" stands for**

- A. stationery
- B. writing and ideas
- C. teachers
- D. soldiers

**Answer: B.** This is metonymy: the pen, which is associated with writing, stands for written ideas, and the sword stands for military force.

**9. In "The keys are under the mat", the expression "under the mat" is a**

- A. noun clause
- B. prepositional phrase functioning adverbially
- C. adjectival clause
- D. verb phrase

**Answer: B.** It begins with the preposition "under", has no subject and finite verb, and tells **where** the keys are.

**10. "It was no mean achievement for a village girl to top the national examination." The expression "no mean achievement" is an example of**

- A. hyperbole
- B. litotes
- C. irony
- D. paradox

**Answer: B.** Litotes uses a negative to express a strong positive: "no mean achievement" means "a great achievement".
