# JAMB Use of English Notes: The Writer's Intention, Mood, Attitude and Opinion

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Comprehension and Summary. It covers **Objective iv:** deduce or infer the writers' intentions including mood, attitude to the subject matter and opinion.

These questions appear with the **comprehension passage (5 questions)** and the **summary passage (5 questions)**, and similar questions about characters' feelings and attitudes appear with the **reading text (5 questions)**.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Work out the writer's **intention** (purpose) in a whole passage, a paragraph or a single sentence.
2. Identify the **mood** of the writer, a character or the passage.
3. Identify the writer's **attitude** to the subject matter, using precise words such as *critical, sympathetic, sceptical, objective* and *ironic*.
4. Identify the writer's **opinion**, and tell it apart from facts and from other people's opinions that the writer reports.
5. Use language clues (word choice, modals, punctuation, figures of speech, irony) as evidence.
6. Avoid the traps in attitude and tone questions.

You will use the skills from A1 (types of passage), A2a (implied meanings) and A2b (meaning of words and figures of speech) throughout this file.

**About the passages in these notes:** every passage here is original, written for practice. The people, small communities and events in them are invented, even when a real state or city is named for a Nigerian setting. Do not treat them as real news or history.

## The key terms: intention, mood, tone, attitude and opinion

These words are related, and different books use them in slightly different ways. Learn the core idea of each.

| Term | Basic question | Example answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Intention (purpose)** | **Why** did the writer write this? | To warn parents about the dangers of drug abuse |
| **Mood** | What **feeling** does the writer (or a character, or the passage) show or create? | Gloomy, joyful, anxious, nostalgic |
| **Tone** | **How** does the writer sound? (the "voice") | Serious, humorous, angry, ironic, formal |
| **Attitude** | What does the writer **feel or think about the subject**? | Critical of the government; sympathetic to the farmers |
| **Opinion** | What does the writer **believe or judge** about the issue? | "The writer believes that examination malpractice should be punished severely." |

**Note on textbook variation:**

- In literature textbooks, **mood** usually means the **atmosphere or feeling a text creates in the reader**, while **tone** means **the writer's attitude** shown through the voice. Many Nigerian English textbooks and exam guides, however, use "mood" to mean the **writer's or a character's state of mind** (for example, "The writer's mood is one of regret"). JAMB questions may use either sense, so read the question carefully: "the mood **of the passage**", "the writer's **mood**" or "the **character's** mood".
- **Tone** and **attitude** are often used almost interchangeably in exam questions ("The writer's tone/attitude is…"). The answer options (critical, sarcastic, sympathetic…) are usually the same kinds of words.
- The syllabus objective names **intentions, mood, attitude** and **opinion**; tone is included here because it is the main way attitude and mood are shown.

## Part 1: The writer's intention (purpose)

### What it means

The writer's **intention** is the **reason** for writing. Ask: "What does the writer want the reader to know, feel, believe or do after reading?"

### Common purposes and their clues

| Purpose | What the writer wants | Clues in the passage | Usual passage type (A1) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| To **inform** | The reader to know facts | Facts, figures, neutral language | Exposition |
| To **explain** | The reader to understand how or why | Process and cause–effect words | Exposition |
| To **describe** | The reader to picture something | Sensory details, imagery | Description |
| To **narrate/entertain** | The reader to enjoy a story | Characters, events, dialogue, humour | Narration |
| To **persuade/convince** | The reader to agree | Reasons, evidence, "should", rhetorical questions | Argumentation/persuasion |
| To **warn** | The reader to avoid a danger | Consequences, "if… then", "beware", urgent tone | Exposition or persuasion |
| To **advise** | The reader to follow a course of action | "You should", "It is advisable", "Try to" | Exposition or persuasion |
| To **criticise** | The reader to see faults | Negative words, irony, listing failures | Argumentation |
| To **praise/commend** | The reader to admire someone or something | Positive words, achievements | Description or argumentation |
| To **ridicule/satirise** | The reader to laugh at foolishness | Exaggeration, irony, sarcasm | Often narrative or argumentative |
| To **appeal** | The reader to help or act | "Please", "We call on", emotional language | Persuasion |
| To **compare** | The reader to see likenesses and differences | "whereas, similarly, unlike" | Exposition |

### The purpose of a part of the passage

JAMB also asks: "The writer mentions X in order to…" or "The purpose of the second paragraph is to…". Common purposes of a part:

| Purpose of a part | Typical clue |
| --- | --- |
| To introduce the topic | First sentence or paragraph; a general statement or question |
| To give an example/illustration | "For instance", a specific case or story |
| To provide evidence | Facts, statistics, research |
| To present the opposing view | "Some people argue", "Critics say" |
| To contrast/qualify | "However", "But", "Yet" |
| To explain a cause or effect | "Because", "As a result" |
| To emphasise a point | Repetition, a short sentence, an exclamation |
| To conclude/summarise | "In conclusion", "Thus", final paragraph |
| To create suspense | Delayed information, short sentences in a narrative |

### How to find the writer's intention: step by step

1. **Identify the type of passage** (A1): describe, narrate, explain or argue?
2. **Read the first and last paragraphs closely.** The opening often states the subject; the ending often shows what the writer wants.
3. **Look for words that show a stand:** *should, must, it is time, we need*.
4. **Notice what the writer emphasises:** what is repeated, what gets the most space, what comes after "but".
5. **Ask: what change does the writer want in me?** Knowledge, understanding, a feeling, agreement or action?
6. **Choose the most precise option.** If the writer gives facts and then urges action, "to persuade" or "to warn" is more precise than "to inform".

### Worked example: intention

> Every year, many young people lose their savings to online "investment" schemes that promise to double their money in a month. The schemes look professional, with smart websites and testimonies from "happy customers". But no genuine business can pay such high returns so quickly. Early members are usually paid with money from new members, until the scheme collapses. Before you send money to anyone online, check whether the company is registered with the appropriate regulators, and ask yourself one question: if this is so profitable, why are they sharing it with strangers?

**Question:** The writer's main intention is to

- A. advertise an investment scheme
- B. warn readers against fraudulent investment schemes
- C. explain how websites are designed
- D. praise young people for saving money

**Answer: B.** The passage describes how the schemes work ("Early members are usually paid with money from new members") and ends with advice ("Before you send money… check…"). The clues are the warning tone, "But no genuine business can…", and the direct advice to "you".

**Accuracy note:** the scheme described (paying old members with new members' money) is how a **Ponzi scheme** works. In Nigeria, investment companies are regulated by bodies such as the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC); the passage says "the appropriate regulators" so it does not depend on naming one body.

## Part 2: Mood

### What mood means

**Mood** is the **emotional state or atmosphere**:

- the **writer's mood** (how the writer feels while writing);
- a **character's mood** (in a narrative or the reading text);
- the **mood of the passage** (the atmosphere it creates in the reader).

### Mood vocabulary you must know

**Positive moods**

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| jubilant / elated / exultant | very happy and excited, often after success |
| cheerful | happy and positive |
| optimistic / hopeful | expecting good things |
| contented / satisfied | quietly happy with what one has |
| relieved | freed from worry |
| grateful / appreciative | thankful |
| enthusiastic | full of eager interest |
| serene / tranquil / calm | peaceful |
| nostalgic | fondly remembering the past (sometimes with a little sadness) |

**Negative moods**

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| gloomy / sombre / melancholic | sad and dark |
| pessimistic | expecting bad things |
| despondent / dejected / downcast | very sad and without hope |
| anxious / apprehensive / uneasy | worried about what may happen |
| frustrated | annoyed because one cannot achieve something |
| indignant | angry at something unfair |
| bitter / resentful | angry and hurt about past unfair treatment |
| regretful / remorseful | sorry about something one did (remorseful = deeply guilty) |
| resigned | accepting something unpleasant because one cannot change it |
| fearful / terrified | afraid / extremely afraid |
| tense / suspenseful | full of nervous expectation |
| desperate | without hope, ready to do anything |

**Words that are often confused**

| Pair | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| regretful vs remorseful | Regret: sorry about something (not always one's fault). Remorse: deep guilt for a wrong one has done. |
| anxious vs eager | "Anxious" mainly means worried; "eager" means keen. (In everyday speech "anxious to" can also mean eager, but exam options usually use "anxious" for worry.) |
| resigned vs determined | Resigned: has stopped fighting. Determined: still fighting. |
| nostalgic vs regretful | Nostalgic: fond memories of the past. Regretful: sorry about the past. |
| indignant vs bitter | Indignant: angry now about an injustice. Bitter: long-lasting hurt and anger. |

### How mood is created

| Clue | Example | Mood suggested |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word choice (adjectives, verbs) | "The grey clouds hung low over the silent village." | Gloomy, heavy |
| Setting and weather | Bright sunshine, singing birds | Cheerful |
| Characters' actions | "She paced up and down, biting her nails." | Anxious |
| Sentence length | Short, sharp sentences: "Silence. A shot. Then screaming." | Tense, frightening |
| Sounds | Laughter, drumming vs wailing, silence | Joyful vs sorrowful |
| Figurative language | "Hope died in his heart." | Despondent |
| Punctuation | Exclamation marks; ellipses (…) | Excitement; hesitation or sadness |

### How to identify mood: step by step

1. **Underline (mentally) the feeling words** and the descriptive words.
2. **Look at actions and reactions** of the writer or characters.
3. **Notice the setting:** weather, light, sound.
4. **Decide whether the mood is positive, negative or mixed.**
5. **Choose the most precise word.** "Sad" vs "nostalgic": if the sadness is about fond memories of the past, "nostalgic" is more precise.
6. **Check whose mood the question asks about:** the writer's, a character's, or the passage's.

### Worked example: mood

> The old compound is quiet now. Grass grows where we once played football in the evenings, and the mango tree my grandfather planted has lost half its branches. I can still hear Mama's voice calling us in for dinner, and I can almost smell the egusi soup bubbling on the firewood. My brothers live in Lagos and London now. We speak on the phone, but it is not the same. Sometimes I think I would give anything to sit under that tree with them one more time.

**Question:** The writer's mood is best described as

- A. jubilant
- B. nostalgic
- C. indignant
- D. indifferent

**Answer: B.** The writer remembers childhood fondly ("I can still hear Mama's voice", "I can almost smell the egusi soup") and longs to return to the past ("I would give anything…"). There is sadness, but it is the sadness of fond memory, which is nostalgia. A is too happy; C (anger at injustice) has no support; D contradicts the strong feeling.

## Part 3: Attitude to the subject matter

### What attitude means

The writer's **attitude** is how the writer **feels about or judges** the subject: a person, a group, an idea, an event or a policy. It can be **positive**, **negative**, **neutral** or **mixed**.

### Attitude vocabulary you must know

| Attitude | Meaning | Typical clues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Objective / neutral / detached** | Shows no personal feelings; presents facts fairly | Facts, figures, both sides, no emotive words |
| **Subjective** | Personal feelings and views shown | "I", emotive words, judgements |
| **Approving / appreciative / admiring** | Thinks well of the subject | Praise words: *remarkable, commendable, impressive* |
| **Laudatory** | Full of praise | Strong praise, often a list of achievements |
| **Sympathetic** | Shares or understands someone's suffering | *unfortunate, struggling, deserve help* |
| **Critical / disapproving** | Points out faults | *failure, careless, unacceptable, should have* |
| **Condemnatory** | Strongly criticises; declares something wrong | *shameful, disgraceful, must be stopped* |
| **Hostile** | Very unfriendly; strongly against | Aggressive, insulting language |
| **Sceptical / doubtful** | Not easily convinced; questions claims | *so-called, claims, allegedly, remains to be seen* |
| **Cynical** | Believes people act only from selfish motives | "As usual, the promises will be forgotten after the election." |
| **Ironic** | Says one thing but means another | Praise that clearly means criticism |
| **Sarcastic** | Mocking, bitter irony meant to hurt | "What a brilliant idea, to build a school on a flood plain!" |
| **Satirical** | Uses humour and exaggeration to criticise | Absurd examples, mock praise |
| **Humorous** | Amusing, light-hearted | Jokes, funny situations |
| **Ambivalent** | Has mixed feelings | "On one hand… on the other…", no final verdict |
| **Indifferent / apathetic** | Does not care | "Whatever", "It makes no difference" |
| **Condescending / patronising** | Talks down to others as if they are inferior | "Even simple villagers can understand this." |
| **Contemptuous / scornful** | Shows that something is worthless | *pathetic, laughable, beneath notice* |
| **Concerned / worried** | Anxious about a problem | *alarming, worrying, dangerous trend* |
| **Optimistic / hopeful** | Expects good outcomes | *will improve, bright future* |
| **Pessimistic** | Expects bad outcomes | *bound to fail, will only get worse* |
| **Respectful / reverent** | Shows deep respect | Formal titles, gentle language |
| **Nostalgic** | Fond of the past | Memories, "those days" |

### Degrees of attitude: choosing the precise word

Attitude words have different **strengths**. JAMB often includes a correct option and a stronger or weaker one.

| Weak → strong (negative) | Weak → strong (positive) |
| --- | --- |
| doubtful → sceptical → cynical | approving → admiring → laudatory |
| concerned → critical → condemnatory → hostile | sympathetic → supportive → enthusiastic |
| disapproving → indignant → furious | satisfied → pleased → jubilant |

**Rule:** choose the word that matches the **strength** of the language. A writer who says a policy "has some weaknesses that should be corrected" is **critical**, not **hostile**.

### Positive, negative or neutral? The connotation test

Look at the words the writer chooses (connotation, see A2b):

| Neutral | Positive | Negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The governor **said** | The governor **assured** | The governor **claimed** |
| The traders **gathered** | The traders **assembled peacefully** | The traders **swarmed** |
| The policy is **new** | The policy is **innovative** | The policy is **untested** |
| He **changed** his mind | He **reconsidered** | He **made a U-turn** |

"Claimed" suggests the writer doubts what was said (sceptical). "Assured" suggests trust.

### Language clues to attitude

| Clue | Example | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emotive adjectives | "a **shameful** waste of public funds" | Condemnation |
| Modal verbs | "The government **should** have acted" | Criticism (something was not done) |
| Hedging words | "It **may** perhaps help" | Caution, doubt |
| Intensifiers | "**utterly** unacceptable" | Strong feeling |
| Inverted commas (scare quotes) | The "reforms" have made things worse. | Sarcasm or scepticism: the writer doubts they are real reforms |
| "So-called" | the so-called experts | Contempt or scepticism |
| Rhetorical questions | "How long must the people suffer?" | Frustration, criticism |
| Irony | "Our roads are so smooth that cars swim through the potholes." | Criticism through mockery |
| Exclamation | "What a tragedy!" | Strong emotion |
| Balanced structures | "Although… nevertheless…" | Fairness, balance |
| Absence of feeling | Facts only, passive voice | Objectivity |

### Irony and sarcasm: when the words mean the opposite

Irony is a favourite JAMB trap. On the surface the writer praises; underneath, the writer criticises.

> Our council has done wonderfully this year. It took only eleven months to fill one pothole on Market Road, and the new streetlights work beautifully, especially in daylight.

- Literal meaning: the council has done well.
- Real meaning: the council has done very badly (eleven months for one pothole; streetlights that "work" only when not needed).
- Attitude: **ironic/sarcastic, critical**.

**How to spot irony:** the praise does not match the facts given. If the evidence is negative but the words are positive, think irony.

### How to identify attitude: step by step

1. **Identify the subject** the question asks about (the government? the youths? the new policy?). The writer's attitude may differ for different subjects in the same passage.
2. **Collect the words** the writer uses about that subject. Are they positive, negative or neutral?
3. **Check for irony**: do the words match the facts?
4. **Judge the strength** of the feeling.
5. **Eliminate options**: wrong direction (positive/negative), wrong strength (too strong/too weak), or wrong subject.
6. **Choose the most precise word** supported by evidence.

### Worked example: attitude

> The new law requiring motorcyclists to wear helmets is a sensible one. Head injuries from okada accidents fill hospital wards across the country, and a helmet is a cheap way to save lives. However, the way the law is being enforced leaves much to be desired. In some places, officials seize motorcycles and demand large sums before releasing them, while no effort is made to make affordable helmets available. A law meant to protect riders has become, for some officials, a business.

**Question 1:** The writer's attitude to the helmet law itself is

- A. hostile
- B. approving
- C. indifferent
- D. sarcastic

**Answer: B.** "A sensible one" and "a cheap way to save lives".

**Question 2:** The writer's attitude to the way the law is enforced is

- A. approving
- B. critical
- C. objective
- D. nostalgic

**Answer: B.** "Leaves much to be desired" (a polite but clear criticism), "demand large sums", "has become… a business".

**Lesson:** the writer's attitude **differs** for two subjects in the same passage. Always check what the question is asking about.

## Part 4: The writer's opinion

### What an opinion is

An **opinion** is a belief or judgement that cannot be proved true or false in the way a fact can. The writer's opinion is what **the writer** thinks about the issue. (Fact vs opinion is introduced in A1.)

### Signals of the writer's opinion

- **Opinion verbs and phrases:** *I believe, I think, in my view, it seems to me, I am convinced that*.
- **Judgement words:** *good, bad, best, worst, wise, foolish, important, unfortunate, necessary*.
- **Modals of obligation or advice:** *should, must, ought to, need to*.
- **Evaluative adverbs:** *clearly, sadly, fortunately, surprisingly, unfortunately, rightly, wrongly*.
- **Conclusions** in the last paragraph of an argument.

### The writer's opinion vs other people's opinions

Writers often **report** other people's opinions, sometimes to agree with them and sometimes to reject them. This is a big JAMB trap.

> Many parents believe that children should not study science unless they want to become doctors. This view is understandable, but it is mistaken. Science teaches young people to observe, question and solve problems, skills that are useful in every career.

| Statement | Whose opinion? |
| --- | --- |
| Children should study science only if they want to become doctors | **The parents'** (the writer rejects it) |
| This view is understandable but mistaken | **The writer's** |
| Science teaches useful skills for every career | **The writer's** |

**How to separate them:** look for reporting phrases (*many believe, some argue, critics claim, it is often said, according to*). What follows is someone else's opinion. Then look for the writer's reaction (*but, however, this is true, this is mistaken*).

### Questions on opinion

- "The writer believes that…"
- "Which of the following is the writer's opinion?"
- "The writer would most likely agree/disagree that…"
- "Which of the following statements is a fact, not an opinion?"
- "The writer's view on X is that…"

### How to answer "would most likely agree with" questions: step by step

1. **State the writer's main position in your own words.**
2. **Check each option** against that position. Would the writer accept it, given what the passage says?
3. **Reject options that go too far**, even if they are in the same direction ("Science should be the only subject taught" goes beyond "science is useful for every career").
4. **Reject options that state the reported view the writer rejects.**

## Part 5: Putting it all together

### Worked example: a full passage

> At the end of every term, schools across the country hold prize-giving days. Parents dress in their best clothes, the principal makes a long speech, and the "best students" walk proudly to the stage to collect dictionaries and certificates.
>
> Nobody would deny that hard work deserves praise. But I sometimes wonder what happens to the other students, the ones who sit in the hall clapping, year after year, without ever being called. Many of them are talented footballers, artists, mechanics and singers, but our prize lists rarely have space for such gifts. They leave the hall convinced that they are failures.
>
> Surely a school's duty is to discover every child's talent, not only the talent that shows up in examination scores. Until our prize-giving days reflect that duty, they will continue to celebrate a few and quietly discourage the many.

**1. The writer's intention is mainly to**

- A. describe a prize-giving ceremony
- B. argue that schools should recognise a wider range of talents
- C. criticise parents for dressing well
- D. praise the best students

**Answer: B.** The first paragraph describes, but the second and third paragraphs argue ("Surely a school's duty is to discover every child's talent"). The description serves the argument.

**2. The quotation marks around "best students" suggest that the writer**

- A. admires these students greatly
- B. questions the way "best" is decided
- C. does not know who the students are
- D. is quoting the principal's exact words

**Answer: B.** Scare quotes signal doubt about the term: the writer believes "best" is measured too narrowly. D is possible in general, but nothing in the passage supports it, and the rest of the passage shows the writer's doubt.

**3. The writer's attitude to students who never win prizes is**

- A. contemptuous
- B. sympathetic
- C. indifferent
- D. sarcastic

**Answer: B.** The writer worries that they "leave the hall convinced that they are failures" and points out their talents.

**4. The writer's mood in the second paragraph is best described as**

- A. jubilant
- B. reflective and concerned
- C. furious
- D. resigned

**Answer: B.** "I sometimes wonder" shows reflection; the concern is for the students who feel like failures. "Furious" is too strong; "resigned" is wrong because the writer calls for change.

**5. Which of the following is the writer's opinion?**

- A. Schools hold prize-giving days at the end of every term.
- B. Parents dress in their best clothes.
- C. A school's duty is to discover every child's talent.
- D. Dictionaries and certificates are given as prizes.

**Answer: C.** It is a judgement introduced by "Surely". The others are descriptive statements that could be checked.

## Common traps in intention, mood and attitude questions

| Trap | Example | How to avoid it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confusing a character's feeling with the writer's attitude | A character is angry, but the writer is amused by the character | Check whose feeling the question asks about |
| Taking irony literally | "The council has done wonderfully" read as praise | Compare the words with the facts |
| Choosing an option that is too strong | "hostile" for a mildly critical passage | Match the strength of the language |
| Choosing "neutral" for an argumentative passage | The writer clearly takes a side | Neutral/objective only when no stand is taken |
| Confusing a reported opinion with the writer's opinion | "Some people argue…" taken as the writer's view | Look for the writer's reaction after the reported view |
| Choosing the purpose of one paragraph for the whole passage | "To describe" for a passage that describes and then argues | Purpose of the whole = dominant purpose |
| Choosing a positive/negative word in the wrong direction | "Optimistic" for "things will only get worse" | Decide positive/negative/neutral first |
| Ignoring the subject of the attitude | Attitude to the law vs attitude to its enforcement | Identify exactly what the attitude is towards |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Intention (purpose) | The reason the writer wrote the passage or a part of it |
| Mood | The emotional state of the writer or a character, or the atmosphere a passage creates |
| Tone | The writer's "voice": how the writer sounds (serious, humorous, ironic, angry) |
| Attitude | The writer's feelings or judgement about the subject |
| Opinion | A belief or judgement that cannot be proved like a fact |
| Objective | Not influenced by personal feelings; factual |
| Subjective | Based on personal feelings and opinions |
| Sceptical | Doubtful; not easily convinced |
| Cynical | Believing people act only out of selfishness |
| Ironic | Saying one thing but meaning the opposite |
| Sarcastic | Using mocking irony to hurt or criticise |
| Ambivalent | Having mixed feelings |
| Nostalgic | Fondly remembering the past |
| Connotation | The positive or negative feeling a word carries |
| Scare quotes | Quotation marks used to show doubt about a word, e.g. the "reforms" |
| Emotive language | Words chosen to stir feelings |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Start with the type of passage:** narration usually aims to entertain or inform, exposition to inform or explain, argument to persuade.
- **Look at the end of the passage:** the writer's conclusion often reveals the intention and opinion.
- **Decide positive, negative or neutral first,** then choose the precise word.
- **Match the strength:** critical is not hostile; approving is not laudatory.
- **Watch for irony:** if praise does not fit the facts, the writer is being ironic.
- **"Objective" means no personal feelings.** An argumentative writer is not objective.
- **Separate the writer's opinion from reported opinions** (*some people believe, critics claim*).
- **Check the subject of the question:** the writer's attitude to one thing may differ from the attitude to another in the same passage.
- **Know your mood and attitude vocabulary:** nostalgic, sceptical, cynical, ambivalent, indignant, resigned, sombre and similar words appear often as options.
- **"Mood" may mean the writer's feeling or the passage's atmosphere.** Read the question carefully.

## Quick summary

1. The writer's intention is the reason for writing: to inform, explain, describe, entertain, persuade, warn, advise, criticise, praise, ridicule or appeal.
2. The purpose of a part of a passage may be to introduce, illustrate, give evidence, present the opposing view, contrast, emphasise or conclude.
3. Mood is the emotional state of the writer or a character, or the atmosphere of the passage; it is shown through word choice, setting, actions, sentence length and figurative language.
4. Attitude is the writer's feeling or judgement about the subject: positive, negative, neutral or mixed. Choose the word with the right direction and strength.
5. Irony and sarcasm mean the opposite of the literal words; spot them when praise does not match the facts.
6. The writer's opinion is shown by opinion phrases, judgement words, modals and conclusions; it must be separated from opinions the writer reports.
7. Books use "mood", "tone" and "attitude" in overlapping ways; focus on the evidence in the passage.

## Practice questions

Read the passage below and answer questions 1 to 5.

> Last month, our local government chairman launched a "massive" tree-planting campaign. Television cameras filmed him planting a single seedling outside the council secretariat, and the next day the newspapers praised his "commitment to the environment". Today, that seedling is the only tree that was planted. It is, I must admit, a very healthy tree.
>
> Meanwhile, the forests on the edge of town are disappearing. Every week, lorries carry away logs, and the land is left bare. Farmers complain that the streams dry up earlier every year. If our leaders are serious about the environment, they must protect the forests we already have, not merely pose for photographs with seedlings.

**1. The writer's main intention is to**

- A. praise the chairman's tree-planting campaign
- B. criticise the chairman's campaign as mere show while forests are destroyed
- C. explain how to plant trees
- D. describe the council secretariat

**Answer: B.** The writer points out that only one tree was planted for the cameras and ends by saying leaders "must protect the forests… not merely pose for photographs".

**2. The quotation marks around "massive" show that the writer is being**

- A. objective
- B. ironic
- C. respectful
- D. optimistic

**Answer: B.** The campaign produced only one tree, so calling it "massive" is ironic. The quotation marks show the writer does not accept the word.

**3. "It is, I must admit, a very healthy tree." The tone of this sentence is**

- A. admiring
- B. sarcastic
- C. sympathetic
- D. fearful

**Answer: B.** The writer pretends to praise the single tree in order to mock the campaign's failure.

**4. The writer's attitude to the destruction of the forests is one of**

- A. indifference
- B. concern
- C. approval
- D. amusement

**Answer: B.** The writer describes bare land and drying streams and calls on leaders to protect the forests.

**5. Which of the following is the writer's opinion?**

- A. Lorries carry away logs every week.
- B. Television cameras filmed the chairman.
- C. Leaders must protect existing forests if they are serious about the environment.
- D. The chairman planted a seedling outside the secretariat.

**Answer: C.** It uses "must" and states what the writer believes leaders should do. The others are statements of what happened.

**6. A writer who presents facts about a disease without showing any personal feelings is**

- A. subjective
- B. objective
- C. sarcastic
- D. sentimental

**Answer: B.** Objective writing is factual and free from personal feelings.

**7. "Sadly, the once-busy railway station now stands empty, its benches covered in dust, its clock stopped at twenty past three." The mood of the sentence is**

- A. jubilant
- B. melancholic
- C. humorous
- D. indignant

**Answer: B.** "Sadly", "once-busy", "stands empty", "covered in dust" and the stopped clock create a sad, gloomy atmosphere.

**8. "Some critics claim that the new curriculum is too difficult. They are wrong." The writer's view of the new curriculum is that it is**

- A. too difficult
- B. not too difficult
- C. unnecessary
- D. too easy

**Answer: B.** The critics' view is reported and then rejected ("They are wrong"). D goes further than the writer does.

**9. A writer who says, "The plan has some merits, but it also has serious weaknesses, and I am not yet sure whether it will work," is best described as**

- A. hostile
- B. enthusiastic
- C. ambivalent
- D. contemptuous

**Answer: C.** The writer has mixed feelings: some merits, serious weaknesses and uncertainty.

**10. "The so-called experts have once again predicted an end to fuel scarcity by Christmas." The writer's attitude to the experts is**

- A. admiring
- B. sceptical
- C. neutral
- D. sympathetic

**Answer: B.** "So-called" and "once again" show that the writer doubts the experts, whose earlier predictions apparently failed.
