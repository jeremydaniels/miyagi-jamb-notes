# JAMB Use of English Notes: Antonyms

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Lexis and Structure, content item **(b) antonyms**. It also draws on content item (g), **ordinary usage, figurative usage and idiomatic usage**, because many antonym questions test a word or phrase used in a special sense.

It covers these objectives:

- **Objective ii:** determine similar and opposite meanings of words. These notes deal with the **opposite** meanings. Similar meanings are in `B1-synonyms.md`.
- **Objective i:** identify words and expressions in their ordinary, figurative and idiomatic contexts. In antonym questions, you must first work out what the word means **in that sentence** before you can find its opposite.

**How many questions?** The exam structure gives **5 questions** on antonyms in Section B. That is 5 marks out of 60, so every one is worth getting right.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Explain what an antonym is and recognise the main types of antonyms.
2. Use prefixes and suffixes to recognise opposite words, and avoid the traps they set.
3. Find the opposite of a word **as it is used in the sentence**, not just its dictionary opposite.
4. Find the opposite of a phrase or an idiom.
5. Follow a step-by-step method to answer JAMB antonym questions and avoid the common traps.

**A note on how words are marked:** in the real JAMB paper, the tested word is usually **underlined** or printed in *italics*. In these notes, the tested word or phrase is always printed in *italics*.

## What is an antonym?

An **antonym** is a word that is **opposite in meaning** to another word.

| Word | Antonym |
| --- | --- |
| rich | poor |
| early | late |
| arrive | depart |
| generous | stingy (or mean) |
| victory | defeat |
| ancient | modern |
| increase | decrease |
| accept | reject (or refuse) |

The word comes from Greek: *anti* means "against" or "opposite", and *onyma* means "name". So an antonym is an "opposite name".

**Important:** a word can have more than one antonym, and different meanings of the same word have different antonyms.

- *light* (not heavy) → antonym: **heavy**. "This bag of garri is light."
- *light* (not dark) → antonym: **dark**. "The room is light and airy."
- *light* (not serious) → antonym: **serious** or **grave**. "They had a light conversation after the meeting."

This is the most important idea in this whole topic. JAMB almost always tests the word **in a sentence**, and the sentence tells you which meaning is being used.

## The JAMB antonym question format

The instruction in the exam paper looks like this:

> In each of questions X to Y, choose the option **opposite in meaning** to the word(s) or phrase in italics (or underlined).

Then you get a sentence:

> **Example:** The manager's reply was *curt*.
>
> - A. rude
> - B. short
> - C. polite
> - D. brief

*Curt* means short and rude (said quickly, with no politeness). The opposite is **polite** (C). Options A, B and D are all **synonyms** or near-synonyms of *curt*. This is the most common JAMB trap: the examiners fill the options with synonyms to catch students who forget that the question asks for the **opposite**.

### What JAMB tests in antonyms

From the syllabus and the style of past questions, antonym questions test:

1. **Single words** in ordinary use: *scarce* → plentiful.
2. **Words used in a special or figurative sense:** "His *cold* reply" → warm, friendly.
3. **Phrases and phrasal verbs:** "The meeting was *called off*" → held (or went ahead).
4. **Idioms:** "She was *on cloud nine*" → very unhappy.
5. **Words whose opposite is formed with a prefix or suffix:** *legal* → illegal; *careful* → careless.

## Types of antonyms

Grammar and vocabulary books group antonyms in slightly different ways and use different names. The three groups below are the ones found in most books on English meaning. **Not every Nigerian textbook uses these names, and JAMB does not ask you to name the type.** But knowing the types helps you see why one option is a better opposite than another.

### 1. Gradable antonyms

Gradable antonyms are opposites at the two ends of a **scale**. There are degrees in between.

| Word | Middle of the scale | Antonym |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hot | warm, lukewarm, cool | cold |
| big | medium-sized | small |
| old | middle-aged | young |
| fast | moderate | slow |
| happy | content, so-so | sad |

**Test:** you can say "very hot", "hotter", "quite cold". If you can use *very*, *more* or *-er* with a word, its antonym is usually gradable.

**Why it matters in JAMB:** with gradable antonyms, look for the option at the **other end** of the scale, not an option in the middle.

> **Example:** The water in the pot was *scalding*.
>
> - A. warm
> - B. boiling
> - C. lukewarm
> - D. icy

*Scalding* means extremely hot (hot enough to burn skin). The opposite end of the scale is **icy** (D), extremely cold. *Warm* and *lukewarm* are in the middle. *Boiling* is a synonym.

### 2. Complementary (non-gradable) antonyms

Complementary antonyms are pairs where there is **no middle ground**. If one is true, the other must be false.

| Word | Antonym |
| --- | --- |
| dead | alive |
| true | false |
| present | absent |
| pass (an exam) | fail |
| married | single (unmarried) |
| guilty | innocent (not guilty) |

You cannot be "a little bit dead" or "very present". (People sometimes say these things in jokes, but in normal meaning there is no middle.)

### 3. Relational (converse) antonyms

Relational antonyms describe the **same relationship from two sides**. One cannot exist without the other.

| Word | Antonym |
| --- | --- |
| buy | sell |
| teacher | student (pupil) |
| doctor | patient |
| parent | child |
| employer | employee |
| lend | borrow |
| give | receive (or take) |
| above | below |
| husband | wife |

If Ada **sells** a phone to Musa, then Musa **buys** the phone from Ada. It is the same event seen from two sides.

**Some books** add other groups, such as **reversive** antonyms (actions that undo each other: *tie / untie*, *enter / exit*, *arrive / depart*, *build / demolish*). Others put these under relational antonyms. Either way, they are still opposites.

## Forming antonyms with prefixes and suffixes

Many English antonyms are made by adding a **prefix** (letters at the beginning) or by changing a **suffix** (letters at the end). Knowing these helps with antonym questions and also with spelling (objective iii).

### Negative prefixes

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| un- | not | happy → unhappy; able → unable; fair → unfair; known → unknown |
| in- | not | active → inactive; complete → incomplete; correct → incorrect; sane → insane |
| im- (before b, m, p) | not | possible → impossible; mature → immature; balance → imbalance; polite → impolite |
| il- (before l) | not | legal → illegal; literate → illiterate; logical → illogical; legible → illegible |
| ir- (before r) | not | regular → irregular; relevant → irrelevant; responsible → irresponsible; rational → irrational |
| dis- | not / opposite of | honest → dishonest; agree → disagree; obey → disobey; appear → disappear |
| mis- | wrongly | understand → misunderstand; behave → misbehave; lead → mislead |
| non- | not | violent → non-violent; existent → non-existent; fiction → non-fiction |
| a- (an- before a vowel) | without, not | moral → amoral; typical → atypical; symmetrical → asymmetrical; (an)aerobic |
| anti- | against | social → antisocial; climax → anticlimax |
| de- | reverse / remove | activate → deactivate; centralise → decentralise; motivate → demotivate |

**Spelling rule to remember:** when you add *il-*, *im-* or *ir-*, keep both letters: **i**ll**egal** (double l), **imm**ature (double m), **irr**egular (double r). Many students write "ilegal" or "iregular". These are wrong.

**There is no rule that tells you which prefix a word takes.** You say *unhappy*, not "inhappy", and *inactive*, not "unactive". You must learn them by reading and by using a good dictionary.

### Suffix pairs

| Suffix | Opposite suffix | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -ful (with, full of) | -less (without) | careful → careless; hopeful → hopeless; useful → useless; harmful → harmless |
| -er/-or (the one who does) | -ee (the one who receives) | employer → employee; trainer → trainee; payer → payee; interviewer → interviewee |

**Watch out:** not every *-ful* word has a *-less* partner. *Beautiful* does not become "beautiless". *Priceless* does not mean "without value" (see the traps below).

### Prefix traps: words that LOOK like opposites but are not

These are favourite JAMB traps. Learn them well.

| Word | What it really means | Common wrong idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **inflammable** | easily set on fire. It means the **same** as *flammable* | "not flammable". The real opposite of both is **non-flammable** |
| **invaluable** | extremely valuable, too valuable to measure | "not valuable". The opposite of *valuable* is **worthless** or **valueless** |
| **priceless** | so valuable that no price can be put on it | "having no price, cheap" |
| **infamous** | well known for something bad | "not famous". The opposite of *famous* is **unknown** or **obscure** |
| **disinterested** | not taking sides; impartial, neutral | "not interested". In careful formal English, "not interested" is **uninterested**. (Many people now use *disinterested* to mean "not interested" too, and some dictionaries accept this. But in exams, keep the formal difference.) |
| **inhabitable** | fit to live in; the same as *habitable* | "not habitable". The opposite is **uninhabitable** |
| **indifferent** | not caring, not interested | "the same, not different" |

> **Example:** The old manuscripts in the National Museum, Lagos, are *invaluable*.
>
> - A. precious
> - B. worthless
> - C. expensive
> - D. priceless

*Invaluable* means extremely valuable. Its opposite is **worthless** (B). A student who thinks "in- means not" may look for "valuable", but *valuable* is not even an option. *Precious* and *priceless* are synonyms.

## Words with more than one meaning: context decides the antonym

This is where most marks are lost. Always read the **whole sentence** and decide what the word means **there**.

| Sentence | Meaning of the word here | Antonym |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The road to Jos is *steep*. | sharply sloping | gentle, gradual |
| Garri prices are *steep* this month. | very high (cost) | low, cheap, reasonable |
| She gave a *fair* judgement. | just, unbiased | unfair, biased |
| The weather is *fair* today. | bright, not rainy | stormy, foul |
| He has *fair* skin. | light in colour | dark |
| The result was only *fair*. | average, not very good | excellent |
| The soup is too *rich* for me. | heavy, full of oil/fat | light, plain |
| Chief Okafor is very *rich*. | wealthy | poor |
| His *hard* work paid off. | done with great effort | lazy, half-hearted |
| The yam is *hard*. | firm, not soft | soft |
| The question was *hard*. | difficult | easy |
| She *raised* her hand. | lifted up | lowered |
| They *raised* prices. | increased | reduced, lowered |
| The light is *on*. | working, switched on | off |

### Worked example: same word, two questions

> **(a)** The new governor took a *firm* stand against corruption.
>
> - A. strong
> - B. weak
> - C. hard
> - D. steady

Here *firm* means strong and determined. The opposite is **weak** (B). Also accept "wavering" or "half-hearted" if they appear.

> **(b)** Press the dough until it is *firm*.
>
> - A. soft
> - B. solid
> - C. weak
> - D. heavy

Here *firm* means solid, not soft to touch. The opposite is **soft** (A). *Weak* would be wrong here, even though it was right in (a).

## Antonyms of words used figuratively

When a word is used **figuratively** (not in its literal meaning), its antonym must also match the figurative meaning.

| Sentence | Figurative meaning | Antonym |
| --- | --- | --- |
| She gave the visitors a *warm* welcome. | friendly, enthusiastic | cold, hostile, unfriendly |
| He has a *heart of stone*. | is cruel, without pity | is kind-hearted, compassionate |
| The plan is *crystal clear*. | very easy to understand | confusing, obscure, vague |
| The class was *a beehive of activity*. | very busy | idle, quiet, inactive |
| Her *bright* idea saved the day. | clever | foolish, stupid |
| The company's profits *soared*. | rose very quickly | fell sharply, plummeted |
| He gave a *dry* speech. | dull, boring | lively, interesting |

> **Example:** The minister's explanation *threw light on* the matter.
>
> - A. clarified
> - B. confused
> - C. illuminated
> - D. explained

To *throw light on* something means to make it clearer. The opposite is **confused** (B). A, C and D are all synonyms.

## Antonyms of phrases, phrasal verbs and idioms

JAMB often puts a whole phrase in italics. Treat the phrase as one unit of meaning. First translate it into a simple word, then find the opposite of that simple word.

### Phrasal verbs

| Phrasal verb (in a sentence) | Meaning | Antonym |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The match was *called off*. | cancelled | held, went ahead |
| He *gave in* to their demands. | surrendered, yielded | resisted, held out |
| Prices have *gone up*. | increased | gone down, fallen |
| She *turned down* the offer. | refused | accepted |
| The fire *died down*. | became weaker | flared up, grew stronger |
| They *set up* a new school in Kano. | established | closed down, dissolved |
| He *showed up* early. | arrived | was absent, failed to appear |
| The doctor *played down* the danger. | made it seem less serious | exaggerated, played up |
| The thief *gave himself up*. | surrendered | escaped, resisted arrest |

### Idioms

The syllabus note says: "Idioms to be tested are those that are formal and expressed in Standard British English (SBE)." So learn formal, common British idioms. Fuller idiom notes are in `B7b-idiomatic-usage.md`.

| Idiom | Meaning | Opposite meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *on cloud nine* | extremely happy | very sad, depressed |
| *a wet blanket* | a person who spoils others' fun | a lively, cheerful person |
| *in the black* (business) | making a profit | in the red (making a loss) |
| *a feather in one's cap* | an achievement to be proud of | a disgrace, an embarrassment |
| *at loggerheads* | quarrelling, in strong disagreement | in agreement, in harmony |
| *let the cat out of the bag* | revealed a secret | kept the secret |
| *burn the midnight oil* | work or study late into the night | be idle, relax |
| *a dark horse* | a person whose abilities are not known, who surprises people | a well-known quantity, a predictable person |
| *by leaps and bounds* | very quickly (progress) | slowly, gradually |
| *see eye to eye* | agree completely | disagree |
| *keep a low profile* | avoid attention | seek attention, be in the limelight |
| *a stone's throw* | a very short distance | a long way off |

> **Worked example:** The two brothers have been *at daggers drawn* for years.
>
> - A. at war
> - B. on friendly terms
> - C. very angry
> - D. in court

*At daggers drawn* means very hostile towards each other, ready to fight. The opposite is **on friendly terms** (B). A and C are close in meaning to the idiom. D is a possible result of the quarrel, not its opposite.

## How to answer JAMB antonym questions: step by step

**Step 1. Read the instruction.** Remind yourself: "I want the OPPOSITE." Some students write a small "OPP" beside the question block in their rough work to stop themselves choosing a synonym.

**Step 2. Read the whole sentence.** Don't look at the options yet.

**Step 3. Decide what the word means in this sentence.** Put a simple word or phrase in its place. For *The chairman's speech was* ***verbose***, you think: "long-winded, using too many words".

**Step 4. Think of your own opposite before you look at the options.** Opposite of "using too many words" = "short and to the point", i.e. *concise*, *brief*, *succinct*.

**Step 5. Look at the options and remove the synonyms first.** There are usually one or two synonyms in the options. Cross them out.

**Step 6. Remove options that are unrelated.** Some options are neither similar nor opposite. They may be "linked" words (for example, *speech* → "loud"), but they are not opposites.

**Step 7. Choose the option that is the opposite in the same sense and the same word class.** If the italic word is an adjective, the answer should work as an adjective in the same sentence.

**Step 8. Test it by putting the answer back into the sentence.** "The chairman's speech was *concise*." Does the sentence now mean the opposite? Yes. Choose it.

### Full worked example

> The new principal is known to be *lenient* with latecomers.
>
> - A. kind
> - B. strict
> - C. careless
> - D. friendly

- Step 3: *Lenient* means not strict; easy-going with punishment.
- Step 4: Opposite: strict, harsh, severe.
- Step 5: Remove synonyms: A (*kind*) and D (*friendly*) are close to *lenient*.
- Step 6: C (*careless*) is unrelated: a principal can be careless whether or not they are lenient.
- Step 7 and 8: "The new principal is known to be *strict* with latecomers." This is the opposite. **Answer: B.**

### Full worked example with an idiom

> After the election, the losing candidate decided to *bury the hatchet* with his rival.
>
> - A. make peace
> - B. continue the quarrel
> - C. dig a grave
> - D. sue

- *Bury the hatchet* means to stop quarrelling and make peace.
- A is a synonym. C is the literal meaning (a trap for students who take idioms word for word). D is unrelated.
- The opposite is **continue the quarrel** (B).

## Same word class: the answer must fit the grammar

An antonym should be the **same part of speech** as the tested word, so it fits into the same place in the sentence.

| Tested word | Wrong option (different word class) | Right option |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *generous* (adjective) | meanness (noun) | mean / stingy (adjective) |
| *succeed* (verb) | failure (noun) | fail (verb) |
| *happily* (adverb) | sad (adjective) | sadly / unhappily (adverb) |
| *victory* (noun) | lose (verb) | defeat (noun) |

If two options have similar opposite meanings, the one that fits the grammar of the sentence is usually the answer.

## Common JAMB traps in antonym questions

1. **The synonym trap.** Two or three options mean the same as the tested word. These are there to catch students who forget to look for the opposite.
2. **The literal-meaning trap.** For idioms and figurative uses, one option is the literal meaning (e.g. "dig a grave" for *bury the hatchet*). It is never the answer.
3. **The "related but not opposite" trap.** For *The patient was* ***conscious***, an option like "sleepy" is related but the real opposite is *unconscious* or *insensible*.
4. **The wrong-meaning trap.** The option is the opposite of a **different** meaning of the word. For "*fair* weather", the option *unjust* is the opposite of another meaning of *fair*. The answer is *stormy* or *foul*.
5. **The middle-of-the-scale trap.** For gradable words like *scalding* or *ecstatic*, an option in the middle (*warm*, *content*) is not the true opposite.
6. **The false-prefix trap.** *Inflammable*, *invaluable*, *infamous* and *disinterested* do not mean what their prefixes suggest.
7. **The word-class trap.** An option with the right "idea" but the wrong part of speech (e.g. a noun for an adjective).
8. **The degree trap.** For a strong word, choose a strong opposite. The opposite of *adore* (love very much) is *detest* or *loathe*, not just "dislike", if both are offered.

## Nigerian English pitfalls related to antonyms

Some words are used in Nigerian English with meanings that differ from Standard British English. JAMB tests Standard British English, so be careful:

| Nigerian usage | Standard British English | Effect on antonyms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "He is very *stubborn*" (sometimes used for a playful, naughty child) | *Stubborn* means unwilling to change one's mind | Antonym in SBE: *flexible*, *compliant*, *amenable* |
| "*Borrow* me your pen" | *Lend* me your pen | *borrow* and *lend* are relational opposites, not the same |
| "I'm *coming*" (meaning "I'm going out and will return") | "I'll be back" / "I'm going" | *come* and *go* are opposites |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Antonym | A word opposite in meaning to another word, e.g. *ancient* / *modern* |
| Synonym | A word similar in meaning to another word, e.g. *ancient* / *old* |
| Gradable antonyms | Opposites at two ends of a scale with degrees in between, e.g. *hot* / *cold* |
| Complementary antonyms | Opposites with no middle ground, e.g. *dead* / *alive* |
| Relational (converse) antonyms | Opposites describing the same relationship from two sides, e.g. *buy* / *sell* |
| Prefix | Letters added to the beginning of a word to change its meaning, e.g. *un-* in *unhappy* |
| Suffix | Letters added to the end of a word, e.g. *-less* in *careless* |
| Context | The words and sentence around a word, which show which meaning is being used |
| Figurative usage | Using a word in a non-literal, imaginative sense, e.g. a *warm* welcome |
| Idiom | A fixed expression whose meaning cannot be worked out from its individual words, e.g. *bury the hatchet* |
| Phrasal verb | A verb plus a particle (adverb or preposition) with its own meaning, e.g. *call off* |
| Standard British English (SBE) | The formal British variety of English that JAMB tests |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Opposite, not similar.** The biggest mistake in this section is choosing a synonym. Remind yourself of the instruction before every question.
- **Meaning in the sentence first.** Work out what the word means **in that sentence** before you look at the options.
- **Think of your own answer first.** Then look for it, or something close to it, in the options.
- **Cross out synonyms** before you choose. With five antonym questions, careful elimination saves marks.
- **Idioms are not literal.** *Kick the bucket* is about dying, not about buckets. The literal option is a trap.
- **Prefixes can lie.** *Inflammable* = *flammable*. *Invaluable* = extremely valuable. *Infamous* = famous for bad things.
- **Match the word class and the degree.** A strong word needs a strong opposite of the same part of speech.
- **Spelling of negative prefixes:** *illegal*, *immature*, *irregular*, *unnecessary* (one *c*, double *s*), *dissatisfied* (double *s*).
- **Formal British English:** *lend* and *borrow* are opposites. Don't use them as the same word.

## Quick summary

1. An antonym is a word or phrase opposite in meaning to another; JAMB sets 5 antonym questions in Section B.
2. Many words have several meanings, so the sentence decides which antonym is correct.
3. The main types are gradable (*hot/cold*), complementary (*dead/alive*) and relational (*buy/sell*) antonyms; books use slightly different names.
4. Prefixes (*un-, in-, im-, il-, ir-, dis-, mis-, non-*) and suffix pairs (*-ful/-less*, *-er/-ee*) often form opposites, but *inflammable*, *invaluable*, *priceless* and *infamous* are traps.
5. For phrases, phrasal verbs and idioms, translate the expression into a simple word first, then find its opposite.
6. Method: read the sentence, find the meaning in context, think of your own opposite, cross out synonyms and unrelated options, check the word class, and test the answer in the sentence.

## Practice questions

In each question, choose the option **opposite in meaning** to the word or phrase in *italics*.

**1. The witness gave a *candid* account of what happened at the motor park.**

- A. frank
- B. evasive
- C. honest
- D. detailed

**Answer: B.** *Candid* means open and honest. *Evasive* (avoiding giving direct answers) is the opposite. A and C are synonyms; D is unrelated.

**2. Water is *scarce* in many parts of northern Nigeria during the dry season.**

- A. rare
- B. expensive
- C. plentiful
- D. clean

**Answer: C.** *Scarce* means in short supply. *Plentiful* means available in large amounts. *Rare* is a synonym.

**3. The village head was *jubilant* when the new borehole was commissioned.**

- A. excited
- B. dejected
- C. surprised
- D. relieved

**Answer: B.** *Jubilant* means extremely happy, rejoicing. *Dejected* means sad and in low spirits. A is close to a synonym; C and D are unrelated.

**4. The council decided to *call off* the strike.**

- A. postpone
- B. cancel
- C. continue
- D. discuss

**Answer: C.** To *call off* a strike is to end or cancel it. The opposite is to *continue* it. B is a synonym. *Postpone* means move to a later date, which is not the opposite.

**5. The manager has a reputation for being *frugal* with company funds.**

- A. careful
- B. economical
- C. extravagant
- D. honest

**Answer: C.** *Frugal* means careful not to waste money. *Extravagant* means spending too much, wastefully. A and B are synonyms.

**6. The doctor's words *threw cold water on* our hopes.**

- A. encouraged
- B. discouraged
- C. destroyed
- D. ignored

**Answer: A.** To *throw cold water on* something is to discourage it or show no enthusiasm for it. The opposite is *encouraged*. B and C are close to synonyms; D is unrelated.

**7. The lecturer's explanation was *lucid*.**

- A. clear
- B. long
- C. confusing
- D. brief

**Answer: C.** *Lucid* means clear and easy to understand. *Confusing* is the opposite. A is a synonym; B and D describe length, not clarity.

**8. The old documents in the archive are *invaluable* to historians.**

- A. priceless
- B. worthless
- C. useful
- D. expensive

**Answer: B.** *Invaluable* means extremely valuable, not "not valuable". The opposite is *worthless*. A is a synonym; C and D are related but not opposites.

**9. The two communities have been *at loggerheads* over the farmland.**

- A. in agreement
- B. in conflict
- C. in court
- D. in mourning

**Answer: A.** *At loggerheads* means in strong disagreement. The opposite is *in agreement*. B is a synonym; C and D are unrelated.

**10. The prices of foodstuffs in Bodija Market are rather *steep* this month.**

- A. gentle
- B. high
- C. reasonable
- D. rising

**Answer: C.** Here *steep* means very high (in cost), so the opposite is *reasonable* (fair, not too high). *Gentle* is the opposite of *steep* only when describing a slope, which is a different meaning. B is a synonym; D is related.
