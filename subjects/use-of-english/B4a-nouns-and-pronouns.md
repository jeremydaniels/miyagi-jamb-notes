## Word classes: an introduction

**Word classes** (also called **parts of speech**) are groups of words that behave in the same way in sentences.

### How many word classes are there?

Books differ here, and you should know why.

- **Traditional grammar** (used in many older school books) lists **eight parts of speech**: noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction and interjection.
- Many **modern grammars** add **determiners** (*the, a, this, some, every, my*) as a separate class, instead of calling them adjectives. Some also separate **auxiliary verbs** from main verbs, and some treat **numerals** as their own class. This gives nine or more classes.
- Some books call **articles** (*a, an, the*) a separate part of speech.

JAMB questions don't ask "how many word classes are there?". They ask you to identify a word's class or use a word correctly. Knowing both systems helps you when an option says "determiner" or "article".

### Open and closed classes

| Open classes (new words are added all the time) | Closed classes (fixed; new words are rarely added) |
| --- | --- |
| Nouns (*selfie, okada*), verbs (*google, text*), adjectives, adverbs | Pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions, determiners, auxiliary verbs |

### A word's class depends on how it is used

The same word can belong to different classes in different sentences. This is a common JAMB question.

| Sentence | Word | Class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The *round* table is broken. | round | adjective |
| We danced *round* the fire. | round | preposition |
| Our team lost in the second *round*. | round | noun |
| The cows *round* the corner slowly. | round | verb |
| Come *round* tomorrow. | round | adverb |
| *Fast* food is unhealthy. | fast | adjective |
| He runs *fast*. | fast | adverb |
| Muslims *fast* during Ramadan. | fast | verb |
| She broke her *fast* at sunset. | fast | noun |

**Rule:** never decide a word's class from the word alone. Look at its **job** in the sentence.

## Nouns

### What is a noun?

A **noun** is a word that names a person, place, thing, animal, idea, quality or feeling.

- Person: *teacher, Chinua, farmer, nurse*
- Place: *Kaduna, market, school, Africa*
- Thing: *phone, mortar, cutlass*
- Animal: *goat, lion*
- Idea, quality or feeling: *freedom, honesty, joy, anger*

**Clues that a word is a noun:**

- It can follow *a, an, the, this, my, some*: *the* **market**, *my* **phone**.
- It can usually be made plural: *market → markets*.
- It can be the subject or object of a verb.
- Many nouns end in suffixes such as *-ness, -ment, -tion, -sion, -ity, -ance, -ence, -ship, -hood, -dom, -er, -or, -ist*: *kindness, government, education, ability, importance, friendship, childhood, freedom, teacher, actor, scientist*.

### Kinds of nouns

#### 1. Proper nouns

A **proper noun** names a **particular** person, place, organisation, day, month or event. It always begins with a **capital letter**.

- People: *Wole Soyinka, Chimamanda, Musa*
- Places: *Lagos, River Niger, Zuma Rock, Nigeria*
- Organisations: *JAMB, the University of Ibadan, NAFDAC*
- Days, months and festivals: *Monday, December, Christmas, Eid-el-Fitr, Durbar*

#### 2. Common nouns

A **common noun** names **any** member of a group, not a particular one. It does not begin with a capital letter (unless it starts a sentence).

- *city, river, boy, school, month, festival*

| Common noun | Proper noun |
| --- | --- |
| city | Abuja |
| river | Benue |
| university | Ahmadu Bello University |
| novel | *Things Fall Apart* |

#### 3. Concrete nouns

A **concrete noun** names something you can see, touch, hear, smell or taste.

- *table, rain, music, perfume, pepper soup*

#### 4. Abstract nouns

An **abstract noun** names an idea, quality, state or feeling that you cannot touch.

- *honesty, love, poverty, childhood, knowledge, bravery, wisdom, justice*

Many abstract nouns are formed from adjectives and verbs:

| Adjective / verb | Abstract noun |
| --- | --- |
| brave | bravery |
| wise | wisdom |
| free | freedom |
| happy | happiness |
| able | ability |
| poor | poverty |
| child (noun) | childhood |
| know | knowledge |
| decide | decision |
| grow | growth |
| believe | belief |
| succeed | success |

#### 5. Collective nouns

A **collective noun** names a **group** of people, animals or things as one unit.

| Collective noun | Group |
| --- | --- |
| a **team** of players | people |
| a **committee**, **jury**, **panel** | people |
| a **congregation** of worshippers | people |
| an **audience** at a play | people |
| a **crew** of sailors | people |
| a **herd** of cattle | animals |
| a **flock** of sheep / birds | animals |
| a **swarm** of bees / locusts | insects |
| a **pride** of lions | animals |
| a **school** (or **shoal**) of fish | animals |
| a **bunch** of keys / bananas | things |
| a **fleet** of ships / cars | things |
| a **pack** of cards / wolves | things / animals |

**Singular or plural verb?** In British English, a collective noun can take a **singular** verb when you think of the group as one unit, or a **plural** verb when you think of the members as individuals:

- *The committee **has** made its decision.* (one unit)
- *The committee **are** arguing among themselves.* (individual members)

American English usually prefers the singular. Some exam guides insist on the singular in all cases. **In JAMB, the safe guide is: the whole sentence must be consistent.** If the verb is singular, the pronoun must be singular (*its*); if plural, the pronoun must be plural (*their*).

- ✗ *The team **has** won **their** first match.* (mixed)
- ✓ *The team **has** won **its** first match.*
- ✓ *The team **have** won **their** first match.* (British usage)

More in `B5b-number-and-concord.md`.

**Nouns like *police*, *cattle*, *people*, *clergy*, *vermin*** are always plural, even without *-s*:

- ✓ *The police **are** investigating the case.* ✗ *The police is ...*
- ✓ *The cattle **are** grazing.* ✗ *The cattles ...*
- ✓ *Many people **were** there.* (*peoples* is used only to mean different nations or ethnic groups: *the peoples of West Africa*)

#### 6. Countable and uncountable nouns

This is one of the most tested noun areas in JAMB, and a common area of error in Nigerian English.

- A **countable noun** can be counted: it has a singular and plural form and can take *a/an* and numbers. *one chair, two chairs; a book, many books*.
- An **uncountable (mass) noun** cannot be counted: it has no plural form in normal use and does not take *a/an*. *water, rice, sand, information, advice*.

| | Countable | Uncountable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plural form | Yes: *chairs* | No: ✗ *furnitures* |
| *a / an* | Yes: *a chair* | No: ✗ *a furniture* |
| Numbers | *three chairs* | ✗ *three furnitures* → ✓ *three pieces of furniture* |
| *many / few / a few / fewer / number of* | Yes: *many chairs, fewer chairs* | No |
| *much / little / a little / less / amount of* | No | Yes: *much furniture, less furniture* |
| *some / any / a lot of / lots of / plenty of* | Yes | Yes |

**Uncountable nouns that Nigerian speakers often make plural (JAMB favourites):**

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| furnitures | furniture / pieces of furniture |
| informations | information / pieces of information |
| equipments | equipment / pieces of equipment |
| luggages, baggages | luggage, baggage / pieces of luggage |
| advices | advice / pieces of advice |
| jewelleries | jewellery / pieces of jewellery |
| stationeries | stationery (writing materials) |
| machineries | machinery / machines |
| sceneries | scenery |
| offsprings | offspring (same form for singular and plural) |
| aircrafts | aircraft (same form for singular and plural) |
| slangs | slang / slang expressions |
| mails (for letters) | mail / letters / emails |
| evidences | evidence / pieces of evidence |
| feedbacks | feedback |
| behaviours (for one person's conduct) | behaviour |
| knowledges | knowledge |
| researches (as "studies") | research / studies (note: *researches* exists in some uses, but most exam guides treat *research* as uncountable) |

**Words that can be countable in one meaning and uncountable in another:**

| Uncountable | Countable |
| --- | --- |
| *Glass* breaks easily. (the material) | I need two *glasses*. (drinking glasses) / He wears *glasses*. (spectacles) |
| *Paper* is made from wood. | I bought three *papers*. (newspapers) / He presented a *paper*. (an article) |
| She has grey *hair*. | There are two *hairs* in my soup. (single strands) |
| *Experience* is the best teacher. | I had a strange *experience*. (an event) |
| We need more *room*. (space) | The house has five *rooms*. |
| *Work* is important. | The *works* of Achebe (books); The steel *works* (factory) |
| *Iron* is a metal. | an *iron* for clothes |

**To count an uncountable noun, use a "unit" word:**

- *a piece of* advice / information / furniture / news / luggage / equipment
- *an item of* news / clothing
- *a bag of* rice / cement
- *a loaf of* bread
- *a bottle of* water
- *a cup of* tea
- *a grain of* rice / sand
- *a bar of* soap
- *a sheet of* paper
- *a word of* advice

#### 7. Compound nouns

A **compound noun** is made of two or more words that together name one thing.

- Written as one word: *classroom, toothbrush, headmaster, bookshop*
- Written with a hyphen: *mother-in-law, passer-by, editor-in-chief*
- Written as separate words: *bus stop, swimming pool, post office*

**Note:** spelling (one word, hyphen or separate) differs between dictionaries and has changed over time (for example, *e-mail* / *email*). Use one form consistently, and check a British dictionary when in doubt.

**Plurals of compound nouns:** usually the **main (most important) word** takes the plural:

| Singular | Plural | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mother-in-law | **mothers**-in-law | *mother* is the main word |
| son-in-law | **sons**-in-law | |
| passer-by | **passers**-by | |
| commander-in-chief | **commanders**-in-chief | |
| editor-in-chief | **editors**-in-chief | |
| attorney general | **attorneys** general (also *attorney generals*; both are found in dictionaries) | |
| court martial | **courts** martial (also *court martials*) | |
| man-of-war | **men**-of-war | |
| looker-on | **lookers**-on | |
| runner-up | **runners**-up | |
| spoonful | spoonful**s** (also *spoonsful*, less common) | one word: plural at the end |
| handful | handful**s** | |
| bus stop | bus stop**s** | last word is the main word |
| manservant | **men**servants | both change |
| woman doctor | **women** doctors | both change |

## Forming plurals: spelling rules

### Regular plurals

| Rule | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Most nouns: add **-s** | book → books; girl → girls |
| Nouns ending in **-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -z**: add **-es** | bus → buses; class → classes; dish → dishes; church → churches; box → boxes; quiz → quizzes |
| Consonant + **-y**: change *y* to *i*, add **-es** | lady → ladies; city → cities; country → countries |
| Vowel + **-y**: just add **-s** | boy → boys; day → days; key → keys; valley → valleys; monkey → monkeys |
| Proper names ending in -y: just add -s | the Kennedys; two Marys |

### Nouns ending in -o

| Add **-es** | Add **-s** | Either (both accepted) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| potato → potatoes | photo → photos | mango → mangoes / mangos |
| tomato → tomatoes | piano → pianos | mosquito → mosquitoes / mosquitos |
| hero → heroes | radio → radios | zero → zeros / zeroes |
| echo → echoes | kilo → kilos | volcano → volcanoes / volcanos |
| veto → vetoes | studio → studios | cargo → cargoes / cargos |
| torpedo → torpedoes | video → videos | buffalo → buffaloes / buffalo / buffalos |
| | kangaroo → kangaroos | |
| | zoo → zoos | |

**Note:** dictionaries differ on some *-o* words. *Potatoes*, *tomatoes*, *heroes* and *echoes* are always *-es*. *Photos*, *pianos*, *radios* and *videos* are always *-s*.

**Common errors:**

- ✗ *This criteria is important.* → ✓ *This **criterion** is important.* / *These **criteria** are important.*
- ✗ *A strange phenomena* → ✓ *a strange **phenomenon***
- ✗ *The bacteria is harmful.* → ✓ *The bacteria **are** harmful.* / *The **bacterium** is harmful.*
- **Data:** traditionally plural (*The data **are** ready*). In modern use, *data* is often treated as uncountable and singular (*The data **is** ready*). Both are found in good dictionaries. In very formal or scientific writing, the plural is preferred.
- **Media:** *The media **are** ...* is traditional; *the media **is** ...* is now common. Both are seen.

### Nouns that are always plural

These nouns have **no singular form** in the meaning given and take a **plural verb**:

- Things in pairs: *scissors, trousers, shorts, pants, jeans, spectacles, glasses, pliers, tongs, binoculars*
  - ✓ *My trousers **are** torn.* ✓ *A pair of trousers **is** on the bed.* (Here the subject is *pair*.)
- Others: *goods, outskirts, premises* (building and land), *proceeds, surroundings, belongings, thanks, congratulations, riches, earnings, arms* (weapons), *clothes, savings, valuables*
  - ✓ *The proceeds **were** given to charity.*
  - ✓ *The goods **have** arrived.* ✗ *The good has arrived.*

### Nouns that end in -s but are singular

- Subjects: *mathematics, physics, economics, statistics* (as a subject), *politics* (often), *linguistics, phonetics*
- Diseases: *measles, mumps, rickets, diabetes*
- Others: *news, innings* (cricket), *billiards, athletics*
  - ✓ *The news **is** good.* ✗ *The news are good.*
  - ✓ *Mathematics **is** my best subject.*
  - ✓ *Measles **is** a serious disease.*

**Note:** *statistics* is plural when it means "figures": *These statistics **are** wrong.* *Politics* can be plural when it means someone's views: *His politics **are** extreme.*

### Plural forms of letters, numbers and abbreviations

- *Mind your p's and q's.* (apostrophe used for single lowercase letters to avoid confusion)
- *in the 1990s* (modern British style; *1990's* is considered old-fashioned or wrong by most style guides)
- *two MPs, three CDs* (modern style; no apostrophe)

## Functions of nouns

A noun (or noun phrase) can do several jobs in a sentence. JAMB asks "The word in italics functions as ...".

| Function | Example (noun in italics) | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject of a verb | *Emeka* plays football. | Who plays? |
| Direct object of a verb | Emeka kicked *the ball*. | Kicked what? |
| Indirect object of a verb | Emeka gave *his brother* the ball. | Gave to whom? |
| Subject complement | Emeka is *a footballer*. | Emeka = a footballer |
| Object complement | They made Emeka *captain*. | Emeka = captain |
| Object of a preposition | Emeka plays for *his school*. | Comes after *for* |
| Apposition | Emeka, *the captain*, scored twice. | Renames Emeka, next to it |
| Modifier (noun used like an adjective) | the *school* bus; a *gold* ring | Describes another noun |
| Adverbial (some noun phrases) | We travelled *last week*. | Tells when |
| Vocative (person addressed) | *Emeka*, come here! | Calls someone |

### Nouns used as modifiers (noun adjuncts)

When a noun describes another noun, it is usually **singular**:

- ✓ *a ten-year-old boy* ✗ *a ten-years-old boy*
- ✓ *a five-kilometre race* ✗ *a five-kilometres race*
- ✓ *a two-week holiday* ✗ *a two-weeks holiday*
- ✓ *a shoe shop* (not *shoes shop*)
- ✓ *a ₦500 note*

But: *The boy is ten **years** old.* (Here *years* is not a modifier before a noun.)

**Exceptions** where the plural form is kept: *sports* car, *arms* race, *savings* account, *clothes* shop, *sales* manager.

## The possessive case (apostrophe)

The possessive shows **ownership** or a close relationship.

### Rules for the apostrophe

| Rule | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Singular noun: add **'s** | the boy**'s** bag (one boy); Ngozi**'s** phone |
| Plural noun ending in **-s**: add only **'** | the boy**s'** bags (many boys); the teacher**s'** room |
| Plural noun **not** ending in -s: add **'s** | the children**'s** toys; the men**'s** hostel; women**'s** rights; people**'s** opinions |
| Singular noun ending in **-s** | James**'s** car or James**'** car; the bus**'s** engine (see note) |
| Joint ownership: apostrophe on the **last** name only | Tunde and Kemi**'s** house (they share one house) |
| Separate ownership: apostrophe on **each** name | Tunde**'s** and Kemi**'s** houses (two houses) |
| Compound nouns: apostrophe at the **end** | my mother-in-law**'s** shop; the commander-in-chief**'s** order |
| Time expressions | a week**'s** holiday; two week**s'** notice; today**'s** newspaper |

**Note on names ending in -s:** style guides differ. Most modern British guides prefer adding **'s** (*James's*, *Charles's*, *Moses's*), but writing just the apostrophe (*James'*, *Moses'*) is also widely accepted, especially for classical and biblical names (*Jesus'*, *Moses'*). Be consistent.

### Possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe

- ✓ *hers, ours, yours, theirs, its*
- ✗ *her's, our's, your's, their's, it's* (as possessive)

**its vs it's:**

- *its* = belonging to it: *The dog wagged **its** tail.*
- *it's* = it is / it has: ***It's** raining.* ***It's** been a long day.*

This is one of the most common spelling and punctuation errors in English.

### "Of" or "'s"?

- For people and animals, *'s* is normal: *the teacher's car*, *the dog's tail*.
- For things, *of* is more usual: *the leg **of** the table* (rather than *the table's leg*), *the roof **of** the house*.
- With time, places and organisations, *'s* is common: *Nigeria's economy*, *the company's profits*, *today's lesson*.

### The double possessive

*A friend of my father's* and *a friend of my father* are both correct. *A friend of mine* (not *a friend of me*) is correct.

### "One of" + plural noun

**One of** is always followed by a **plural** noun:

- ✓ *one of my **friends*** ✗ *one of my friend*
- ✓ *one of the **students*** ✗ *one of the student*

And the verb agrees with *one* (singular): ✓ *One of my friends **is** a doctor.*

## Pronouns

### What is a pronoun?

A **pronoun** is a word used **in place of a noun** (or noun phrase), to avoid repeating it.

- *Bisi* is my friend. *Bisi* lives in Osogbo. *Bisi* is a nurse.
- *Bisi* is my friend. ***She*** lives in Osogbo. ***She*** is a nurse.

The noun a pronoun refers to is called its **antecedent**. Here, *Bisi* is the antecedent of *she*.

### Kinds of pronouns

#### 1. Personal pronouns

Personal pronouns stand for people or things. They change form according to **person**, **number**, **gender** and **case** (subject or object).

| Person | Subject form | Object form | Possessive determiner | Possessive pronoun | Reflexive/emphatic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st singular | I | me | my | mine | myself |
| 2nd singular | you | you | your | yours | yourself |
| 3rd singular (male) | he | him | his | his | himself |
| 3rd singular (female) | she | her | her | hers | herself |
| 3rd singular (thing) | it | it | its | (its, rare) | itself |
| 1st plural | we | us | our | ours | ourselves |
| 2nd plural | you | you | your | yours | yourselves |
| 3rd plural | they | them | their | theirs | themselves |

**Note on "possessive determiner":** words like *my, your, his, her, its, our, their* come before a noun (*my book*). Traditional grammar calls them **possessive adjectives**; modern grammar calls them **possessive determiners**. Words like *mine, yours, hers, ours, theirs* stand alone (*The book is mine*) and are **possessive pronouns**.

#### 2. Subject case and object case: the most tested pronoun area

- Use the **subject form** (*I, he, she, we, they, who*) when the pronoun is the **subject** of a verb.
- Use the **object form** (*me, him, her, us, them, whom*) when the pronoun is the **object** of a verb or **after a preposition**.

**Common errors and corrections:**

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Between you and I, he is lying. | Between you and **me**, he is lying. | *between* is a preposition → object form |
| Me and my friend went to the market. | My friend and **I** went to the market. | subject of *went* → *I*; politeness puts *I* last |
| The principal invited my wife and I. | The principal invited my wife and **me**. | object of *invited* → *me* (test: "invited me") |
| Let you and I go. | Let you and **me** go. | *let* takes an object → *me* |
| Us students are tired. | **We** students are tired. | subject → *we* (test: "We are tired") |
| They gave the prize to we winners. | They gave the prize to **us** winners. | after *to* → *us* |
| Myself and my wife thank you. | My wife and **I** thank you. | reflexive pronouns can't be subjects on their own |
| Please call either John or myself. | Please call either John or **me**. | *myself* is only reflexive/emphatic |

**Test for pairs:** remove the other person and say the pronoun alone. "The principal invited ~~my wife and~~ **I**" → "invited I"? Wrong. "invited **me**" → right.

#### After "than" and "as"

- *She is taller than **I** (am).* — formal; the verb *am* is understood.
- *She is taller than **me**.* — common in everyday English and accepted by many modern dictionaries and usage guides.

In formal exams, the **subject form** is the safer choice when a verb is understood. Note how the choice can change meaning:

- *I like him more than **she**.* = I like him more than **she likes him**.
- *I like him more than **her**.* = I like him more than **I like her**.

#### After "be"

- *It is **I**.* — traditional formal English (subject form after *be*).
- *It's **me**.* — normal everyday English.

Grammar books differ: traditional books insist on *It is I*; modern ones accept *It's me* as standard in speech. In a formal JAMB question, if both appear, the subject form (*It is I / It was he who ...*) is usually the expected answer.

#### 3. Reflexive and emphatic pronouns

The same *-self/-selves* forms are used in two ways:

- **Reflexive:** the object is the same person as the subject. *She hurt **herself**.* *We enjoyed **ourselves**.*
- **Emphatic (intensive):** adds emphasis; it can be removed. *The governor **himself** opened the hospital.* *I did it **myself**.*

**Errors:**

- ✗ *hisself, theirselves, theirself, ourself* (except in formal royal or editorial use), *themself* (see note below)
- ✓ *himself, themselves, ourselves*
- ✗ *He enjoyed.* (in Standard English, *enjoy* needs an object) → ✓ *He enjoyed **himself**.* / *He enjoyed the party.*
- ✗ *We met ourselves at the party.* (when you mean "met each other") → ✓ *We met **each other** at the party.* / *We met at the party.*

**Note:** *themself* is used by some speakers today for a singular person whose gender is unknown or not stated, and some modern dictionaries record it. Traditional grammar and most exam guides do not accept it. Use *himself or herself* or *themselves* in exams.

#### 4. Demonstrative pronouns

Point to things: *this, that, these, those*.

- ***This** is my seat.* (near, singular)
- ***Those** are my books.* (far, plural)

When they come before a noun (*this seat*, *those books*), many books call them **demonstrative adjectives** or **determiners**, not pronouns.

#### 5. Interrogative pronouns

Ask questions: *who, whom, whose, what, which*.

- ***Who** called you?* (subject)
- ***Whom** did you call?* (object — formal; *Who did you call?* is common in speech)
- ***Whose** is this phone?*
- ***Which** do you prefer, rice or beans?*

#### 6. Relative pronouns

Introduce relative (adjective) clauses: *who, whom, whose, which, that*. These are explained fully in `B3a-clauses.md`.

- *The woman **who** sells akara is my aunt.*
- *The bag **which/that** I bought is red.*

#### 7. Indefinite pronouns

Refer to people or things in a general way:

- Singular: *anyone, anybody, anything, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, something, no one, nobody, nothing, each, either, neither, one, another*
- Plural: *both, few, many, several, others*
- Singular or plural (depending on the noun they refer to): *all, any, some, most, none, more*

**Agreement:** the singular indefinite pronouns take **singular verbs**:

- ✓ *Everybody **is** here.* ✗ *Everybody are here.*
- ✓ *Each of the students **has** a book.* ✗ *Each of the students have a book.*
- ✓ *Neither of them **was** present.* (formal; *were* is common in speech)
- ✓ *Some of the rice **is** spoilt.* (rice: uncountable) / *Some of the books **are** torn.* (books: plural)

**None:** *None of the students **was/were** absent.* Traditional grammar insists on the singular (*none* = *not one*). Modern usage guides accept both, and the plural is very common. In JAMB, the singular is the safer formal choice when "not one" is meant.

#### 8. Reciprocal pronouns

Show a two-way action: *each other, one another*.

- *Ada and Ngozi helped **each other**.*
- *The students in the class helped **one another**.*

**Traditional rule:** *each other* for **two** people; *one another* for **more than two**. Many modern grammars say the two are interchangeable, but some JAMB-style questions still follow the traditional rule. If the sentence clearly has two people, *each other* is the safe answer; for a large group, *one another*.

#### 9. Distributive pronouns

Refer to members of a group one at a time: *each, either, neither*. They take **singular** verbs and pronouns.

- ***Each** of the boys **has** **his** own bed.*
- ***Either** of the two roads **leads** to Ibadan.* (*either* = one or the other of **two**)
- ***Neither** of the twins **likes** okra.*

**Either/neither** are for **two** items. For more than two, use *any* or *none*:

- ✗ *Either of the three candidates can win.* → ✓ *Any of the three candidates can win.*

## Pronoun–antecedent agreement

A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in **number**, **person** and **gender**.

- ✓ *The **girl** lost **her** book.*
- ✓ *The **girls** lost **their** books.*
- ✗ *Every **student** must bring **their** book.* — traditional grammar marks this wrong.
- ✓ *Every **student** must bring **his or her** book.* — traditional formal answer.

**Usage note on singular "they":** using *they/their* for a singular person whose gender is unknown (*Everyone must bring their book*) is very old in English and is accepted by modern dictionaries and style guides. However, many Nigerian exam guides still mark it wrong. In JAMB, if the options include *his or her* (or *his* alone in older-style questions), choose that when the sentence uses a singular indefinite word like *everyone*, *each*, *every student*.

### The pronoun "one"

When you start with *one*, traditional British usage continues with *one/one's/oneself*:

- ✓ *One should do **one's** duty.* (formal British)
- *One should do **his** duty.* (traditional American; often marked wrong in British-based exams)
- ✗ *One should do **your** duty.* (mixing persons)
- ✗ *One should do **their** duty.* (marked wrong in most exam guides)

### Clear reference

A pronoun must clearly point to one antecedent.

- ✗ *Musa told Femi that **he** had won.* (Who won? Musa or Femi?)
- ✓ *Musa told Femi, "I have won."* / *Musa told Femi that Femi had won.*

### Gender errors in Nigerian English

Many Nigerian languages (for example Yoruba, with the single third-person pronoun *ó*) do not mark gender in third-person pronouns. So some Nigerian speakers mix up *he* and *she* in English: ✗ *My mother said **he** will come.* → ✓ *My mother said **she** will come.* Be careful in writing and in exams.

## How to answer JAMB noun and pronoun questions: step by step

1. **Read the whole sentence** and find the gap or the italic word.
2. **Ask what job the word does:** subject, object, after a preposition, before a noun?
3. **For pronouns,** choose the case: subject form (*I, he, we, who*) for subjects; object form (*me, him, us, whom*) for objects and after prepositions. Use the "remove the other person" test for pairs.
4. **For nouns,** ask: is it countable or uncountable? Singular or plural? Does it need an apostrophe?
5. **Check agreement:** does the verb or pronoun match the noun in number?
6. **Choose the formal Standard British English option** if two options seem possible.

### Worked example

> The money was shared between Adamu and ______.
>
> - A. I
> - B. me
> - C. myself
> - D. mine

- *between* is a preposition → object form.
- Test: "shared between ... **me**". Not "between I".
- **Answer: B.** C is wrong because *myself* is reflexive/emphatic, not a replacement for *me*.

### Worked example

> The ______ were all damaged in the fire.
>
> - A. furnitures
> - B. furniture
> - C. pieces of furniture
> - D. furniture's

- *Furniture* is uncountable, so A is wrong.
- The verb is *were* (plural), so we need a plural subject. *Furniture* would take *was*.
- D is a possessive, not a plural.
- **Answer: C.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Word class (part of speech) | A group of words that behave in the same way, e.g. nouns, verbs |
| Noun | A word that names a person, place, thing, idea or feeling |
| Proper noun | The name of a particular person, place or thing; begins with a capital letter |
| Common noun | A general name for any member of a group |
| Abstract noun | The name of an idea, quality or feeling, e.g. *honesty* |
| Collective noun | The name of a group as one unit, e.g. *team, herd* |
| Countable noun | A noun with singular and plural forms that can be counted |
| Uncountable (mass) noun | A noun that cannot be counted and has no normal plural, e.g. *advice* |
| Compound noun | A noun made of two or more words, e.g. *mother-in-law* |
| Possessive case | The form of a noun or pronoun that shows ownership, e.g. *Ada's*, *hers* |
| Pronoun | A word used in place of a noun |
| Antecedent | The noun that a pronoun refers to |
| Subject case | The form of a pronoun used as a subject: *I, he, she, we, they, who* |
| Object case | The form of a pronoun used as an object or after a preposition: *me, him, her, us, them, whom* |
| Reflexive pronoun | A *-self/-selves* pronoun referring back to the subject, e.g. *She hurt herself* |
| Reciprocal pronoun | *each other, one another* |
| Indefinite pronoun | A pronoun that refers to people or things in general, e.g. *everyone, none* |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Uncountable nouns have no plural:** *furniture, information, equipment, luggage, advice, evidence, stationery, jewellery*. Use *a piece of* to count them.
- **Always plural:** *police, cattle, people, trousers, scissors, goods, proceeds*. **Always singular:** *news, mathematics, measles*.
- **Foreign plurals:** *criterion → criteria, phenomenon → phenomena, crisis → crises, analysis → analyses, bacterium → bacteria*.
- **Compound plurals:** the main word changes: *mothers-in-law, passers-by, commanders-in-chief*.
- ***one of* + plural noun + singular verb:** *One of my friends is ...*
- **After prepositions, use the object form:** *between you and me*, *for him and me*.
- **No apostrophe** in *hers, ours, yours, theirs, its* (possessive). *It's* = it is / it has.
- **Plurals of -o and -f words:** *potatoes, tomatoes, heroes, photos, pianos*; *knives, wives, leaves*, but *roofs, chiefs, beliefs*.
- **Either/neither** are for two; *any/none* for more than two.
- **Nouns as modifiers** stay singular: *a ten-year-old girl*.

## Quick summary

1. Word classes include nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions and interjections; many modern books add determiners. A word's class depends on how it is used.
2. Nouns may be proper or common, concrete or abstract, collective, countable or uncountable, and compound.
3. Plurals follow spelling rules (*-s, -es, -ies, -ves*), with irregular, foreign (*criteria, phenomena*) and compound (*mothers-in-law*) forms to learn.
4. Uncountable nouns such as *furniture*, *information*, *equipment* and *advice* never take *-s* or *a/an*; *police*, *cattle* and *people* are always plural; *news* is singular.
5. Nouns can work as subject, object, complement, object of a preposition, in apposition or as a modifier.
6. Apostrophes: *boy's* (one), *boys'* (many), *children's*; possessive pronouns (*hers, its*) never take an apostrophe.
7. Use subject pronouns for subjects and object pronouns after verbs and prepositions (*between you and me*); pronouns must agree with their antecedents.

## Practice questions

In questions 1 to 7, choose the option that best completes the gap.

**1. The new ______ for the laboratory has arrived.**

- A. equipments
- B. equipment
- C. equipments have
- D. equipment's

**Answer: B.** *Equipment* is uncountable, so it has no plural form, and it agrees with the singular verb *has*.

**2. The money was divided equally between Tolu and ______.**

- A. I
- B. me
- C. myself
- D. mine

**Answer: B.** *Between* is a preposition, so it is followed by the object form *me*.

**3. One of the ______ in our class won a scholarship.**

- A. student
- B. students
- C. student's
- D. students'

**Answer: B.** *One of* is followed by a plural noun. No possession is shown, so no apostrophe is needed.

**4. The committee will announce the ______ for selecting the candidates.**

- A. criterias
- B. criterion's
- C. criteria
- D. criterions

**Answer: C.** The plural of *criterion* is *criteria*. *Criterias* and *criterions* are wrong forms.

**5. Both of my ______ are lawyers.**

- A. brother-in-laws
- B. brothers-in-law
- C. brothers-in-laws
- D. brother's-in-law

**Answer: B.** In compound nouns like this, the main word (*brother*) takes the plural.

**6. The police ______ the suspects since Monday.**

- A. has been questioning
- B. have been questioning
- C. is questioning
- D. was questioning

**Answer: B.** *Police* is always plural, and *since Monday* needs the present perfect continuous.

**7. The cat licked ______ paws after eating.**

- A. it's
- B. its'
- C. its
- D. it

**Answer: C.** *Its* (no apostrophe) is the possessive. *It's* means "it is" or "it has", and *its'* does not exist.

**8. In the sentence "We elected Funke *treasurer*", the word in italics functions as**

- A. direct object
- B. indirect object
- C. object complement
- D. subject

**Answer: C.** *Funke* is the object, and *treasurer* renames her (Funke = treasurer). That makes it an object complement.

**9. Which of the following is correctly written?**

- A. The childrens' toys are broken.
- B. The children's toys are broken.
- C. The childrens toys are broken.
- D. The children toys' are broken.

**Answer: B.** *Children* is a plural that does not end in *-s*, so the possessive is *children's*.

**10. Choose the option that best completes the gap: The two sisters always help ______ with their homework.**

- A. themselves
- B. each other
- C. one other
- D. theirselves

**Answer: B.** The action is two-way between two people, so the reciprocal *each other* is correct. *Themselves* would mean each sister helps herself; *theirselves* is not Standard English.
