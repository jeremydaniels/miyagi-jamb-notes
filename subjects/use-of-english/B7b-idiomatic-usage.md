# JAMB Use of English Notes: Idiomatic Usage

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B (Lexis and Structure), content item (g) of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: "ordinary usage, figurative usage and idiomatic usage". These notes cover **idiomatic usage**. Ordinary and figurative usage are in `B7a-ordinary-and-figurative-usage.md`.

The objective covered here is:

- **Objective i:** identify words and expressions in their ordinary, figurative and idiomatic contexts.

The syllabus adds an important note:

> "Idioms to be tested are those that are formal and expressed in Standard British English. (SBE)."

This means JAMB will not test slang, American-only expressions or Nigerian Pidgin. It tests the kind of idioms you find in British dictionaries such as the *Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary* and the *Cambridge Dictionary*.

How it is tested (Section D of the syllabus):

- Idioms appear mostly in **Sentence interpretation (5 questions)** ("choose the option that best explains the information conveyed in the sentence"), in **Synonyms (5)** and **Antonyms (5)** ("choose the option nearest/opposite in meaning to the expression in italics"), in **Basic Grammar (10)** (completing an idiom with the right word, often a preposition) and in **comprehension passages**.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Explain what an idiom is and how it differs from ordinary and figurative expressions.
2. Interpret common formal British English idioms correctly.
3. Use the right fixed words (especially prepositions) in idioms.
4. Recognise phrasal verbs used idiomatically.
5. Tell idioms apart from proverbs.
6. Avoid Nigerian English expressions that are not Standard British English.

## What an idiom is

An **idiom** is a group of words with a fixed form whose meaning **cannot be worked out from the meanings of the individual words**.

- *to let the cat out of the bag* = to reveal a secret by mistake. (No cat, no bag.)
- *to kick the bucket* = to die (informal). (No kicking, no bucket.)
- *to bury the hatchet* = to stop quarrelling and become friends again.
- *a red-letter day* = a very important or happy day.

### Two key features of idioms

1. **The meaning is not literal.** You must learn it as a whole, the way you learn a single word.
2. **The form is fixed.** You cannot freely change the words. We say *to beat about the bush*, not *to beat around the tree*; *to take the bull by the horns*, not *to take the cow by the horns*. Changing a word usually destroys the idiom.

Some idioms do allow small changes (for example the pronoun: *she kept her head*, *they kept their heads*), but the key words cannot be swapped for synonyms.

### Idiomatic vs figurative vs ordinary usage

| Point | Ordinary usage | Figurative usage | Idiomatic usage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Literal | Imaginative, but can often be worked out from the picture | Fixed meaning that must be learnt |
| Fixed wording? | No | Usually not (writers invent new metaphors) | Yes |
| Example | *He broke the plate.* | *Her silence broke my heart.* | *He broke the ice at the party* (started the conversation and made people relax) |
| Can you guess the meaning? | Yes | Usually | Often not |

**Textbook variation:** many idioms began life as metaphors, so some books treat idioms as a type of figurative language. Other books keep them separate, as the JAMB syllabus does. The practical difference is that an idiom is **fixed and conventional**, while a figure of speech can be created freely.

## How to interpret an idiom step by step

1. **Spot the idiom.** Look for a group of words that sounds odd if taken literally (*in hot water*, *on cloud nine*).
2. **Recall it if you know it.** If you have learnt it, its meaning comes straight away. This is why a large idiom vocabulary is essential.
3. **If you don't know it, use the context.** What is happening before and after it? Is the situation positive or negative? Is someone happy, in trouble, lucky, angry?
4. **Think about the picture behind it.** Many idioms come from a clear picture (*to burn the midnight oil* = to work late into the night, from oil lamps). This can help, but be careful, because some pictures mislead.
5. **Reject the literal options.** JAMB almost always includes an option that takes the idiom literally.
6. **Match the strength and tone.** *To be on cloud nine* is "extremely happy", not just "satisfied". *To pass away* is polite; *to kick the bucket* is informal.
7. **Check that your choice makes sense in the whole sentence.**

**Worked example.** *The thief was caught* red-handed.

- Step 1: "red-handed" makes no sense literally here.
- Step 2: known idiom: caught **in the act** of doing something wrong.
- Step 5: an option like "with blood on his hands" is the literal trap.
- **Meaning:** he was caught while actually stealing.

**Worked example.** *After failing the mock exam, Tunde decided to* turn over a new leaf.

- Literal: turn a page or a leaf of a plant. Doesn't fit.
- Context: failure → decision → probably change.
- **Meaning:** to start behaving in a better way.

## Idioms with fixed prepositions and particles

JAMB often tests idioms by asking you to fill a gap with the right preposition or particle. You cannot reason these out; learn them.

| Correct | Common wrong form |
| --- | --- |
| *at* one's wits' end | ~~in~~ one's wits' end |
| *in* the nick of time | ~~at~~ the nick of time |
| *at* the eleventh hour | ~~in~~ the eleventh hour |
| *on* cloud nine | ~~in~~ cloud nine |
| *in* hot water | ~~inside~~ hot water |
| *under* the weather | ~~in~~ the weather |
| *at* loggerheads *with* | ~~in~~ loggerheads |
| *by* and large | ~~in~~ and large |
| *on* the spur of the moment (suddenly, without planning) | ~~at~~ the spur of the moment |
| *in* the long run | ~~at~~ the long run |
| *off* the cuff (without preparation) | ~~on~~ the cuff |
| *at* a loss (unable to understand or know what to do) | ~~in~~ a loss |
| *at* stake (at risk) | ~~in~~ stake |
| *on* the verge of (very near to) | ~~at~~ the verge of |
| *in* the same boat (in the same difficult situation) | ~~on~~ the same boat |

**Worked example.** *The rescue team arrived ____ the nick of time.*

- A. at  B. in  C. on  D. by

The fixed idiom is *in the nick of time*. **Answer: B.**

## Phrasal verbs as idioms

A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus a particle (an adverb or a preposition), such as *give up*, *put off*, *look after*. Many phrasal verbs are **idiomatic**: their meaning is not the sum of the verb and the particle.

| Phrasal verb | Literal or idiomatic? | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *look up* | literal | to raise your eyes | *She looked up at the sky.* |
| *look up* | idiomatic | to search for information in a book or online | *Look up the word in your dictionary.* |
| *look up to* | idiomatic | to admire and respect | *I look up to my elder sister.* |
| *look down on* | idiomatic | to think you are better than someone | *Don't look down on people who sell in the market.* |
| *put off* | idiomatic | to postpone | *The match was put off because of rain.* |
| *put up with* | idiomatic | to tolerate | *I can't put up with the noise from the beer parlour.* |
| *give up* | idiomatic | to stop trying; to stop a habit | *He gave up smoking.* |
| *give in* | idiomatic | to stop resisting; surrender | *The government finally gave in to the union's demands.* |
| *bring up* | idiomatic | to raise a child; to mention a subject | *She was brought up in Ibadan. / He brought up the issue of fees.* |
| *call off* | idiomatic | to cancel | *The strike was called off.* |
| *carry out* | idiomatic | to perform, do | *The doctors carried out the operation.* |
| *turn down* | idiomatic | to refuse an offer | *She turned down the job offer.* |
| *make up* | idiomatic | to invent (a story); to become friends again after a quarrel | *He made up an excuse. / They kissed and made up.* |
| *take after* | idiomatic | to resemble an older family member | *Kunle takes after his father.* |
| *run out of* | idiomatic | to use up completely | *We have run out of kerosene.* |
| *break down* | idiomatic | to stop working; to lose control of feelings | *The car broke down at Ore. / She broke down in tears.* |
| *come across* | idiomatic | to find by chance | *I came across an old photograph.* |
| *see through* | idiomatic | to recognise the truth behind a lie | *We saw through his story.* |
| *set in* | idiomatic | (of something unpleasant) to begin and seem likely to continue | *The rainy season has set in.* |
| *wind up* | idiomatic | to bring to an end; to close down a company | *The company was wound up last year.* |

**Trap:** the same phrasal verb can have several idiomatic meanings. Always use the context. *She made up her face* (put on make-up) is different from *she made up a story* (invented it).

**Textbook variation:** some grammar books separate "phrasal verbs" (verb + adverb particle, e.g. *give up*) from "prepositional verbs" (verb + preposition, e.g. *look after*) and "phrasal-prepositional verbs" (e.g. *put up with*). Most school books call all of them phrasal verbs. For JAMB the meaning matters more than the label.

## Proverbs vs idioms

A **proverb** is a short, well-known saying that gives **advice or states a general truth**. It is a complete sentence. An **idiom** is a phrase whose meaning is not literal; it is usually not a complete sentence and does not give advice by itself.

| Point | Idiom | Proverb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Form | A phrase, part of a sentence | A complete sentence |
| Purpose | Expresses an idea vividly | Gives advice or a moral lesson |
| Can you change the tense or subject? | Usually yes (*she spilt the beans*, *they spill the beans*) | Usually not; it is quoted as fixed |
| Example | *to cry over spilt milk* (to be upset about something that cannot be changed) | *It is no use crying over spilt milk.* |
| Example | *the early bird* | *The early bird catches the worm.* |

More English proverbs and what they mean:

| Proverb | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *A stitch in time saves nine.* | Fixing a problem early prevents bigger problems later. |
| *Too many cooks spoil the broth.* | When too many people are involved, the work is done badly. |
| *Don't count your chickens before they are hatched.* | Don't rely on something good happening until it actually happens. |
| *Where there's a will, there's a way.* | If you are determined, you will find a way. |
| *Actions speak louder than words.* | What you do matters more than what you say. |
| *Birds of a feather flock together.* | People with similar characters or interests spend time together. |
| *All that glitters is not gold.* | Things that look attractive are not always valuable. |
| *Half a loaf is better than none.* | It is better to have a little than nothing at all. |

Nigerian languages have many proverbs too, and Chinua Achebe famously wrote in *Things Fall Apart* that "proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten". JAMB, however, tests **English** idioms and proverbs, not translations of local ones.

## Nigerian English expressions that are not Standard British English

The syllabus says idioms are tested in **Standard British English**. Many expressions common in Nigerian English are not standard and are often used as wrong options. Learn the standard forms.

| Nigerian English | Standard British English |
| --- | --- |
| *Off the light.* | *Switch off / Turn off the light.* |
| *On the generator.* | *Switch on / Turn on the generator.* |
| *Flash me.* | *Give me a missed call* (there is no exact standard idiom; just say it plainly) |
| *There was go-slow on the expressway.* | *There was a traffic jam / There was heavy traffic.* (In British English, a go-slow is a form of industrial protest in which workers deliberately work slowly.) |
| *I'm coming* (meaning "wait, I'll be back") | *I'll be back shortly / I'll be right back.* |
| *Borrow me your pen.* | *Lend me your pen.* |
| *He is my senior brother.* | *He is my elder (or older) brother.* |
| *She is putting to bed.* | *She is in labour / giving birth.* |
| *Let me branch at the market.* | *Let me stop at the market on the way.* |
| *Well done* (as a greeting to someone working) | Standard British English uses *well done* to praise someone after they have done something well, not as a greeting. |
| *I don't have your time.* | *I don't have time for you / I'm not interested.* |
| *It is not my portion.* | No standard equivalent; avoid in formal English. |

These Nigerian forms are normal and useful in everyday speech in Nigeria, and Nigerian English is a recognised variety. But JAMB, following the syllabus, marks the Standard British English forms as correct.

## How JAMB tests idioms

### Type 1: Sentence interpretation

*Choose the option that best explains the information conveyed in the sentence.*

*The manager said he would not* sit on the fence *over the matter.*

- A. The manager would not climb the fence.
- B. The manager would take a clear side.
- C. The manager would avoid the matter.
- D. The manager would build a fence.

**Answer: B.** "Sit on the fence" means avoid taking sides, so "would not sit on the fence" means he would take a side. Watch the **negative**: many students choose C because they recognise the idiom but forget the "not".

### Type 2: Nearest in meaning

*Choose the option nearest in meaning to the expression in italics.*

*The festival is a* red-letter day *for the people of Osogbo.*

- A. a dangerous day
- B. a memorable and important day
- C. a day of mourning
- D. a public holiday

**Answer: B.** Option D is close but too narrow; a red-letter day is any very important, happy day, not necessarily a holiday. (The idiom comes from old calendars that printed festivals and holy days in red.)

### Type 3: Opposite in meaning

*Choose the option opposite in meaning to the expression in italics.*

*His uncle is known to be* tight-fisted.

- A. generous
- B. strict
- C. strong
- D. mean

**Answer: A.** "Tight-fisted" means unwilling to spend money (mean). Its opposite is generous. D is a synonym trap.

### Type 4: Completing the idiom

*Fill the gap with the most appropriate option.*

*After the quarrel, the two friends decided to bury the ____.*

- A. axe B. hatchet C. knife D. cutlass

**Answer: B.** The fixed idiom is *bury the hatchet*. "Axe" means almost the same thing literally, but the idiom does not allow the change.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Idiom | A fixed group of words whose meaning cannot be worked out from the individual words |
| Idiomatic usage | Using words in an idiom, with its special fixed meaning |
| Standard British English (SBE) | The form of English accepted as correct in formal British use, as shown in British dictionaries |
| Phrasal verb | A verb plus a particle that together have a special meaning (e.g. *give up*) |
| Particle | The small adverb or preposition in a phrasal verb (e.g. *up*, *off*, *in*) |
| Proverb | A short, complete saying that gives advice or states a general truth |
| Literal meaning | The basic meaning of the words taken at face value |
| Nigerianism | An expression common in Nigerian English but not in Standard British English |
| Fixed expression | A phrase whose words cannot be changed freely |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Learn idioms as whole units,** like vocabulary. Keep a notebook of idioms with meanings and example sentences.
- **Never change the words of an idiom.** It is *bury the hatchet*, not *bury the axe*; *beat about the bush*, not *beat around the tree*. (Note: *beat around the bush* is the American form; British English prefers *beat about the bush*.)
- **Watch the negative.** "He did not sit on the fence" means he took a side. Many candidates choose the meaning of the idiom and forget the "not".
- **Reject literal options** first. They are nearly always traps.
- **Match the strength:** *at one's wits' end* is "extremely worried", not just "worried".
- **Prepositions in idioms are fixed:** *in the nick of time*, *at the eleventh hour*, *on the verge of*, *at stake*, *by and large*.
- **Use British, not Nigerian or American, forms** in formal answers: *switch off the light*, *lend me your pen*, *elder brother*, *traffic jam*.
- **Phrasal verbs have several meanings.** Let the context decide (*make up a story* vs *make up after a quarrel*).

## Quick summary

1. An idiom is a fixed group of words whose meaning cannot be worked out from the individual words.
2. Idioms tested by JAMB are formal and in Standard British English, as the syllabus note says.
3. To interpret an idiom: spot it, recall or use context, reject literal options, and match the strength and tone of the meaning.
4. The words of an idiom, including its prepositions, are fixed and cannot be replaced by synonyms.
5. Many phrasal verbs are idiomatic and can have more than one meaning depending on context.
6. A proverb is a complete sentence giving advice; an idiom is usually a phrase.
7. Many Nigerian English expressions ("off the light", "borrow me", "senior brother", "go-slow" for traffic jam) are not Standard British English and are often used as wrong options.

## Practice questions

**1. Choose the option that best explains the information conveyed in the sentence: "The new teacher took the bull by the horns and dealt with the noisy class."**

- A. The new teacher was afraid of the noisy class.
- B. The new teacher faced the difficult situation directly and boldly.
- C. The new teacher punished the class like an animal.
- D. The new teacher ignored the noise in the class.

**Answer: B.** "To take the bull by the horns" means to deal with a difficult situation directly and bravely. Option C is a literal reading.

**2. Choose the option nearest in meaning to the expression in italics: "The abandoned refinery has become a *white elephant*."**

- A. a rare and valuable asset
- B. a costly project that is of no use
- C. a symbol of national pride
- D. a dangerous place

**Answer: B.** A white elephant is something expensive to own or maintain that is of little or no use.

**3. Choose the option nearest in meaning to the expression in italics: "The doctor arrived *in the nick of time*."**

- A. very late
- B. very early
- C. just in time
- D. at night

**Answer: C.** "In the nick of time" means at the last possible moment, just before it would have been too late.

**4. Fill the gap with the most appropriate option: "When the boss asked who broke the machine, Musa decided to face the ____."**

- A. truth
- B. music
- C. danger
- D. boss

**Answer: B.** The fixed idiom is "to face the music", meaning to accept the punishment or criticism for something one has done.

**5. Choose the option that best explains the information conveyed in the sentence: "Adaeze's parents are at their wits' end over her poor health."**

- A. Adaeze's parents are very rich.
- B. Adaeze's parents are extremely worried and don't know what to do.
- C. Adaeze's parents have given up on her.
- D. Adaeze's parents are very clever.

**Answer: B.** "At one's wits' end" means so worried and confused that one does not know what to do next. C goes too far; they have not given up.

**6. Choose the option opposite in meaning to the expression in italics: "The two neighbours have always *seen eye to eye* on the matter."**

- A. agreed
- B. looked at each other
- C. disagreed
- D. kept quiet

**Answer: C.** "To see eye to eye" means to agree. Its opposite is to disagree. A is the synonym trap.

**7. Choose the option nearest in meaning to the expression in italics: "The quarrel over the parking space was only *a storm in a teacup*."**

- A. a serious and dangerous fight
- B. a lot of fuss about something unimportant
- C. a fight that took place in the rain
- D. a long-lasting disagreement

**Answer: B.** "A storm in a teacup" means great anger or excitement about a very small matter.

**8. Which of the following is correct in Standard British English?**

- A. Please off the fan before you leave.
- B. Please switch off the fan before you leave.
- C. Please on off the fan before you leave.
- D. Please close the fan before you leave.

**Answer: B.** "Switch off" (or "turn off") is the standard form. "Off the fan" and "close the fan" are Nigerian English expressions, and C is ungrammatical.

**9. Choose the option that best explains the information conveyed in the sentence: "The committee's report was only the tip of the iceberg."**

- A. The report was about ice in cold countries.
- B. The report revealed only a small part of a much bigger problem.
- C. The report was the most important part of the investigation.
- D. The report was cold and unfriendly.

**Answer: B.** "The tip of the iceberg" is a small, visible part of a much larger problem that is mostly hidden.

**10. Choose the option nearest in meaning to the word in italics: "The match was *put off* because of the heavy rain."**

- A. cancelled for ever
- B. postponed
- C. switched off
- D. disliked

**Answer: B.** The phrasal verb "put off" here means to postpone (move to a later time). "Call off" would mean cancel.
