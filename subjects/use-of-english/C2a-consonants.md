# JAMB Use of English Notes: Consonants

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section C (Oral Forms), content item (b) of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: **Consonants (including clusters)**. These notes cover the 24 single consonant sounds of English. Consonant clusters (groups of consonants) are covered in `C2b-consonant-clusters.md`.

The syllabus objectives for Oral Forms are:

1. make distinctions among vowel types;
2. differentiate among consonant types; and
3. identify correct pronunciation of individual words and articulation of connected speech.

Objectives 2 and 3 apply here. In the exam, **consonants carry 2 questions**.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Explain what a consonant sound is and how it differs from a vowel.
2. Describe any English consonant by its **voicing**, **place of articulation** and **manner of articulation**.
3. Name the 24 RP consonants with their IPA symbols and example words.
4. Recognise the different spellings of each consonant sound, including silent letters.
5. Pronounce the past tense ending **-ed** and the plural/verb ending **-s** correctly.
6. Tell apart consonants that many Nigerian speakers confuse, using minimal pairs.
7. Answer JAMB questions such as "Choose the option that has the same consonant sound as the one represented by the letter(s) in italics".

> **How italics are used in these notes:** JAMB prints the tested letters underlined. In these notes, the tested letters are shown in *italics*, for example ma*ch*ine.

> **Stress marking:** in transcriptions, **ˈ** comes before the stressed syllable, e.g. /məˈʃiːn/ = maCHINE.

## Consonant sounds vs consonant letters

English has **21 consonant letters** but **24 consonant sounds**. As with vowels, letters and sounds do not match one-to-one:

- Some sounds have no single letter of their own: /θ/ (*th*in), /ð/ (*th*is), /ʃ/ (*sh*oe), /ʒ/ (mea*s*ure), /tʃ/ (*ch*urch), /ŋ/ (si*ng*).
- Some letters stand for two sounds: *x* in *box* = /ks/.
- Some letters stand for several sounds: *c* = /k/ in *cat*, /s/ in *city*, /ʃ/ in *ocean*.
- Some letters are **silent**: *k* in *know*, *b* in *comb*.

So, as with vowels: **always answer from the sound, not from the spelling.**

### What is a consonant?

A **consonant** is a speech sound made by **blocking or narrowing** the flow of air somewhere in the mouth or throat. The block may be complete (as for /p/, where the lips close) or partial (as for /s/, where air hisses through a narrow gap).

| Feature | Vowels | Consonants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Airflow | Free, no blockage | Blocked or narrowed somewhere |
| Voicing | Always voiced in English | Some voiced, some voiceless |
| Position in a syllable | The centre (nucleus) of the syllable | Usually at the edges (beginning or end) |
| Number in RP | 20 (12 + 8) | 24 |

## How consonants are described (objective ii: "differentiate among consonant types")

Every consonant is described using three labels. Phoneticians call this a **three-term label**:

1. **Voicing:** are the vocal cords vibrating?
2. **Place of articulation:** where in the mouth is the air blocked or narrowed?
3. **Manner of articulation:** how is the air blocked or narrowed?

Example: /b/ is a **voiced bilabial plosive**. /s/ is a **voiceless alveolar fricative**.

### 1. Voicing

Put your fingers on your throat (your "Adam's apple") and say "sssss", then "zzzzz". You feel a buzz for /z/ but not for /s/. The buzz is the **vocal cords vibrating**.

- **Voiced** consonants: the vocal cords vibrate. /b d ɡ v ð z ʒ dʒ m n ŋ l r w j/.
- **Voiceless** consonants: the vocal cords do not vibrate. /p t k f θ s ʃ tʃ h/.

Many consonants come in **voiced–voiceless pairs**, made in the same place and the same way:

| Voiceless | Voiced | Example pair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /p/ | /b/ | pea – bee |
| /t/ | /d/ | ten – den |
| /k/ | /ɡ/ | coat – goat |
| /f/ | /v/ | fan – van |
| /θ/ | /ð/ | thigh – thy |
| /s/ | /z/ | sip – zip |
| /ʃ/ | /ʒ/ | no common minimal pair; compare *pressure* /ˈpreʃə/ and *pleasure* /ˈpleʒə/ |
| /tʃ/ | /dʒ/ | cheap – jeep |

The nasals /m n ŋ/, /l/, /r/, /w/ and /j/ are all voiced and have no voiceless partner in English. /h/ is voiceless and has no voiced partner.

**Fortis and lenis:** some books call voiceless consonants **fortis** (strong) and voiced ones **lenis** (weak), because voiced consonants at the end of words in RP are often only partly voiced. For JAMB, "voiced" and "voiceless" are enough.

| Place | Organs used | Consonants | Example words |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bilabial | Both lips | /p b m w/ | pen, bag, man, wet |
| Labiodental | Lower lip against upper teeth | /f v/ | fan, van |
| Dental | Tongue tip between or against the upper teeth | /θ ð/ | thin, this |
| Alveolar | Tongue tip on the alveolar ridge (the bony ridge behind the upper teeth) | /t d s z n l/ | top, dog, sun, zoo, no, leg |
| Post-alveolar | Tongue just behind the alveolar ridge | /r/ | red |
| Palato-alveolar | Tongue blade against the area between the alveolar ridge and hard palate | /ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/ | shoe, measure, chair, jam |
| Palatal | Front of tongue towards the hard palate | /j/ | yes |
| Velar | Back of tongue against the soft palate (velum) | /k ɡ ŋ/ | cat, go, sing |
| Glottal | The gap between the vocal cords (glottis) | /h/ | hat |

**Textbook variation:**
- /ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/ are called **palato-alveolar** in Gimson and most Nigerian textbooks, and **post-alveolar** in Roach and in the IPA chart. Both names are correct.
- /w/ is usually listed as **bilabial** in school books, but it is more precisely **labial-velar** (lips rounded *and* the back of the tongue raised towards the soft palate). Some books list it as velar.
- /r/ is listed as **post-alveolar** (Roach) or **alveolar** in some books.

### 3. Manner of articulation

| Manner | What happens | Consonants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plosive (stop) | Air is completely blocked, pressure builds, then is released with a small "pop" | /p b t d k ɡ/ |
| Fricative | Air is forced through a narrow gap, making a hissing or friction noise; the sound can be held | /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/ |
| Affricate | Starts as a plosive (complete block), then releases slowly into a fricative | /tʃ dʒ/ |
| Nasal | The mouth is blocked, the soft palate is lowered, and air comes out through the nose | /m n ŋ/ |
| Lateral | The tongue tip touches the alveolar ridge, and air flows out around the **sides** of the tongue | /l/ |
| Approximant (frictionless continuant / semi-vowel) | The organs come close together but not close enough to make friction | /r w j/ |

**Textbook variation:** /w/ and /j/ are often called **semi-vowels** in Nigerian textbooks, because they are made like vowels (/w/ like /uː/, /j/ like /iː/) but behave like consonants. /r/ is called a **frictionless continuant**, an **approximant** or a **post-alveolar approximant**. Some books group /l/ and /r/ together as **liquids**. Plosives are also called **stops**.

## The 24 consonants one by one

### Plosives

#### /p/: voiceless bilabial plosive

Lips close, air builds up, lips open with a puff. At the start of a stressed syllable, /p t k/ are **aspirated** in RP: there is a small puff of air after them (hold a sheet of paper in front of your mouth and say "pin": it moves). After /s/ (as in *spin*), there is no aspiration.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| p, pp | pen /pen/, cup /kʌp/, happy /ˈhæpi/, apple /ˈæpl/ |

Silent p: *psychology* /saɪˈkɒlədʒi/, *pneumonia* /njuːˈməʊniə/, *receipt* /rɪˈsiːt/, *cupboard* /ˈkʌbəd/, *psalm* /sɑːm/.

#### /b/: voiced bilabial plosive

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| b, bb | bag /bæɡ/, rob /rɒb/, rubber /ˈrʌbə/, cabin /ˈkæbɪn/ |

Silent b: after m at the end of a word, and before t: *comb* /kəʊm/, *climb* /klaɪm/, *thumb* /θʌm/, *lamb* /læm/, *bomb* /bɒm/, *tomb* /tuːm/, *womb* /wuːm/, *plumber* /ˈplʌmə/, *debt* /det/, *doubt* /daʊt/, *subtle* /ˈsʌtl/.

#### /t/: voiceless alveolar plosive

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| t, tt | top /tɒp/, cat /kæt/, better /ˈbetə/ |
| -ed (after voiceless sounds) | walked /wɔːkt/, stopped /stɒpt/, laughed /lɑːft/ |
| th (a few words) | Thomas /ˈtɒməs/, Thames /temz/, thyme /taɪm/ |

Silent t: *castle* /ˈkɑːsl/, *listen* /ˈlɪsn/, *fasten* /ˈfɑːsn/, *whistle* /ˈwɪsl/, *Christmas* /ˈkrɪsməs/, *mortgage* /ˈmɔːɡɪdʒ/, *ballet* /ˈbæleɪ/. *Often* is /ˈɒfn/ in RP, but /ˈɒftən/ is also widely heard and accepted.

#### /d/: voiced alveolar plosive

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| d, dd | dog /dɒɡ/, bed /bed/, ladder /ˈlædə/ |
| -ed (after voiced sounds) | played /pleɪd/, lived /lɪvd/, called /kɔːld/ |

Silent d: *handkerchief* /ˈhæŋkətʃɪf/, *Wednesday* /ˈwenzdeɪ/, *handsome* /ˈhænsəm/.

#### /k/: voiceless velar plosive

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| c (before a, o, u or a consonant) | cat /kæt/, cot /kɒt/, cut /kʌt/, clean /kliːn/ |
| k, ck | kite /kaɪt/, back /bæk/, kick /kɪk/ |
| ch (mostly Greek-origin words) | chemist /ˈkemɪst/, character /ˈkærəktə/, chorus /ˈkɔːrəs/, school /skuːl/, stomach /ˈstʌmək/, ache /eɪk/, architect /ˈɑːkɪtekt/, mechanic /məˈkænɪk/, orchestra /ˈɔːkɪstrə/, scheme /skiːm/ |
| qu, que | queen /kwiːn/ (= /k/ + /w/), quay /kiː/, cheque /tʃek/, unique /juˈniːk/ |
| x (= /k/ + /s/) | box /bɒks/, six /sɪks/ |

Silent k: before n at the start of a word: *knife* /naɪf/, *know* /nəʊ/, *knee* /niː/, *knock* /nɒk/, *knowledge* /ˈnɒlɪdʒ/, *knot* /nɒt/.

#### /ɡ/: voiced velar plosive

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| g, gg | go /ɡəʊ/, bag /bæɡ/, egg /eɡ/, begin /bɪˈɡɪn/, give /ɡɪv/, get /ɡet/ |
| gu, gh | guest /ɡest/, guitar /ɡɪˈtɑː/, guard /ɡɑːd/, ghost /ɡəʊst/, spaghetti /spəˈɡeti/ |
| x (= /ɡ/ + /z/) | exam /ɪɡˈzæm/, exact /ɪɡˈzækt/, exist /ɪɡˈzɪst/ |

Silent g: before n: *sign* /saɪn/, *design* /dɪˈzaɪn/, *foreign* /ˈfɒrən/, *reign* /reɪn/, *gnaw* /nɔː/, *gnat* /næt/, *champagne* /ʃæmˈpeɪn/. (But the g is sounded in *signature* /ˈsɪɡnətʃə/ and *signal* /ˈsɪɡnəl/.)

### Fricatives

#### /f/: voiceless labiodental fricative

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| f, ff | fan /fæn/, leaf /liːf/, coffee /ˈkɒfi/ |
| ph | phone /fəʊn/, photograph /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/, physics /ˈfɪzɪks/, elephant /ˈelɪfənt/ |
| gh | laugh /lɑːf/, cough /kɒf/, rough /rʌf/, enough /ɪˈnʌf/, tough /tʌf/ |

**Traps:** *of* is pronounced with /v/ (/ɒv/ strong, /əv/ weak), not /f/. *Shepherd* /ˈʃepəd/ has /p/, not /f/ (the p and h belong to different parts of the word: *sheep + herd*). *Uphill* /ʌpˈhɪl/ has /p/ + /h/.

#### /v/: voiced labiodental fricative

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| v | van /væn/, very /ˈveri/, love /lʌv/, give /ɡɪv/, seven /ˈsevn/ |
| f (in "of") | of /ɒv/ |
| ph (rare) | Stephen /ˈstiːvn/ |

#### /θ/: voiceless dental fricative

Tongue tip lightly between or just behind the upper front teeth; air hisses out over it.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| th | thin /θɪn/, think /θɪŋk/, three /θriː/, thank /θæŋk/, thumb /θʌm/, Thursday /ˈθɜːzdeɪ/, theatre /ˈθɪətə/, author /ˈɔːθə/, method /ˈmeθəd/, nothing /ˈnʌθɪŋ/, bath /bɑːθ/, mouth (noun) /maʊθ/, health /helθ/, month /mʌnθ/, both /bəʊθ/, faith /feɪθ/ |

#### /ð/: voiced dental fricative

Same position as /θ/, but with the voice on.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| th | this /ðɪs/, that /ðæt/, the /ðə/, they /ðeɪ/, there /ðeə/, then /ðen/, though /ðəʊ/, mother /ˈmʌðə/, father /ˈfɑːðə/, brother /ˈbrʌðə/, weather /ˈweðə/, breathe /briːð/, smooth /smuːð/, clothe /kləʊð/ |

**Spelling hint for th:**
- Grammar words (*the, this, that, these, those, they, them, their, there, then, than, though*) start with **/ð/**.
- Most content words starting with "th" (*thin, think, thank, thief, thousand, thunder, theory*) start with **/θ/**.
- Between vowels in native words, "th" is often /ð/ (*mother, father, weather, together*), but not always (*author, method, nothing* have /θ/).
- Noun–verb pairs: the noun has /θ/, the verb has /ð/: *bath* /bɑːθ/ – *bathe* /beɪð/; *breath* /breθ/ – *breathe* /briːð/; *cloth* /klɒθ/ – *clothe* /kləʊð/; *mouth* (noun) /maʊθ/ – *mouth* (verb) /maʊð/.

**Variation:** *with* is /wɪð/ in RP, but /wɪθ/ is also common; dictionaries give both. Avoid using it to test /θ/ or /ð/.

#### /s/: voiceless alveolar fricative

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| s, ss | sun /sʌn/, bus /bʌs/, dress /dres/, cats /kæts/ |
| c (before e, i, y) | city /ˈsɪti/, cell /sel/, cycle /ˈsaɪkl/, mice /maɪs/, rice /raɪs/ |
| sc | science /ˈsaɪəns/, scene /siːn/, muscle /ˈmʌsl/ |
| ps | psychology /saɪˈkɒlədʒi/, psalm /sɑːm/ |
| x (= /k/ + /s/) | box /bɒks/ |

Silent s: *island* /ˈaɪlənd/, *aisle* /aɪl/, *isle* /aɪl/, *viscount* /ˈvaɪkaʊnt/.

#### /z/: voiced alveolar fricative

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| z, zz | zoo /zuː/, zero /ˈzɪərəʊ/, buzz /bʌz/, puzzle /ˈpʌzl/ |
| s (often between vowels or at word end) | rose /rəʊz/, busy /ˈbɪzi/, music /ˈmjuːzɪk/, has /hæz/, is /ɪz/, was /wɒz/, dogs /dɒɡz/, scissors /ˈsɪzəz/ |
| x (at start of word) | xylophone /ˈzaɪləfəʊn/ |

**Noun–verb pairs (s vs z):** *use* (noun) /juːs/ – *use* (verb) /juːz/; *excuse* (n) /ɪkˈskjuːs/ – (v) /ɪkˈskjuːz/; *house* (n) /haʊs/ – (v) /haʊz/; *advice* (n) /ədˈvaɪs/ – *advise* (v) /ədˈvaɪz/; *close* (adj, "near") /kləʊs/ – *close* (v, "shut") /kləʊz/.

#### /ʃ/: voiceless palato-alveolar fricative

Tongue blade raised behind the alveolar ridge; lips slightly rounded and pushed forward. The "shh" sound you make to tell someone to be quiet.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| sh | shoe /ʃuː/, ship /ʃɪp/, fish /fɪʃ/, wash /wɒʃ/ |
| ti, ci, si, ssi (before a vowel) | nation /ˈneɪʃn/, station /ˈsteɪʃn/, special /ˈspeʃl/, ocean /ˈəʊʃn/, mission /ˈmɪʃn/, pension /ˈpenʃn/, delicious /dɪˈlɪʃəs/ |
| s, ss | sugar /ˈʃʊɡə/, sure /ʃʊə/ (also /ʃɔː/), pressure /ˈpreʃə/, issue /ˈɪʃuː/ (also /ˈɪsjuː/) |
| ch (French-origin words) | machine /məˈʃiːn/, chef /ʃef/, brochure /ˈbrəʊʃə/, moustache /məˈstɑːʃ/, parachute /ˈpærəʃuːt/, champagne /ʃæmˈpeɪn/ |
| sc | conscious /ˈkɒnʃəs/, conscience /ˈkɒnʃəns/ |
| x (= /k/ + /ʃ/) | anxious /ˈæŋkʃəs/ |

#### /ʒ/: voiced palato-alveolar fricative

The voiced partner of /ʃ/. It is the rarest English consonant and **almost never begins a native English word**.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| s, si (before u or ion) | measure /ˈmeʒə/, pleasure /ˈpleʒə/, treasure /ˈtreʒə/, leisure /ˈleʒə/, usual /ˈjuːʒuəl/, casual /ˈkæʒuəl/, vision /ˈvɪʒn/, decision /dɪˈsɪʒn/, television /ˈtelɪvɪʒn/, occasion /əˈkeɪʒn/ |
| ge (French-origin, word end) | beige /beɪʒ/, garage /ˈɡærɑːʒ/ (also /ˈɡærɪdʒ/), massage /ˈmæsɑːʒ/, rouge /ruːʒ/ |
| z | seizure /ˈsiːʒə/, azure /ˈæʒə/ |
| x (= /ɡ/ + /ʒ/) | luxurious /lʌɡˈʒʊəriəs/ |

**Compare:** *-sion* after a **vowel** is usually /ʒn/ (*vision, decision, occasion, explosion*); *-sion* after a **consonant** (*l, n*) or *-ssion* is usually /ʃn/ (*pension, tension, mission, passion, expulsion*).

#### /h/: voiceless glottal fricative

Air rushes through the open vocal cords, like breathing on a mirror to fog it.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| h | hat /hæt/, house /haʊs/, behind /bɪˈhaɪnd/, perhaps /pəˈhæps/ |
| wh (before o) | who /huː/, whole /həʊl/, whom /huːm/, whose /huːz/ |

/h/ only occurs **before a vowel**. It never ends an English word.

Silent h: *hour* /ˈaʊə/, *honest* /ˈɒnɪst/, *honour* /ˈɒnə/, *heir* /eə/, *vehicle* /ˈviːəkl/ (also /ˈviːɪkl/), *exhaust* /ɪɡˈzɔːst/, *exhibition* /ˌeksɪˈbɪʃn/, *ghost* /ɡəʊst/, *rhythm* /ˈrɪðəm/, *rhyme* /raɪm/, *what* /wɒt/, *when* /wen/. Because the h is silent, we say **an hour**, **an honest man**, **an heir**.

**Variation:** in RP, *wh-* words like *when, what, which, where* begin with /w/. A small number of speakers (and Scottish and some Irish accents) use /hw/. JAMB follows the /w/ pronunciation.

### Affricates

An affricate is a plosive released slowly into a fricative made at about the same place. English has two, and each counts as **one** consonant sound, even though its symbol has two parts.

| t (before u, or in -ture) | nature /ˈneɪtʃə/, picture /ˈpɪktʃə/, future /ˈfjuːtʃə/, culture /ˈkʌltʃə/, actual /ˈæktʃuəl/ |

#### /dʒ/: voiced palato-alveolar affricate

| Spellings | Examples |

| d (before u, or in -dier) | soldier /ˈsəʊldʒə/, gradual /ˈɡrædʒuəl/, procedure /prəˈsiːdʒə/, education /ˌedʒuˈkeɪʃn/ (also /ˌedjuˈkeɪʃn/) |
| -age (unstressed) | village /ˈvɪlɪdʒ/, message /ˈmesɪdʒ/, cabbage /ˈkæbɪdʒ/, college /ˈkɒlɪdʒ/ |

**The letter g:** usually /ɡ/ before *a, o, u* (*game, go, gun*) and /dʒ/ before *e, i, y* (*gem, ginger, gym*). But many common words break the rule: *get, give, girl, gift, begin, tiger, finger* have /ɡ/ before *e* or *i*.

### Nasals

For the nasals, the mouth is closed at some point and the **soft palate is lowered**, so air escapes through the nose. Try holding your nose while saying "mmm": the sound stops.

#### /m/: voiced bilabial nasal

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| m, mm | man /mæn/, home /həʊm/, summer /ˈsʌmə/ |
| mb, mn (silent b, n) | comb /kəʊm/, lamb /læm/, autumn /ˈɔːtəm/, column /ˈkɒləm/, hymn /hɪm/, condemn /kənˈdem/ |
| lm (silent l) | calm /kɑːm/, palm /pɑːm/, salmon /ˈsæmən/ |

#### /n/: voiced alveolar nasal

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| n, nn | no /nəʊ/, ten /ten/, dinner /ˈdɪnə/ |
| kn, gn, pn | know /nəʊ/, knife /naɪf/, gnaw /nɔː/, sign /saɪn/, pneumonia /njuːˈməʊniə/ |

#### /ŋ/: voiced velar nasal

The back of the tongue presses against the soft palate, as for /k/ and /ɡ/, but air goes through the nose. /ŋ/ **never begins** an English word.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| ng (word end) | sing /sɪŋ/, long /lɒŋ/, thing /θɪŋ/, bring /brɪŋ/, tongue /tʌŋ/ |
| n (before /k/ or /ɡ/) | think /θɪŋk/, bank /bæŋk/, uncle /ˈʌŋkl/, anchor /ˈæŋkə/, anxious /ˈæŋkʃəs/, finger /ˈfɪŋɡə/, English /ˈɪŋɡlɪʃ/ |

**/ŋ/ vs /ŋɡ/:** this is a key exam point.

| /ŋ/ only (no /ɡ/ sound) | /ŋɡ/ (the g is pronounced) |
| --- | --- |
| sing /sɪŋ/, singer /ˈsɪŋə/, singing /ˈsɪŋɪŋ/ | finger /ˈfɪŋɡə/, anger /ˈæŋɡə/, hunger /ˈhʌŋɡə/ |
| long /lɒŋ/, belonging /bɪˈlɒŋɪŋ/ | longer /ˈlɒŋɡə/, longest /ˈlɒŋɡɪst/ |
| hang /hæŋ/, hanger /ˈhæŋə/ | England /ˈɪŋɡlənd/, language /ˈlæŋɡwɪdʒ/ |
| strong /strɒŋ/, young /jʌŋ/ | stronger /ˈstrɒŋɡə/, younger /ˈjʌŋɡə/ |

**Rule:** at the end of a word, and when *-er* or *-ing* is added to a **verb** (*sing → singer, singing*), "ng" = /ŋ/ only. Inside a single unit of meaning (*finger, anger*) and in the comparative and superlative of *long, strong, young* (*longer, stronger, youngest*), "ng" = /ŋɡ/.

Many Nigerian speakers pronounce every "ng" as [ŋɡ], so *sing* sounds like [sɪŋɡ]. In RP, *sing* ends in /ŋ/ alone.

### The lateral

#### /l/: voiced alveolar lateral

Tongue tip on the alveolar ridge; air flows out around the sides of the tongue.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| l, ll | leg /leɡ/, look /lʊk/, ball /bɔːl/, hello /həˈləʊ/, little /ˈlɪtl/ |

**Clear l and dark l:** RP has two kinds of /l/:
- **Clear l** [l] **before a vowel**: *leg, look, believe*. The front of the tongue is raised.
- **Dark l** [ɫ] **after a vowel** (at the end of a word or before a consonant): *ball, milk, feel, help*. The back of the tongue is raised too, giving a "hollow" sound.

They are the **same phoneme** /l/. Dark l does not change meaning, so JAMB does not test it as a separate sound, but it affects how natural your speech sounds. Many Nigerian speakers use clear l everywhere.

Silent l: *walk* /wɔːk/, *talk* /tɔːk/, *chalk* /tʃɔːk/, *calm* /kɑːm/, *half* /hɑːf/, *calf* /kɑːf/, *could* /kʊd/, *should* /ʃʊd/, *would* /wʊd/, *folk* /fəʊk/, *yolk* /jəʊk/, *salmon* /ˈsæmən/, *almond* /ˈɑːmənd/.

**Syllabic l:** after /t, d, p, b, k, ɡ, s, z/ etc. at the end of words, RP often has a syllable with no vowel, just /l/: *bottle* /ˈbɒtl/, *little* /ˈlɪtl/, *middle* /ˈmɪdl/, *apple* /ˈæpl/, *table* /ˈteɪbl/. The same happens with /n/: *button* /ˈbʌtn/, *garden* /ˈɡɑːdn/, *listen* /ˈlɪsn/. Some books mark this with a small line under the consonant: /ˈbɒtl̩/.

### The approximants (semi-vowels)

#### /r/: voiced post-alveolar approximant

The tongue tip curls up towards (but does not touch) the area just behind the alveolar ridge. There is no tap or trill in RP.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| r, rr | red /red/, road /rəʊd/, carry /ˈkæri/, sorry /ˈsɒri/, very /ˈveri/ |
| wr | write /raɪt/, wrong /rɒŋ/, wrap /ræp/, wrist /rɪst/ |
| rh | rhyme /raɪm/, rhythm /ˈrɪðəm/, rhino /ˈraɪnəʊ/ |

**RP is non-rhotic:** /r/ is pronounced **only before a vowel sound**. In *car, card, bird, four, mother, here*, the written "r" is silent:

| Word | RP | "r" pronounced? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| red | /red/ | Yes (before a vowel) |
| car | /kɑː/ | No (end of word) |
| card | /kɑːd/ | No (before a consonant) |
| carry | /ˈkæri/ | Yes (before a vowel) |
| bird | /bɜːd/ | No |
| mother | /ˈmʌðə/ | No |

**Linking r:** when a word ending in a silent "r" is followed by a word beginning with a vowel, the /r/ is pronounced to link them: *far away* /fɑːr əˈweɪ/, *mother and father* /ˈmʌðər ən ˈfɑːðə/, *four o'clock* /fɔːr əˈklɒk/. This is part of "articulation of connected speech" (objective iii).

**Intrusive r:** some RP speakers also insert an /r/ where there is no "r" in the spelling, between two vowels: *law and order* [lɔːr ən ˈɔːdə], *idea of* [aɪˈdɪər əv]. It is common but is often regarded as careless; don't use it as a model.

**Symbol variation:** the IPA symbol for the English r sound is strictly **/ɹ/** (the IPA's /r/ is a rolled r). British dictionaries and textbooks, including the Cambridge English Pronouncing Dictionary, write **/r/** for simplicity. JAMB uses /r/.

Most Nigerian speakers pronounce every written "r" (rhotic speech), often as a tap [ɾ] or roll. That is fine in Nigerian English, but in JAMB questions about sounds, remember that RP does not pronounce the r in *car* or *bird*.

#### /w/: voiced labial-velar approximant (semi-vowel)

Lips rounded as for /uː/, back of the tongue raised, then glide into the following vowel.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| w | wet /wet/, wind /wɪnd/, away /əˈweɪ/ |
| wh | when /wen/, what /wɒt/, white /waɪt/, which /wɪtʃ/ |
| u (after q, g, s) | queen /kwiːn/, quick /kwɪk/, language /ˈlæŋɡwɪdʒ/, penguin /ˈpeŋɡwɪn/, persuade /pəˈsweɪd/, suite /swiːt/ |
| o (in "one", "once") | one /wʌn/, once /wʌns/ |
| oi (in "choir") | choir /ˈkwaɪə/ |

Silent w: *write, wrong, wrap, two* /tuː/, *answer* /ˈɑːnsə/, *sword* /sɔːd/, *who* /huː/, *whole* /həʊl/.

#### /j/: voiced palatal approximant (semi-vowel)

The "y" sound. Front of the tongue raised towards the hard palate, as for /iː/, then glide into the following vowel.

**Important:** the IPA symbol /j/ stands for the **"y" sound** in *yes*, **not** the "j" sound in *jam* (that is /dʒ/). This is a common mistake.

| Spellings | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| y | yes /jes/, yet /jet/, young /jʌŋ/, year /jɪə/, yellow /ˈjeləʊ/ |
| u, ew, eu, ue (as /juː/) | use /juːz/ (v), unit /ˈjuːnɪt/, music /ˈmjuːzɪk/, few /fjuː/, new /njuː/, Europe /ˈjʊərəp/, cute /kjuːt/, argue /ˈɑːɡjuː/, beauty /ˈbjuːti/, tube /tjuːb/ |
| i (before a vowel) | onion /ˈʌnjən/, opinion /əˈpɪnjən/ |

**So *unit, use, Europe, university* begin with a consonant sound /j/.** That is why we say "**a** university", "**a** European", "**a** unit", "**a** useful book", not "an". (Compare "**an** umbrella" /ʌ/, a vowel sound.)

**Variation:** in RP, /j/ is kept after /t d n/ in words like *tune* /tjuːn/, *duty* /ˈdjuːti/, *new* /njuː/, *student* /ˈstjuːdnt/. American English drops it (/tuːn, nuː/). Many younger RP speakers say /tʃuːn/, /dʒuːti/ (this is called **yod coalescence**). Dictionaries list both.

## Pronouncing the endings -ed and -s

These two endings are among the most tested points in JAMB Oral English. The rule depends on the **last sound** (not the last letter) of the base word.

### The past tense / past participle ending -ed

| Last sound of the base word | -ed is pronounced | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /t/ or /d/ | /ɪd/ (an extra syllable) | wanted /ˈwɒntɪd/, needed /ˈniːdɪd/, waited /ˈweɪtɪd/, decided /dɪˈsaɪdɪd/, started /ˈstɑːtɪd/ |
| Any other **voiceless** sound: /p k f θ s ʃ tʃ/ | /t/ | stopped /stɒpt/, walked /wɔːkt/, laughed /lɑːft/, kissed /kɪst/, washed /wɒʃt/, watched /wɒtʃt/, fixed /fɪkst/ |
| Any other **voiced** sound, including all vowels: /b ɡ v ð z ʒ dʒ m n ŋ l/ and vowels | /d/ | robbed /rɒbd/, begged /beɡd/, lived /lɪvd/, breathed /briːðd/, used /juːzd/, judged /dʒʌdʒd/, called /kɔːld/, played /pleɪd/, cried /kraɪd/, cleaned /kliːnd/ |

**Why?** /t/ after a voiceless sound and /d/ after a voiced sound keep the voicing the same, which is easier to say. /ɪd/ after /t/ and /d/ adds a vowel so the ending can be heard.

**Watch the sound, not the letter:** *laughed* ends in the sound /f/ (spelt "gh"), so -ed = /t/: /lɑːft/. *Fixed* ends in /s/ (from "x" = /ks/), so -ed = /t/: /fɪkst/.

**Exceptions: -ed adjectives with /ɪd/.** Some adjectives ending in -ed are pronounced /ɪd/ even after other sounds:

| Word | As an adjective | As a verb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| aged | /ˈeɪdʒɪd/ (very old: "an aged man") | /eɪdʒd/ ("he aged quickly", "aged ten") |
| learned | /ˈlɜːnɪd/ (scholarly: "a learned professor") | /lɜːnd/ |
| blessed | /ˈblesɪd/ ("the Blessed Virgin") | /blest/ |
| beloved | /bɪˈlʌvɪd/ (before a noun) | /bɪˈlʌvd/ |
| naked, wicked, crooked, ragged, rugged, sacred | /ˈneɪkɪd/, /ˈwɪkɪd/, /ˈkrʊkɪd/, /ˈræɡɪd/, /ˈrʌɡɪd/, /ˈseɪkrɪd/ | (the /ɪd/ form is for their use as adjectives) |

Many Nigerian speakers either drop the ending (*He walk home yesterday*) or pronounce every -ed as [ed] (*walked* as [wɔked]). Both are wrong in RP.

### The plural, possessive and verb ending -s / -es

The same rule applies to plural nouns (*cats*), possessives (*John's*) and third person singular verbs (*he runs*).

| Last sound of the base word | -s is pronounced | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A **sibilant** (hissing/hushing sound): /s z ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/ | /ɪz/ (extra syllable) | buses /ˈbʌsɪz/, roses /ˈrəʊzɪz/, wishes /ˈwɪʃɪz/, garages /ˈɡærɑːʒɪz/, watches /ˈwɒtʃɪz/, judges /ˈdʒʌdʒɪz/, boxes /ˈbɒksɪz/, George's /ˈdʒɔːdʒɪz/ |
| Any other **voiceless** sound: /p t k f θ/ | /s/ | cups /kʌps/, cats /kæts/, books /bʊks/, laughs /lɑːfs/, months /mʌnθs/, Pat's /pæts/ |
| Any other **voiced** sound, including all vowels | /z/ | dogs /dɒɡz/, bags /bæɡz/, pens /penz/, songs /sɒŋz/, boys /bɔɪz/, plays /pleɪz/, cars /kɑːz/, John's /dʒɒnz/, runs /rʌnz/ |

**Watch out:**
- *Says* is /sez/ and *does* is /dʌz/: the vowel changes as well.
- *Houses* is /ˈhaʊzɪz/: the /s/ of *house* becomes /z/ before the ending.
- *Baths* is /bɑːðz/ or /bɑːθs/; *mouths* is /maʊðz/; *paths* /pɑːðz/ or /pɑːθs/. These have two possible forms in RP; JAMB is unlikely to test them.

## Consonants Nigerian speakers commonly confuse (objective ii)

The consonant systems of Nigerian languages differ from English. Some English consonants do not exist in some Nigerian languages, so speakers replace them with the nearest sound they know. This is natural, and Nigerian English is understood across the country, but **JAMB tests the RP distinctions**. Practise them with minimal pairs.

### 1. /θ/ vs /t/ and /ð/ vs /d/

/θ/ and /ð/ are not found in Yoruba, Igbo or Hausa. Many Nigerian speakers replace /θ/ with [t] and /ð/ with [d], so *thin* sounds like *tin* and *they* like *day*.

**Fix:** put the tip of your tongue lightly between your teeth and blow gently. For /t/ the tongue presses the alveolar ridge and blocks the air completely; for /θ/ the tongue touches the teeth and air keeps flowing.

### 2. /ʃ/ vs /s/

Some speakers (this is often reported for some Yoruba speakers' English) use [s] for /ʃ/ (*shoe* sounds like *sue*), and some use [ʃ] for /s/ in other words. RP keeps them apart.

**Fix:** for /s/, the lips are spread and the tongue tip is close to the alveolar ridge (a high, sharp hiss). For /ʃ/, the lips are rounded and pushed forward and the tongue is pulled a little further back (a lower, "hushing" sound).

### 8. /h/ dropped or added

Some speakers (this is often reported for some Yoruba speakers' English) drop /h/ where it should be (*hair* said as *air*) and add it where it should not be (*air* said as *hair*, *egg* as [heɡ]).

| With /h/ | Without /h/ |
| --- | --- |
| heat /hiːt/ | eat /iːt/ |
| heart /hɑːt/ | art /ɑːt/ |
| hair /heə/ | air /eə/ |
| hold /həʊld/ | old /əʊld/ |
| hear /hɪə/ | ear /ɪə/ |
| harm /hɑːm/ | arm /ɑːm/ |
| hill /hɪl/ | ill /ɪl/ |

And remember the words where h is written but **silent**: *hour, honest, honour, heir*.

## How to answer JAMB consonant questions

### Question type 1: "same consonant sound as the letter(s) in italics"

> Choose the option that has the same consonant sound as the one represented by the letters in italics: ma*ch*ine
> A. chemist  B. church  C. chef  D. choir

**Step by step:**

1. Say the key word: *machine* /məˈʃiːn/. The "ch" is /ʃ/.
2. Say each option's "ch": chemist /k/, church /tʃ/, chef /ʃ/, choir /k/.
3. Match: **C (chef)**.
4. Remember: "ch" has three sounds; French-origin words (*machine, chef, brochure*) use /ʃ/.

### Question type 2: "which word contains the sound /x/?"

> Which of the following words contains /θ/?
> A. Thomas  B. Thames  C. theatre  D. those

Thomas /t/, Thames /t/, theatre /θ/, those /ð/. **Answer: C.**

### Question type 3: -ed and -s endings

> In which of the following words is -ed pronounced /ɪd/?
> A. walked  B. wanted  C. played  D. laughed

Only *wanted* ends in /t/ before the ending. **Answer: B.**

### Question type 4: silent letters

> In which of the following words is the letter in italics silent?
> A. ca*s*tle  B. lis*t*en  C. *t*aste  D. fas*t*

Say each word and listen for the italic letter: *castle* /ˈkɑːsl/ (s pronounced), *listen* /ˈlɪsn/ (t **silent**), *taste* /teɪst/ (t pronounced), *fast* /fɑːst/ (t pronounced). **Answer: B.** Always check exactly *which* letter is in italics: in *castle* it is the t that is silent, not the s.

### Question type 5: describe a consonant

> /v/ is a
> A. voiceless labiodental fricative  B. voiced labiodental fricative  C. voiced bilabial plosive  D. voiced dental fricative

**Answer: B.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Consonant | A speech sound made by blocking or narrowing the airflow somewhere in the mouth or throat |
| Voiced | Made with the vocal cords vibrating, e.g. /b/, /z/, /v/ |
| Voiceless | Made without vibration of the vocal cords, e.g. /p/, /s/, /f/ |
| Place of articulation | Where in the mouth the air is blocked or narrowed, e.g. bilabial, alveolar, velar |
| Manner of articulation | How the air is blocked or narrowed, e.g. plosive, fricative, nasal |
| Plosive (stop) | A consonant made by completely blocking the air and releasing it suddenly: /p b t d k ɡ/ |
| Fricative | A consonant made by forcing air through a narrow gap, producing friction: /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/ |
| Affricate | A plosive released slowly into a fricative: /tʃ dʒ/ |
| Nasal | A consonant in which air passes out through the nose: /m n ŋ/ |
| Lateral | A consonant in which air passes over the sides of the tongue: /l/ |
| Approximant / semi-vowel | A consonant made with the organs close together but without friction: /r w j/ |
| Alveolar ridge | The bony ridge just behind the upper front teeth |
| Sibilant | A hissing or hushing sound: /s z ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/ |
| Aspiration | The small puff of air after /p t k/ at the start of a stressed syllable |
| Non-rhotic | An accent (like RP) in which written "r" is pronounced only before a vowel |
| Linking r | An /r/ pronounced at the end of a word because the next word begins with a vowel, e.g. "far away" |
| Silent letter | A letter that is written but not pronounced, e.g. the k in "know" |
| Syllabic consonant | A consonant (usually /l/ or /n/) that forms a syllable on its own, e.g. the /l/ in "bottle" |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Know the three-term label** (voicing, place, manner) for all 24 consonants. JAMB may ask, for example, "Which of these is a voiced velar plosive?" (/ɡ/).
- **/j/ is the "y" sound**, not the "j" sound. *Jam* begins with /dʒ/; *yes* begins with /j/.
- **"ch" has three sounds:** /tʃ/ (church), /k/ (chemist, choir), /ʃ/ (machine, chef).
- **"th" has two main sounds:** /θ/ (thin, thank, author) and /ð/ (this, mother, breathe); and /t/ in *Thomas, Thames*.
- **-ed endings:** /ɪd/ after /t d/; /t/ after other voiceless sounds; /d/ after other voiced sounds. Look at the last **sound**, not the last letter.
- **-s endings:** /ɪz/ after /s z ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/; /s/ after other voiceless sounds; /z/ after other voiced sounds.
- **Silent letters** are a favourite trap: *k* in *know*, *b* in *debt*, *p* in *psychology*, *h* in *honest*, *t* in *listen*, *w* in *sword*, *l* in *calm*.
- **Don't confuse /θ/ with /t/ or /ð/ with /d/,** /ʃ/ with /s/ or /tʃ/, /v/ with /f/, /f/ with /p/, /z/ with /s/, /l/ with /r/. Practise the minimal pairs aloud.
- **RP does not pronounce "r" before a consonant or at the end of a word** (car, bird, mother) unless the next word begins with a vowel (linking r).
- **Textbooks differ in names:** palato-alveolar or post-alveolar for /ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/; semi-vowel or approximant for /w j/; bilabial or labial-velar for /w/. Recognise all of them.

## Quick summary

1. RP has 24 consonant sounds, written with IPA symbols; English spelling does not match the sounds one-to-one, and many letters are silent.
2. Every consonant is described by voicing (voiced or voiceless), place of articulation (e.g. bilabial, dental, alveolar, velar, glottal) and manner of articulation (e.g. plosive, fricative, affricate, nasal, lateral, approximant).
3. The 24 consonants are: plosives /p b t d k ɡ/, fricatives /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/, affricates /tʃ dʒ/, nasals /m n ŋ/, lateral /l/ and approximants /r w j/.
4. The -ed ending is /ɪd/ after /t d/, /t/ after other voiceless sounds and /d/ after other voiced sounds; the -s ending is /ɪz/ after sibilants, /s/ after other voiceless sounds and /z/ after other voiced sounds.
5. Letter groups such as ch, th, gh, c, g, s and x stand for several different sounds; always answer from the sound.
6. Nigerian speakers commonly confuse /θ/–/t/, /ð/–/d/, /ʃ/–/s/, /ʃ/–/tʃ/, /v/–/f/, /f/–/p/, /z/–/s/ and /l/–/r/, and drop or add /h/; JAMB tests the RP distinctions.
7. RP is non-rhotic: written "r" is pronounced only before a vowel sound, including across word boundaries (linking r).

## Practice questions

In questions 2, 5 and 6, the tested letters are in *italics* (JAMB underlines them).

**1. Which of the following words contains the consonant /θ/?**

- A. Thomas
- B. Thames
- C. theatre
- D. those

**Answer: C.** *Theatre* /ˈθɪətə/ begins with /θ/. *Thomas* and *Thames* begin with /t/; *those* begins with /ð/.

**2. Choose the option that has the same consonant sound as the one represented by the letters in italics: ma*ch*ine**

- A. chemist
- B. church
- C. chef
- D. choir

**Answer: C.** The "ch" in *machine* /məˈʃiːn/ is /ʃ/, as in *chef* /ʃef/. *Chemist* and *choir* have /k/; *church* has /tʃ/.

**3. In which of the following words is the -ed ending pronounced /ɪd/?**

- A. walked
- B. wanted
- C. played
- D. laughed

**Answer: B.** *Wanted* /ˈwɒntɪd/: after /t/, -ed is /ɪd/. *Walked* and *laughed* end in /t/ (after voiceless sounds); *played* ends in /d/ (after a vowel).

**4. In which of the following words is the -es ending pronounced /ɪz/?**

- A. books
- B. dogs
- C. watches
- D. cups

**Answer: C.** *Watches* /ˈwɒtʃɪz/: after the sibilant /tʃ/, the ending is /ɪz/. *Books* and *cups* end in /s/; *dogs* ends in /z/.

**5. In which of the following words is the letter in italics silent?**

- A. lis*t*en
- B. mis*t*ake
- C. tas*t*e
- D. fas*t*

**Answer: A.** *Listen* /ˈlɪsn/: the t is silent. In *mistake*, *taste* and *fast*, the t is pronounced.

**6. Choose the option that has the same consonant sound as the one represented by the letter in italics: mea*s*ure**

- A. pressure
- B. vision
- C. passion
- D. sure

**Answer: B.** The "s" in *measure* /ˈmeʒə/ is /ʒ/, as is the "s" in *vision* /ˈvɪʒn/. *Pressure, passion* and *sure* have /ʃ/.

**7. Which of the following consonants is voiced?**

- A. /f/
- B. /θ/
- C. /v/
- D. /s/

**Answer: C.** /v/ is a voiced labiodental fricative. /f/, /θ/ and /s/ are voiceless.

**8. Which of the following words begins with the sound /k/?**

- A. cheap
- B. character
- C. cello
- D. chef

**Answer: B.** *Character* /ˈkærəktə/ begins with /k/. *Cheap* and *cello* begin with /tʃ/; *chef* begins with /ʃ/.

**9. Which of the following words contains the same consonant sound as the "ph" in *phone*?**

- A. shepherd
- B. laugh
- C. Stephen
- D. uphill

**Answer: B.** The "gh" in *laugh* /lɑːf/ is /f/, like "ph" in *phone* /fəʊn/. *Shepherd* has /p/, *Stephen* has /v/ and *uphill* has /p/ + /h/.

**10. In which of the following words is "ng" pronounced /ŋ/ without a following /ɡ/?**

- A. finger
- B. singer
- C. longer
- D. anger

**Answer: B.** *Singer* /ˈsɪŋə/ is *sing* + *-er* (a verb + ending), so the g is not pronounced. *Finger* /ˈfɪŋɡə/, *longer* /ˈlɒŋɡə/ (comparative) and *anger* /ˈæŋɡə/ all have /ŋɡ/.
