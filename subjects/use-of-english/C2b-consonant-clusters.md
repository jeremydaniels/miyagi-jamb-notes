# JAMB Use of English Notes: Consonant Clusters

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section C (Oral Forms), content item (b) of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: **Consonants (including clusters)**. The 24 single consonant sounds are covered in `C2a-consonants.md`. These notes cover **consonant clusters**: groups of consonant sounds that come together in a syllable with no vowel between them.

The syllabus objectives for Oral Forms are:

1. make distinctions among vowel types;
2. differentiate among consonant types; and
3. identify correct pronunciation of individual words and articulation of connected speech.

Objectives 2 and 3 apply here. Clusters fall under the **2 consonant questions** in the exam. They also affect how you count sounds and syllables, and how you pronounce endings like *-ed* and *-s*.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Explain what a consonant cluster is, and how it differs from a digraph (two letters, one sound).
2. Describe the structure of an English syllable (onset, nucleus, coda).
3. List the possible two- and three-consonant clusters at the beginning of English words, and the main clusters at the end.
4. Count the consonant sounds in a cluster correctly, ignoring silent letters.
5. Recognise how clusters are simplified in connected speech, and how Nigerian speakers often change them (by adding vowels or dropping consonants).
6. Answer JAMB-style questions on clusters.

> **How italics are used in these notes:** JAMB prints the tested letters underlined. In these notes, the tested letters are shown in *italics*.

> **Stress marking:** in transcriptions, **ˈ** comes before the stressed syllable, e.g. /ˈstrenθ/ is a single stressed syllable.

## What is a consonant cluster?

A **consonant cluster** is a sequence of **two or more consonant sounds** with **no vowel sound** between them, in the same syllable.

| Word | Transcription | Cluster | Number of consonant sounds in the cluster |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| play | /pleɪ/ | /pl/ at the start | 2 |
| street | /striːt/ | /str/ at the start | 3 |
| hand | /hænd/ | /nd/ at the end | 2 |
| texts | /teksts/ | /ksts/ at the end | 4 |

### Clusters are about sounds, not letters

This is where most mistakes happen. A cluster is counted in **sounds**, so you must ignore how the word is spelt.

**1. Two letters can make one sound (a digraph).** These are **not** clusters:

| Word | Letters | Sound | Cluster? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ship | sh | /ʃ/ | No, one sound |
| thin | th | /θ/ | No, one sound |
| chop | ch | /tʃ/ | No, one sound (an affricate counts as one consonant) |
| phone | ph | /f/ | No, one sound |
| sing | ng | /ŋ/ | No, one sound |
| back | ck | /k/ | No, one sound |
| judge | dge | /dʒ/ | No, one sound |

**2. Silent letters don't count:**

| Word | Spelling suggests | Actual sounds | Cluster? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| know | kn | /n/ | No |
| write | wr | /r/ | No |
| psychology | ps | /s/ | No |
| comb | mb | /m/ | No |
| listen | st | /s/ (t silent) | No |
| gnaw | gn | /n/ | No |

**3. One letter can make two sounds**, so a word can have a cluster even if you see only one consonant letter:

| Word | Letter | Sounds | Cluster |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| box | x | /ks/ | /ks/ at the end |
| exam | x | /ɡz/ | /ɡz/ across the syllables (/ɪɡ.ˈzæm/) |
| queen | qu | /kw/ | /kw/ at the start |
| music | u | /j/ + /uː/ | /mj/ at the start |
| cute | u | /j/ + /uː/ | /kj/ at the start |

**4. Two letters and two sounds** form a real cluster: *bl* in *blue* /bluː/, *st* in *stop* /stɒp/, *nd* in *and* /ænd/.

**5. Digraph + another consonant can make a cluster:** *thr* in *three* /θr/ (2 sounds), *shr* in *shrink* /ʃr/ (2 sounds), *nch* in *lunch* /ntʃ/ (2 sounds).

## The structure of an English syllable

To understand clusters, you need the parts of a syllable:

| Part | What it is | Example: *street* /striːt/ | Example: *texts* /teksts/ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | The consonant(s) **before** the vowel | /str/ | /t/ |
| Nucleus | The vowel (the centre of the syllable) | /iː/ | /e/ |
| Coda | The consonant(s) **after** the vowel | /t/ | /ksts/ |

The **nucleus** must be present; the onset and coda are optional. (A syllabic consonant such as /l/ in *bottle* /ˈbɒtl/ can also act as a nucleus.)

### English syllable formula

An English syllable can have:

- **0 to 3** consonants in the onset, and
- **0 to 4** consonants in the coda.

This is often written as **(C)(C)(C)V(C)(C)(C)(C)**, where C = consonant and V = vowel, and brackets mean "optional".

| Pattern | Example | Transcription |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V | eye | /aɪ/ |
| CV | go | /ɡəʊ/ |
| VC | egg | /eɡ/ |
| CVC | cat | /kæt/ |
| CCV | tree | /triː/ |
| CCCV | spray | /spreɪ/ |
| CVCC | hand | /hænd/ |
| CCVCC | trust | /trʌst/ |
| CCCVCC | strict | /strɪkt/ |
| CVCCC | next | /nekst/ |
| CCVCCC | glimpsed | /ɡlɪmpst/ |
| CVCCCC | texts | /teksts/ |
| CCCVCCC | strengths | /streŋθs/ (also /streŋkθs/) |

### Comparing English with Nigerian languages

| Feature | English | Yoruba and Igbo (typical) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usual syllable shape | Many shapes, up to CCCVCCCC | Mostly CV or V (open syllables); syllabic nasals also occur |
| Consonant clusters at the start | Yes, up to 3 (e.g. /str/) | Generally not, in the native vocabulary |
| Consonant clusters at the end | Yes, up to 4 (e.g. /ksts/) | No; syllables usually end in a vowel |

Hausa allows some closed syllables (ending in a consonant), but it does not have long English-type clusters either. This difference is the main reason Nigerian speakers find English clusters hard, and it explains the common changes described later.

### Three-consonant initial clusters

All English three-consonant initial clusters follow **one pattern**:

> **/s/ + voiceless plosive (/p/, /t/ or /k/) + approximant (/l/, /r/, /w/ or /j/)**

| Cluster | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| /spl/ | splash /splæʃ/, split /splɪt/, splendid /ˈsplendɪd/ |
| /spr/ | spring /sprɪŋ/, spray /spreɪ/, spread /spred/ |
| /spj/ | spew /spjuː/, spurious /ˈspjʊəriəs/ |
| /str/ | street /striːt/, strong /strɒŋ/, string /strɪŋ/ |
| /stj/ | student /ˈstjuːdnt/, stupid /ˈstjuːpɪd/ (RP; also /ˈstʃuː-/) |
| /skr/ | scream /skriːm/, screw /skruː/, script /skrɪpt/ |
| /skw/ | square /skweə/, squeeze /skwiːz/, squad /skwɒd/ |
| /skj/ | skew /skjuː/ |

There is no four-consonant cluster at the start of an English word.

**Rules worth remembering:**
- /ŋ/ **never** begins a syllable (so no /ŋk/ or /ŋɡ/ at the start).
- /ʒ/ almost never begins an English word (only in loanwords like *genre* /ˈʒɒnrə/).
- If an initial cluster has three consonants, the first is **always /s/**.

### Four-consonant final clusters

These are rare, but they exist:

| Cluster | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| /ksts/ | texts /teksts/, contexts /ˈkɒnteksts/ |
| /ksθs/ | sixths /sɪksθs/ |
| /mpts/ | tempts /tempts/, attempts /əˈtempts/ |
| /mpst/ | glimpsed /ɡlɪmpst/ |
| /lfθs/ | twelfths /twelfθs/ |

Note how many of the longest clusters are made by **adding endings** (-s, -ed, -th) to words that already end in a cluster: *text* → *texts*, *six* → *sixth* → *sixths*, *tempt* → *tempts*.

## How grammatical endings create clusters

The endings -s, -ed and -th (from C2a) often turn one consonant into a cluster, or make a cluster longer:

| Base word | Base ending | With ending | New cluster |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ask /ɑːsk/ | /sk/ | asked /ɑːskt/ | /skt/ |
| ask | /sk/ | asks /ɑːsks/ | /sks/ |
| test /test/ | /st/ | tests /tests/ | /sts/ |
| help /help/ | /lp/ | helped /helpt/ | /lpt/ |
| month /mʌnθ/ | /nθ/ | months /mʌnθs/ | /nθs/ |
| fix /fɪks/ | /ks/ | fixed /fɪkst/ | /kst/ |
| six /sɪks/ | /ks/ | sixth /sɪksθ/ | /ksθ/ |
| strength /streŋθ/ | /ŋθ/ | strengths /streŋθs/ | /ŋθs/ |

Because of this, dropping the last consonant of a cluster can change the **grammar** of what you say: *He asks* (present) vs *He asked* (past); *one test* vs *two tests*. In RP, all these consonants should be heard in careful speech.

## Clusters across syllables and words

A cluster belongs to **one syllable**. When consonants meet across a syllable boundary, they are not strictly a single cluster, but they still need to be pronounced without inserting a vowel:

| Word or phrase | Transcription | Consonants that meet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| extra | /ˈekstrə/ | /k/ + /str/ |
| instruct | /ɪnˈstrʌkt/ | /n/ + /str/ |
| exclaim | /ɪkˈskleɪm/ | /k/ + /skl/ |
| next street | /nekst striːt/ | /kst/ + /str/ |
| asked questions | /ɑːskt ˈkwestʃənz/ | /skt/ + /kw/ |

## Cluster simplification in connected speech (objective iii: articulation of connected speech)

Native RP speakers also simplify some clusters in **fast, natural speech**. This is called **elision** (the dropping of a sound). The most common type is dropping /t/ or /d/ when it is in the **middle of three consonants**:

| Careful speech | Fast natural RP | What is dropped |
| --- | --- | --- |
| next day /nekst deɪ/ | [neks deɪ] | /t/ |
| last night /lɑːst naɪt/ | [lɑːs naɪt] | /t/ |
| handbag /ˈhændbæɡ/ | [ˈhæmbæɡ] | /d/ (and /n/ becomes [m]) |
| exactly /ɪɡˈzæktli/ | [ɪɡˈzækli] | /t/ |
| mostly /ˈməʊstli/ | [ˈməʊsli] | /t/ |
| friendship /ˈfrendʃɪp/ | [ˈfrenʃɪp] | /d/ |

**Important differences from the Nigerian simplifications below:**
- RP elision happens in **fast speech** and mainly between words or inside long clusters. In careful speech, the full form is used.
- RP speakers do not drop grammatical endings at the end of a sentence: "*She asked.*" keeps the /t/.
- RP speakers **never add a vowel** to break a cluster.

JAMB tests the **careful (citation)** form of words, so count every consonant in the dictionary form.

## How Nigerian speakers often change clusters

Because Yoruba, Igbo and many other Nigerian languages mostly have open CV syllables, speakers often reshape English clusters in two ways. Both are understood in Nigeria, but both are **non-standard** in RP.

### 1. Adding a vowel (vowel insertion, or epenthesis)

A vowel is inserted to break up the cluster, or added at the end of a word:

| Word | RP | Common non-standard form | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| film | /fɪlm/ | [fi-lim] | vowel inserted between /l/ and /m/ |
| school | /skuːl/ | [si-kul] | vowel inserted between /s/ and /k/ |
| spread | /spred/ | [si-pred] | vowel inserted after /s/ |
| street | /striːt/ | [si-tri-ti] | vowels inserted inside the cluster and after the final /t/ |
| bread | /bred/ | [bu-red] | vowel inserted between /b/ and /r/ |
| milk | /mɪlk/ | [mi-li-ki] | vowels inserted and added at the end |
| sport | /spɔːt/ | [si-pɔt] | vowel inserted after /s/ |

**Fix:** say the consonants smoothly with no breath or vowel between them. Practise slowly: *s-t-r-eet*, keeping the tongue moving from one consonant to the next without opening the mouth for a vowel.

### 2. Dropping a consonant (consonant deletion)

One or more consonants, usually at the **end** of a word, are left out:

| Word | RP | Common non-standard form | What is lost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| asked | /ɑːskt/ | [ask] or [as] | /t/ (the past tense) and sometimes /k/ |
| tests | /tests/ | [tes] | /t/ and /s/ (the plural) |
| next | /nekst/ | [nes] | /k/ and /t/ |
| wants | /wɒnts/ | [wɔn] | /t/ and /s/ |
| months | /mʌnθs/ | [mɔns] | /θ/ (and /ʌ/ changed) |
| hands | /hændz/ | [hans] | /d/ |
| sixth | /sɪksθ/ | [siks] | /θ/ |
| world | /wɜːld/ | [wɔl] | /d/ |

This matters because the dropped consonant often carries meaning: past tense (-ed), plural (-s), third person (-s) or ordinal number (-th). *I asked him* and *I ask him* are different sentences.

### 3. Other cluster changes

- **/θ/ and /ð/ in clusters** are replaced by [t] and [d]: *three* /θriː/ → [tri], *months* /mʌnθs/ → [mɔnts]. (See C2a.)
- **Pronouncing silent letters** as if they formed a cluster: *knife* as [knaif], *psalm* as [psalm], *listen* as [listen]. RP: /naɪf/, /sɑːm/, /ˈlɪsn/.
- **Adding /ɡ/ after /ŋ/:** *sing* /sɪŋ/ said as [siŋɡ], which creates a false cluster. (See C2a.)

## How to answer JAMB questions on clusters

JAMB questions on clusters are less common than questions on single sounds, but they appear in several forms. Here is how to approach each.

### Type 1: Which word has a cluster of a given size?

> Which of the following words begins with a three-consonant cluster?
> A. school  B. spring  C. shrink  D. three

**Steps:**
1. Transcribe the start of each word: school /sk/ (2), spring /spr/ (3), shrink /ʃr/ (2: sh is one sound), three /θr/ (2: th is one sound).
2. **Answer: B.**
3. Trap: *shrink* and *three* have three or four letters before the vowel, but only two **sounds**.

### Type 2: Count the consonant sounds

> How many consonant sounds are there at the end of the word *asked*?
> A. 1  B. 2  C. 3  D. 4

*asked* = /ɑːskt/: /s/, /k/, /t/. **Answer: C.** (The "e" is not pronounced, and the "-ed" here is /t/.)

### Type 3: Spot the cluster hidden in one letter

> Which of the following words contains a consonant cluster, although it is spelt with only one consonant letter after the vowel?
> A. box  B. bus  C. bat  D. bed

*box* = /bɒks/: the letter x = /ks/. **Answer: A.**

### Type 4: Which word does NOT begin with a cluster?

> Which of the following words does NOT begin with a consonant cluster?
> A. blue  B. thin  C. try  D. queen

blue /bl/, thin /θ/ (one sound), try /tr/, queen /kw/. **Answer: B.**

### Type 5: Possible and impossible clusters

> Which of the following is NOT a possible initial consonant cluster in English?
> A. /str/  B. /spl/  C. /ŋk/  D. /skw/

/ŋ/ can never begin an English syllable. **Answer: C.**

### Checklist for cluster questions

1. **Write or picture the transcription.** Don't trust the spelling.
2. **Remove silent letters** (*k*now, *w*rite, com*b*, lis*t*en).
3. **Treat digraphs as one sound** (sh, th, ch, ph, ng, ck).
4. **Split letters that stand for two sounds** (x = /ks/ or /ɡz/; qu = /kw/; u = /juː/).
5. **Count only the consonants in the same syllable,** next to each other with no vowel between.

## Comparing related terms

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consonant cluster | Two or more consonant **sounds** together in one syllable | /str/ in *street* |
| Digraph | Two **letters** that stand for one sound | *sh* in *ship* = /ʃ/ |
| Affricate | One consonant sound made of a stop + fricative; counts as one sound, not a cluster | /tʃ/ in *chop* |
| Silent letter | A letter that is written but not pronounced | *k* in *know* |
| Epenthesis | Adding a vowel to break a cluster (non-standard in RP) | *film* as [filim] |
| Elision | Dropping a sound in connected speech (normal in fast RP, within limits) | *next day* as [neks deɪ] |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Consonant cluster | Two or more consonant sounds with no vowel between them in the same syllable, e.g. /spr/ in "spring" |
| Initial cluster | A cluster at the beginning of a syllable (in the onset), e.g. /bl/ in "blue" |
| Final cluster | A cluster at the end of a syllable (in the coda), e.g. /nd/ in "hand" |
| Syllable | A unit of pronunciation built around one vowel sound (or syllabic consonant) |
| Onset | The consonant(s) before the vowel in a syllable |
| Nucleus | The vowel (centre) of a syllable |
| Coda | The consonant(s) after the vowel in a syllable |
| Open syllable | A syllable that ends in a vowel (no coda), e.g. "go" |
| Closed syllable | A syllable that ends in a consonant, e.g. "cat" |
| Digraph | Two letters representing one sound, e.g. "th", "sh", "ph" |
| Epenthesis (vowel insertion) | Adding a vowel to break up a cluster, e.g. "film" as [filim] |
| Elision | Leaving out a sound in connected speech, e.g. /t/ in "next day" |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Count sounds, not letters.** *Shrink* begins with 2 consonant sounds (/ʃr/), not 3; *three* begins with 2 (/θr/); *knife* begins with 1 (/n/).
- **Silent letters are not part of a cluster:** *know, write, psalm, comb, listen*.
- **One letter can be two sounds:** x = /ks/ (box) or /ɡz/ (exam); qu = /kw/ (queen); u can be /juː/ (music, cute), giving clusters such as /mj/ and /kj/.
- **Three-consonant initial clusters always start with /s/,** then /p/, /t/ or /k/, then /l/, /r/, /w/ or /j/: spl, spr, str, skr, skw, spj, stj, skj.
- **/ŋ/ never begins a syllable,** so any option with /ŋ/ at the start is impossible.
- **Final clusters can have up to 4 consonants:** texts /ksts/, sixths /ksθs/, twelfths /lfθs/, glimpsed /mpst/.
- **Don't add vowels** to break clusters (*film, school, milk*) and **don't drop endings** (*asked, tests, months*). These are the two most common Nigerian cluster errors.
- **JAMB uses citation (dictionary) forms.** Count every consonant in the careful pronunciation, even if fast speech drops one.

## Quick summary

1. A consonant cluster is a group of two or more consonant sounds with no vowel between them in the same syllable.
2. Clusters are counted in sounds: digraphs (sh, th, ch, ph, ng) are one sound, silent letters don't count, and x or qu can give two sounds.
3. An English syllable has an onset, a nucleus and a coda, and can be as large as CCCVCCCC.
4. Initial clusters have up to 3 consonants, and every three-consonant initial cluster is /s/ + /p, t, k/ + /l, r, w, j/.
5. Final clusters have up to 4 consonants (e.g. texts /teksts/), and many are created by the endings -s, -ed and -th.
6. Fast RP speech sometimes drops /t/ or /d/ in the middle of a cluster (elision), but never inserts a vowel.
7. Nigerian speakers often add vowels (film → [filim]) or drop consonants (asked → [ask]) because most Nigerian languages use open CV syllables; JAMB expects the full RP clusters.

## Practice questions

**1. Which of the following words begins with a three-consonant cluster?**

- A. school
- B. spring
- C. shrink
- D. three

**Answer: B.** *Spring* /sprɪŋ/ begins with /spr/. *School* has /sk/ (2), *shrink* has /ʃr/ (2) and *three* has /θr/ (2), because "sh" and "th" are single sounds.

**2. Which of the following words ends with the cluster /ksts/?**

- A. sixths
- B. texts
- C. tests
- D. desks

**Answer: B.** *Texts* /teksts/. *Sixths* ends in /ksθs/, *tests* in /sts/ and *desks* in /sks/.

**3. Which of the following words does NOT begin with a consonant cluster?**

- A. blue
- B. thin
- C. try
- D. queen

**Answer: B.** *Thin* begins with the single sound /θ/. *Blue* (/bl/), *try* (/tr/) and *queen* (/kw/) all begin with clusters.

**4. How many consonant sounds are there at the end of the word "asked"?**

- A. 1
- B. 2
- C. 3
- D. 4

**Answer: C.** *Asked* is /ɑːskt/, ending in /s/, /k/ and /t/. The letter "e" is silent and "-ed" is pronounced /t/.

**5. Which of the following words contains a consonant cluster although only one consonant letter follows the vowel?**

- A. box
- B. bus
- C. bat
- D. ship

**Answer: A.** In *box* /bɒks/, the letter x stands for two sounds, /k/ and /s/. *Bus*, *bat* and *ship* each end in one consonant sound.

**6. Which of the following words has a different number of consonant sounds before its vowel from the others?**

- A. knife
- B. snake
- C. smile
- D. sleep

**Answer: A.** *Knife* /naɪf/ begins with one sound, /n/ (the k is silent). *Snake* (/sn/), *smile* (/sm/) and *sleep* (/sl/) each begin with two.

**7. Which of the following is NOT a possible initial consonant cluster in English?**

- A. /str/
- B. /spl/
- C. /ŋk/
- D. /skw/

**Answer: C.** /ŋ/ never begins an English syllable. /str/ (street), /spl/ (splash) and /skw/ (square) are all possible.

**8. Which of the following words ends with the cluster /mpt/?**

- A. jumped
- B. lamb
- C. jump
- D. comb

**Answer: A.** *Jumped* /dʒʌmpt/ ends in /mpt/. *Lamb* and *comb* end in /m/ only (the b is silent); *jump* ends in /mp/.

**9. Which of the following words has the largest number of consonant sounds before its vowel?**

- A. tree
- B. street
- C. seat
- D. stay

**Answer: B.** *Street* /striːt/ has three (/s/, /t/, /r/). *Tree* (/tr/) and *stay* (/st/) have two; *seat* has one.

**10. Which of the following words ends in a consonant cluster?**

- A. knock
- B. comb
- C. sign
- D. world

**Answer: D.** *World* /wɜːld/ ends in /ld/ (the r is not pronounced in RP). *Knock* ends in /k/, *comb* in /m/ and *sign* in /n/, because the c, b and g are not separate sounds there.
