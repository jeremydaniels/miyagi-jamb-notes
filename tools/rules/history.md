## History-specific rules (these override the style example wherever they differ)

CORRECTNESS
- NEVER INVENT dates, names, figures, treaties, battles, titles, quotations or statistics. Use only what you are confident is correct. If you are unsure of a date, a name, a spelling, a number (such as casualties, populations, soldiers, treaty terms or years of reign), leave it out and describe the event by period (for example "in the early 19th century" or "in the 1960s") or by what happened. Prefer "about" or "around" for approximate figures, and never present a guess as a fact.
- HEDGE anything uncertain or disputed. Use wording such as "historians disagree", "most textbooks say", "according to oral tradition", "the exact date is uncertain" or "sources differ". Where oral tradition, written sources and archaeology tell different stories (for example the origins of the Hausa states, Kanuri and Borno, Ife, Benin and Oyo, Nok dating, the origins of the Igbo and the Efik, the date and course of the Sokoto Jihad's early years, the Yoruba wars), say so and explain the main views fairly.
- FLAG WHERE TEXTBOOKS DIFFER. Spellings and names vary (Usman dan Fodio and Uthman dan Fodio; Seku Ahmadu and Seku Amadu; Al-Hajj Umar and Umar Tall; Samori Toure and Samori Ture; Theodore II and Tewodros II; Mohammed Ali and Muhammad Ali; Lyttleton and Lyttelton): give the form the syllabus uses first, then the common alternatives. Say when dates or the order of events differ between textbooks (for example the dates of the Ekumeku resistance, the phases of the British conquest, which census figures were announced, the exact details of the coups of 1966).
- Use only the dates the syllabus itself prints (for example 1800, 1851, 1900, 1898, 1911, 1906, 1914, 1918, 1922, 1929, 1946, 1951, 1954, 1957, 1958, 1959, 1960, 1962/63, 1963, 1964/65, 1966, 1886) and other dates you are certain of. A date you are not sure of is described only by period.
- The Transcription notes list printed errors and mismatches. Follow them: teach the objective as the likely intended topic (for example "coastal areas", "Mandinka Empire"), mention the OAU and the African Union together, and where a topic has no printed contents (the Gowon, Murtala/Obasanjo, Second Republic, Buhari, Babangida, ING, Abacha and Abdulsalami topics) use the objectives and the topic title as the scope, covering the main events, achievements and challenges.
- Facts about recent history are sensitive and disputed. Be neutral, evidence-based and respectful. Do not give party-political opinions. Do not blame a whole ethnic group or religion. Describe the causes and effects of the Civil War, the coups, the military regimes and the transition to civilian rule fairly, giving the main views. Do not name current office-holders.
- Never give quotations unless they are short, famous and certain; otherwise paraphrase.
- Do not rely on one textbook's view where historians disagree; the notes may name the commonly used view in Nigerian school textbooks while saying that others exist.

HOW TO TEACH
- Write as a clear story in simple English for Nigerian secondary school students: what happened, when (by period, or by a date you are certain of), who was involved, why it happened (causes), how it unfolded (course) and what followed (effects and significance). Use headings that match the objectives.
- Define every term (for example jihad, caliphate, emirate, acephalous, indirect rule, pacification, consular authority, Mfecane, apartheid, neo-colonialism) the first time it appears. Give maps, timelines and cause-and-effect tables where they help.
- Explain every content item, named example and objective fully with its own heading. Use comparison tables wherever the syllabus says compare, differentiate, distinguish or contrast (for example centralized and non-centralized states, the Jihads of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar, patterns of British, French, Portuguese, Belgian, German and Spanish colonial rule, the constitutions of 1922, 1946, 1951 and 1954).
- Add an `[IMAGE: ...]` placeholder wherever a map, timeline, chart or illustration helps (the geographical zones, the early centres of civilization, trade routes, the Sokoto Caliphate emirates, the amalgamation, the Civil War fronts, the scramble for Africa, the Mfecane, the Great Trek), with a detailed description including every label, region, line and date you are sure of. Describe maps broadly; never invent exact borders or distances.
- Include a "Common mistakes" note in each major section (for example mixing up the Sokoto Jihad and the Jihads of Seku Ahmadu and Al-Hajj Umar, the amalgamation and indirect rule, the Clifford, Richards and Macpherson constitutions, the first and second coups of 1966).
- Use Nigerian and African examples and connect the past to the present, as the general objectives ask, without adding recent claims you cannot verify. For recent topics, say "at the time of writing".

PRACTICE QUESTIONS (exactly 10)
- Real JAMB style: short multiple-choice questions like past UTME papers, mixing easy, medium and harder items and covering all objectives of the file (people, places, causes, effects, comparisons and significance). Avoid questions whose answer depends on a disputed date or detail; if one must be used, make the question say "according to most textbooks".
- The wrong options must be plausible and each based on a typical confusion (for example a neighbouring period, a similar leader or a similar treaty). Only one option may be correct, and it must be correct under the commonly used textbook view.
- After "**Answer: X.**" (on the same line) give a clear explanation, then name the confusion behind the most tempting wrong option, and make sure that explanation is TRUE: the option must really come from the confusion you describe.
