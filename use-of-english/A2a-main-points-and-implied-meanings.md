# JAMB Use of English Notes: Main Points, Implied Meanings and Logical Reasoning

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section A of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Comprehension and Summary. It covers:

- **Objective i:** identify main points/topic sentences in passages.
- **Objective ii:** determine implied meanings.
- **Test (a):** comprehension of the whole or part of each passage.
- **Test (c):** coherence and logical reasoning (deductions, inferences, etc.).

These skills are used in all the Section A passages: the **comprehension passage (5 questions)**, the **summary passage (5 questions)**, the **cloze passage (10 questions)** and, in a different way, the **reading text (5 questions)**. They are the foundation of every comprehension question JAMB asks.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Answer questions on the whole passage and on a single part of it.
2. Find the topic sentence of a paragraph, wherever it is placed, and tell main points from supporting details.
3. State the main idea of a whole passage and choose a suitable title.
4. Tell the difference between what a passage **states** and what it **implies**, and work out implied meanings step by step.
5. Follow the logic of a passage: what words like *it*, *this* and *such* refer to, how ideas are linked, and what can be deduced or inferred.
6. Avoid the common JAMB traps: outside knowledge, over-reading, partial truths and extreme options.

The other comprehension skills are in A2b (meanings and grammatical functions of words, phrases, clauses, figures of speech and idioms) and A2c (the writer's intention, mood, attitude and opinion). The types of passage are in A1.

**About the passages in these notes:** every passage here is original, written for practice. The people, small communities and events in them are invented, even when a real state or city is named for a Nigerian setting. Do not treat them as real news or history.

## (a) Comprehension of the whole or part of a passage

### What the syllabus means

JAMB can ask about:

- **the whole passage** (global questions): its main idea, purpose, best title or general message;
- **part of the passage** (local questions): a fact in one sentence, the idea of one paragraph, the reason for an event, the meaning of one expression.

### Global vs local questions

| Feature | Global (whole-passage) questions | Local (part-of-passage) questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What they ask about | The passage as a unit | One sentence, line or paragraph |
| Typical wording | "The passage is mainly about…", "A suitable title…", "The writer's main point is…" | "According to paragraph 2…", "In the passage, the farmers were worried because…", "The word 'it' in line 5 refers to…" |
| Where the answer is | In the overall message; often in the first and last paragraphs | In one specific place |
| How to answer | Think about all the paragraphs together | Go back to the exact place and read around it |
| Common trap | An option that is true of only one paragraph | An option that is true of a different paragraph |

### Literal comprehension: "According to the passage…"

Many local questions test **literal** (stated) meaning. The answer is written in the passage, but usually in **different words** (a paraphrase). JAMB rarely copies the exact sentence into the correct option. A wrong option often **copies words** from the passage but changes the meaning.

**Example:**

> The new bridge at Ikom was completed in 2020, but traders still complain that it is too narrow for heavy lorries.

Question: According to the sentence, what is the traders' complaint?

- A. The bridge was completed too late. *(Wrong: the passage does not say this.)*
- B. Heavy lorries cannot use the bridge easily. *(Correct: a paraphrase of "too narrow for heavy lorries".)*
- C. The bridge is too heavy. *(Wrong: copies "heavy" but changes the meaning.)*
- D. The traders want a new bridge. *(Wrong: not stated.)*

**Note:** the place and date in this example are invented for practice. In the examination, take all facts from the passage only.

## Objective i: identifying main points and topic sentences

### Key ideas

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topic | What the paragraph is about, in a word or short phrase | "Rainwater harvesting" |
| Main idea (main point) | What the writer says about the topic: the central message | "Rainwater harvesting can solve water shortages in dry areas" |
| Topic sentence | The sentence that states the main idea of a paragraph | "Rainwater harvesting offers a cheap answer to water shortages in the North." |
| Supporting details | Facts, examples, reasons and explanations that develop the main idea | "A simple tank can store water for weeks." |
| Controlling idea | The part of the topic sentence that limits what the paragraph will discuss | "cheap answer to water shortages" |

**Topic vs main idea:** the topic is just the subject ("cassava"). The main idea is a full statement about it ("Cassava is an important source of income for farmers in Benue"). JAMB options for "main point" questions are usually full statements, so you need the main idea, not just the topic.

### Where is the topic sentence?

The topic sentence is often the **first sentence**, but not always.

**1. At the beginning (most common).** The writer states the point, then supports it.

> **Reading for pleasure improves students' performance in every subject.** A student who reads widely meets new words daily and learns how sentences are built. Such a student also learns to concentrate for long periods, a skill that helps in mathematics and science as much as in English.

**2. At the end.** The writer gives details first, then sums them up.

> The roads in the village are now tarred. A new health centre opened last year, and the primary school has a borehole. Young people who left for Lagos are coming back to start businesses. **Life in Umuobi has changed greatly in the past decade.**

**3. In the middle.** The writer begins with an introduction or a link from the previous paragraph, then states the point.

> Many people think that only rich countries can afford solar power. **In fact, solar energy is often the cheapest way to bring electricity to remote villages.** Extending the national grid to a small village far from existing power lines can be very costly, while a set of panels and batteries can be installed in days.

Here the first sentence states a belief the writer will **reject**. The topic sentence comes after "In fact".

**4. At the beginning and repeated at the end.** Common in argumentative writing; the last sentence restates the point.

**5. Implied (no topic sentence).** The writer never states the main idea directly; you must work it out from the details.

> Mama Nkechi wakes at four to grind pepper for her food canteen. By six she is serving breakfast to bus drivers. At noon she sends money to her son at university, and in the evening she checks her daughter's homework by lamplight before sleeping at midnight.

No single sentence states the point, but every detail supports this implied main idea: **Mama Nkechi works extremely hard for her family.**

### How to find the main idea of a paragraph: step by step

1. **Find the topic.** Ask: "Who or what is this paragraph about?" Look for the word or idea that keeps returning.
2. **Ask what the writer says about the topic.** What is the one general point all the sentences have in common?
3. **Look for a general sentence.** The topic sentence is broader than the others; the others are specific examples, facts or reasons.
4. **Test it.** Can every other sentence answer "How do you know?" or "Give an example" about it? If yes, it is the main idea.
5. **Watch signal words.** *In fact, however, but, the truth is, therefore, thus, in short, in conclusion* often come just before or within the main point.
6. **If there is no topic sentence,** sum up the details in your own words.

### Main point vs supporting detail

| Main point | Supporting detail |
| --- | --- |
| General, broad | Specific, narrow |
| Answers "What is the paragraph mainly saying?" | Answers "How? Why? For example?" |
| Usually one per paragraph | Usually several per paragraph |
| Example: "Social media has changed how Nigerian businesses advertise." | Example: "A tailor in Aba can now show new designs to customers in Kano through short videos." |

**JAMB trap:** wrong options for "main idea" questions are often **true supporting details**. They are true, and they are in the passage, but they are too narrow. Always ask: "Is this the **whole** point or only **part** of it?"

### The main idea of the whole passage

To find the main idea (theme or central message) of the whole passage:

1. Find the main idea of each paragraph.
2. Ask what they have in common.
3. Check the **first and last paragraphs** carefully. The introduction often states the subject; the conclusion often states the writer's final point.
4. Check that your answer covers **all** the paragraphs, not just one.

### Choosing a suitable title

A good title:

- covers the **whole** passage (not one paragraph);
- is **neither too broad nor too narrow**;
- matches the **writer's focus** (for an argument, the writer's position; for an exposition, the subject explained).

| Kind of option | Example (for a passage on how rainwater harvesting helps dry northern villages) | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Too broad | "Water" | Wrong: the passage is not about water in general |
| Too narrow | "How to clean a rain tank" | Wrong: only one detail |
| Off focus | "The causes of drought in the Sahel" | Wrong: the passage is about a solution, not causes |
| Just right | "Rainwater Harvesting: A Solution for Dry Villages" | Correct |

### Worked example: main points

Read this original passage.

> (1) Nigeria's population is very young. A large share of its people are under the age of 30, and every year millions of young people finish school and look for work.
>
> (2) This youthful population can be a great advantage. Young people are energetic, quick to learn new skills and open to new technology. Nigerian youths have already built successful music, film and technology businesses that are known across Africa.
>
> (3) However, the advantage will be wasted unless young people find meaningful work. Many graduates spend years searching for jobs that do not exist, and frustration can push some into crime. A growing population without jobs is a burden rather than a blessing.
>
> (4) The country's youth can become its greatest asset only if government and businesses invest in skills training, small business loans and industries that create jobs.

**Main idea of each paragraph:**

| Paragraph | Topic sentence / main idea | Type of support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "Nigeria's population is very young." | Fact: many people under 30; millions seek work yearly |
| 2 | "This youthful population can be a great advantage." | Reasons (energy, skills) and examples (music, film, technology) |
| 3 | "However, the advantage will be wasted unless young people find meaningful work." | Problems: unemployment, frustration, crime |
| 4 | The youth can be an asset only with investment in skills, loans and jobs | The writer's conclusion |

**Main idea of the whole passage:** Nigeria's young population can be a great asset, but only if enough jobs and opportunities are created.

**Which title is best?**

- A. "Crime among Nigerian Youths" *(too narrow; only part of paragraph 3)*
- B. "Nigeria's Music Industry" *(too narrow; one example in paragraph 2)*
- C. "Nigeria's Youth: Asset or Burden?" *(correct: covers advantage, danger and condition)*
- D. "The History of Nigeria" *(too broad and off focus)*

**Accuracy note:** the passage speaks generally ("a large share", "millions") rather than giving exact statistics. Population and unemployment figures vary between sources and years, so if a JAMB passage gives figures, use the passage's figures only.

## Objective ii: determining implied meanings

### Stated vs implied meaning

- A **stated** (explicit, literal) meaning is written directly in the passage.
- An **implied** (implicit) meaning is **suggested** but not written directly. The reader works it out from clues. This process is called **inferring**, and the conclusion you reach is an **inference**.

**Example:**

> When the principal walked into the classroom, the noise stopped at once, and thirty pairs of eyes dropped to their books.

- Stated: the noise stopped; the students looked at their books.
- Implied: the students were afraid of (or deeply respected) the principal, and they had probably not been studying before.

| Feature | Stated meaning | Implied meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where found | Directly in the words | "Between the lines" |
| How you get it | Read and paraphrase | Combine clues and reason |
| Question wording | "According to the passage…", "The writer states that…" | "It can be inferred that…", "The writer implies…", "The passage suggests…", "It is likely that…" |
| Risk | Choosing an option that copies words but changes meaning | Going too far, or using outside knowledge |

### How writers imply meaning

1. **Through actions:** what characters do shows what they feel. (*She tore the letter into pieces* → she was angry or upset.)
2. **Through word choice:** "The minister *strolled* in two hours late" suggests he did not care about being late; "rushed in" would suggest the opposite.
3. **Through details the writer chooses to include:** a description of empty shelves and long queues implies scarcity.
4. **Through contrast:** "Others went home; Bola stayed to finish the work" implies Bola is hard-working.
5. **Through what is left out:** if a report praises every part of a project except the budget, it may imply the budget was a problem.
6. **Through tone and figurative language:** irony or sarcasm means the opposite of the literal words (see A2c).

### How to work out an implied meaning: step by step

1. **Find the relevant part** of the passage that the question refers to.
2. **List the clues:** actions, words, details, contrasts.
3. **Ask what the clues together suggest.** What must be true, or is very likely to be true, for the writer to write this?
4. **Test each option against the clues.** Is there evidence in the passage for it?
5. **Reject options that go too far.** An inference must be **reasonable and supported**, not a wild guess.
6. **Reject options from outside knowledge.** Even if an option is true in life, it is wrong if the passage does not support it.

### Worked example: implied meanings

> Ifeanyi checked the notice board three times. His name was not on the list of successful candidates. He walked slowly to the bus stop, ignoring the calls of his friends, and when he got home he went straight to his room without greeting his mother.

**Question:** It can be inferred that Ifeanyi

- A. hated his friends *(too far: ignoring them once does not mean hatred)*
- B. was deeply disappointed by the result *(correct: checking three times, walking slowly, ignoring friends and avoiding his mother all point to disappointment)*
- C. would rewrite the examination next year *(not supported: the passage does not mention his plans)*
- D. did not care about the result *(contradicted: checking three times shows he cared a lot)*

**Why B?** Every clue points to disappointment. A goes beyond the evidence, C is a guess about the future, and D is the opposite of what the clues show.

### Degrees of inference

Not every possible conclusion is a good inference. Think of a scale:

| Level | Description | Example (from the Ifeanyi passage) | Correct in JAMB? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stated | Written directly | His name was not on the list | Correct for "according to the passage" questions |
| Strong inference | Clearly suggested by several clues | He was disappointed | Correct for "it can be inferred" questions |
| Weak guess | Possible, but little evidence | He would study harder next time | Usually wrong |
| Unsupported or contradicted | No evidence, or the passage says the opposite | He did not care | Wrong |

### Implied meanings in different passage types

| Type of passage | What is often implied |
| --- | --- |
| Narrative | Characters' feelings, motives and relationships; what will probably happen next |
| Descriptive | The mood of a place; the writer's feelings about it |
| Expository | Consequences that follow from the facts; what the writer considers important |
| Argumentative | The writer's attitude to opposing groups; the assumptions behind the argument |

## (c) Coherence and logical reasoning

### Coherence: how a passage holds together

A passage is **coherent** when its ideas follow each other logically, so the reader can follow the writer's thinking. JAMB tests coherence through questions about:

1. **Reference words** (what *it, this, they, such, the former, the latter, here, there* refer to).
2. **Linking words** (what *however, therefore, moreover, thus, instead* tell us about the relationship between ideas).
3. **Sequence** (the order of events or steps).
4. **Relationships between sentences or paragraphs** (cause–effect, contrast, example, conclusion).

### Reference words: step by step

A **reference word** points back (or sometimes forward) to something already mentioned. JAMB asks: "The word 'it' in line 4 refers to…"

**Steps:**

1. Go to the exact line.
2. Look **back** to the nearest suitable noun or idea before the reference word (sometimes the whole previous sentence).
3. Check **number:** *it/this* = singular or a whole idea; *they/these* = plural.
4. **Substitute** each option for the reference word and read the sentence again. Only the correct one makes sense.

**Example:**

> The farmers built a small dam across the stream. It stored enough water to last through the dry season, and this allowed them to grow vegetables all year round.

- "It" refers to **the small dam** (a dam stores water; the stream does not "store" water in this sense).
- "this" refers to **the fact that the dam stored enough water** (a whole idea, not just one noun).
- "them" refers to **the farmers**.

**Example with *former* and *latter*:**

> Both rice and maize grow well in Kebbi, but the former needs far more water than the latter.

- "the former" = **rice** (the first of the two mentioned).
- "the latter" = **maize** (the second).

**Accuracy note:** rice, especially lowland (paddy) rice, generally needs much more water than maize; this is widely stated in agriculture textbooks. In the exam, use only what the passage says.

### Linking words and the logic they signal

| Relationship | Linking words | What to expect next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Addition | and, also, moreover, furthermore, in addition, besides | Another point in the same direction |
| Contrast | but, however, yet, nevertheless, although, on the other hand, whereas | A different or opposite point |
| Cause | because, since, as, owing to, due to | A reason |
| Effect/result | so, therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence | A result or conclusion |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, like, namely | A specific case of the general point |
| Sequence | first, then, next, after that, finally, subsequently | The next step or event |
| Conclusion/summary | in conclusion, in short, to sum up, on the whole, in brief | The writer's overall point |
| Concession | admittedly, granted, of course, it is true that | The writer accepts part of the opposing view |
| Restatement | in other words, that is, namely | The same idea in different words |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, as long as | A condition for something to happen |

**Why this matters:** the most important idea often comes **after a contrast word**. In "The project was expensive, **but** it saved many lives", the writer's main point is that it saved lives.

### Logical reasoning: deductions and inferences

The syllabus mentions "deductions, inferences, etc." Both are ways of drawing conclusions.

| Feature | Deduction | Inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic meaning | A conclusion that **must** follow from the information given | A conclusion that is **reasonable/likely** from the clues given |
| Certainty | Certain, if the information is true | Probable, well supported |
| Example | "All the candidates who scored above 250 were invited. Ada scored 270." → Ada was invited. | "Ada smiled widely as she read the letter." → The letter probably contained good news. |
| Typical question wording | "It follows from the passage that…", "Which conclusion can be drawn?" | "It can be inferred that…", "The passage suggests/implies…" |

**Note on textbook variation:** many school textbooks and exam guides use "deduce" and "infer" as if they meant the same thing ("work out from the passage"). In logic, a deduction is necessarily true while an inference may only be probable. In JAMB, the practical skill is the same: **draw a conclusion that is supported by the passage, and no more**.

### Other kinds of logical reasoning questions

**1. Cause and effect.** "The farmers lost their crops because…" Find the cause in the passage; check that the option gives the cause the **passage** gives, not another possible cause.

**2. Assumptions.** An assumption is something the writer **takes for granted** without stating it. For example, a writer who says "Since more people now own smartphones, banks should offer more mobile services" assumes that smartphone owners are willing to use mobile banking.

**3. Conclusions.** "Which of the following conclusions is supported by the passage?" The correct conclusion follows from the facts given; wrong ones go too far or contradict the passage.

**4. Prediction.** "What is most likely to happen next?" Choose the option most strongly supported by the trend in the passage.

**5. Consistency.** "Which statement is NOT consistent with the passage?" Find the one option that contradicts the passage.

**6. Sequence.** "Which happened first?" Use time words, and remember that flashbacks can tell events out of order.

### Worked example: logical reasoning

Read this original passage.

> (1) In 2019, a cooperative of women in a village near Abeokuta began making soap from palm kernel oil. At first, they sold only to neighbours. (2) Then one member, a former teacher, opened a social media page for the group and posted photographs and short videos of the soap-making process. (3) Within six months, orders were arriving from Ibadan and Lagos, and the women had to employ four young men to help with packaging and delivery.
>
> (4) The group's success was not only about the internet. The women had spent a year improving their recipe and had agreed to sell at a fixed price, so customers trusted them. (5) Without a good product, the online page would have attracted attention but not loyal buyers.

**Question 1 (cause):** According to the passage, orders began to come from Ibadan and Lagos mainly after

- A. the women employed four young men
- B. a member opened a social media page for the group
- C. the women agreed to sell at a fixed price
- D. the women began to sell to neighbours

**Answer: B.** Sentence (2) describes the social media page, and sentence (3) says "Within six months, orders were arriving". A is a result, not a cause. C contributed to trust (sentence 4), but the question asks what the orders came after in the sequence of events described.

**Question 2 (reference):** "them" in sentence (4) refers to

- A. the four young men
- B. the customers
- C. the women
- D. the neighbours

**Answer: C.** "...had agreed to sell at a fixed price, so customers trusted **them**": the customers trusted the women who set the fixed price.

**Question 3 (deduction):** It follows from the passage that the business

- A. created jobs for people outside the cooperative
- B. made more profit than any business in Lagos
- C. stopped selling to neighbours
- D. was started by a former teacher

**Answer: A.** The women employed four young men (sentence 3), so jobs were created outside the cooperative. B is unsupported, C is not stated, and D is wrong (the teacher opened the page; the cooperative started the business).

**Question 4 (writer's reasoning):** The writer's main point in the second paragraph is that

- A. the internet alone guarantees success
- B. social media is harmful to small businesses
- C. a good product and fair pricing were essential to the success
- D. customers prefer to buy from neighbours

**Answer: C.** "The group's success was not only about the internet" (4) and "Without a good product, the online page would have attracted attention but not loyal buyers" (5).

**Question 5 (assumption):** The writer assumes that

- A. customers return to buy products they trust
- B. all online businesses fail
- C. palm kernel oil is expensive
- D. former teachers are good at business

**Answer: A.** The argument in paragraph 2 depends on the idea that trust and quality bring "loyal buyers". None of the other options is needed for the argument.

## Common JAMB traps in comprehension questions

| Trap | Example | How to beat it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Word-copying option** | An option repeats the passage's words but changes the meaning (e.g. "The bridge is too heavy") | Check the meaning, not just the words |
| **True but not in the passage** | "Malaria is caused by a parasite" when the passage does not say so | Ask: "Where does the passage say or suggest this?" |
| **Too narrow** | A supporting detail offered as the main idea | Ask: "Does it cover the whole paragraph or passage?" |
| **Too broad** | "Education" as the main idea of a passage about girls' schooling in the North | Match the writer's exact focus |
| **Extreme words** | always, never, all, none, only, completely | Accept them only if the passage is equally extreme |
| **Reversed cause and effect** | The passage says A caused B; the option says B caused A | Track the direction of cause and effect |
| **Wrong reference** | "it" taken to refer to the nearest noun when it refers to an earlier one | Substitute each option and read again |
| **Over-reading an inference** | Inferring hatred from one rude act | Choose the inference with the most evidence |
| **NOT/EXCEPT questions** | Choosing a true statement when the question asks for the false one | Underline NOT/EXCEPT in your mind and check each option |
| **Opinion as fact** | "The writer states that…" followed by the writer's opinion presented as a fact | Distinguish facts from opinions (A1) |

## Putting it all together: a full worked passage

Read this original passage and the reasoning for each question.

> Every December, thousands of Nigerians living abroad fly home for Christmas and the New Year. Families gather, weddings are held, and markets are crowded with buyers. For many towns in the South-East, this is the busiest and most profitable period of the year.
>
> Yet the season has another side. Prices of food, transport and rent rise sharply, and local residents who earn in naira struggle to keep up with visitors who spend foreign currency. Some landlords even ask tenants to leave so that they can rent rooms to visitors at higher prices.
>
> None of this means the visits are bad. They bring money, renew family ties and encourage investment in hometowns. But local governments could do more to plan for the season, for example by improving roads and monitoring sharp price increases, so that residents do not pay the price for everyone's celebration.

**1. The passage is mainly about**

- A. why Nigerians travel abroad
- B. the benefits and problems of the festive season visits
- C. the history of Christmas in the South-East
- D. landlords and tenants

**Reasoning:** paragraph 1 gives benefits, paragraph 2 gives problems, paragraph 3 weighs both and suggests action. **B** covers all three. A is outside the passage; C is not discussed; D is a detail in paragraph 2.

**2. "Yet the season has another side" introduces**

- A. more benefits of the season
- B. the problems caused by the season
- C. a story about a landlord
- D. the writer's conclusion

**Reasoning:** "Yet" signals **contrast**: after the benefits in paragraph 1, the writer turns to problems. **B.**

**3. It can be inferred that local residents struggle with prices partly because**

- A. they spend foreign currency
- B. they earn in naira while some visitors spend foreign currency
- C. the visitors refuse to pay rent
- D. the markets close in December

**Reasoning:** the passage contrasts residents "who earn in naira" with visitors "who spend foreign currency". The implication is that visitors can pay more, pushing prices up. **B.** A reverses the facts; C and D are unsupported.

**4. The writer's attitude to the visits is**

- A. completely negative
- B. balanced
- C. indifferent
- D. sarcastic

**Reasoning:** "None of this means the visits are bad" and the listing of benefits and problems show a **balanced** view. **B.** (Attitude questions are covered fully in A2c.)

**5. Which of the following would the writer most likely support?**

- A. Banning Nigerians abroad from visiting at Christmas
- B. Local government action to check sharp price increases during the festive season
- C. Doubling rents in December
- D. Closing the markets to visitors

**Reasoning:** the writer suggests "monitoring sharp price increases". **B.** A and D contradict "None of this means the visits are bad". C is one of the problems the writer complains about.

| Implied meaning | Meaning suggested but not stated directly |
| Inference | A reasonable conclusion drawn from clues in the passage |
| Deduction | A conclusion that must follow from the information given |
| Topic | What a paragraph or passage is about, in a word or phrase |
| Main idea | The central point the writer makes about the topic |
| Topic sentence | The sentence that states the main idea of a paragraph |
| Supporting details | Facts, examples and reasons that develop the main idea |
| Paraphrase | The same meaning expressed in different words |
| Coherence | The logical connection of ideas in a passage |
| Reference word | A word such as *it, this, they, the former* that points to something mentioned elsewhere |
| Linking word (connective) | A word that shows how ideas are related, e.g. *however, therefore* |
| Assumption | Something a writer takes for granted without stating it |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Answer from the passage, not from life.** The most common mistake is choosing an option that is true in the real world but not in the passage.
- **Main idea questions:** reject options that are only one detail (too narrow) or that go beyond the passage (too broad).
- **The topic sentence is not always first.** Check for it at the end, in the middle after "In fact" or "However", or work it out if it is implied.
- **"According to the passage" = stated meaning; "It can be inferred" = implied meaning.** Read the question stem carefully to know which one is being tested.
- **Inferences must be supported.** Choose the option with the most evidence, not the most exciting one.
- **Reference questions:** go back to the line, look backwards, and substitute each option.
- **Contrast words matter:** the main point often comes after *but, however, yet*.
- **Beware word-copying options.** A wrong option often reuses the passage's exact words.
- **NOT/EXCEPT:** you are looking for the one false option.
- **Do not change a correct answer without a clear reason** found in the passage.

## Quick summary

1. JAMB tests comprehension of the whole passage (main idea, title) and of parts of it (facts, reasons, references).
2. The topic sentence states a paragraph's main idea; it is usually first but can be in the middle, at the end or only implied.
3. Main ideas are general; supporting details are specific facts, examples and reasons. Wrong main-idea options are often true details.
4. Stated meaning is written in the passage; implied meaning is worked out from clues such as actions, word choice, details and contrasts.
5. A good inference is reasonable and supported by evidence; it does not go too far or rely on outside knowledge.
6. Coherence questions test reference words, linking words, sequence and relationships between ideas.
7. Deductions must follow from the information; inferences are well-supported likely conclusions. Many textbooks use the two words loosely.

## Practice questions

Read the passage below and answer questions 1 to 6.

> Until recently, the people of Tunga, a farming community in Nasarawa State, depended on a single borehole for drinking water. During the dry season, the queue at the borehole began before dawn, and girls often arrived late at school after waiting for hours.
>
> Two years ago, the community leaders decided to act. Instead of waiting for government, they asked every household to contribute a small sum each month. With the money, they repaired an old well, built a rainwater tank beside the primary school and trained two young men to maintain the borehole pump.
>
> The results surprised even the leaders. The queues became shorter, school attendance improved, and fewer children suffered from stomach illnesses. Visitors from neighbouring villages now come to learn how Tunga did it. The leaders insist, however, that the most important resource was not money but the willingness of the people to work together.

**1. The passage is mainly about**

- A. the dangers of drinking well water
- B. how a community solved its water problem through cooperation
- C. the failure of government in Nasarawa State
- D. girls' education in northern Nigeria

**Answer: B.** All three paragraphs follow how Tunga solved its water shortage, and the conclusion stresses working together. C goes beyond the passage; A and D are not the main focus.

**2. Which sentence is the topic sentence of the second paragraph?**

- A. "Two years ago, the community leaders decided to act."
- B. "Instead of waiting for government, they asked every household to contribute a small sum each month."
- C. "With the money, they repaired an old well."
- D. "They trained two young men to maintain the borehole pump."

**Answer: A.** The rest of the paragraph explains how they acted. B, C and D are supporting details.

**3. It can be inferred from the first paragraph that before the changes**

- A. girls were usually responsible for fetching water
- B. the borehole was always broken
- C. the school had no teachers
- D. boys did not attend school

**Answer: A.** Girls "arrived late at school after waiting for hours" at the borehole, which suggests that fetching water was their task. The other options are not supported.

**4. "they" in the second paragraph ("they asked every household") refers to**

- A. the girls
- B. the government
- C. the community leaders
- D. the visitors

**Answer: C.** The sentence before says "the community leaders decided to act". Substituting "the community leaders" for "they" makes sense.

**5. According to the passage, which of the following was a result of the project?**

- A. The government built a new borehole.
- B. Fewer children suffered from stomach illnesses.
- C. The rainy season became longer.
- D. Every household stopped paying the contribution.

**Answer: B.** This is stated in the third paragraph. A contradicts "Instead of waiting for government"; C and D are not mentioned.

**6. The leaders' view, stated in the last sentence, is that the project's success depended mostly on**

- A. the amount of money collected
- B. help from neighbouring villages
- C. the people's willingness to cooperate
- D. the skill of the two young men

**Answer: C.** "...the most important resource was not money but the willingness of the people to work together." The contrast "not money but" signals the main point.

**7. "Mr Okafor glanced at his watch for the fifth time and drummed his fingers on the desk." What does the sentence imply about Mr Okafor?**

- A. He was angry with his watch.
- B. He was impatient.
- C. He was a musician.
- D. He was sleepy.

**Answer: B.** Checking the time repeatedly and drumming one's fingers are signs of impatience. A and C take the actions too literally; D is contradicted by his restlessness.

**8. "Both cassava and yam are grown in Benue; the latter is especially famous there." The latter refers to**

- A. Benue
- B. cassava
- C. yam
- D. both crops

**Answer: C.** "The latter" means the second of two things mentioned: yam.

**9. "The road was dangerous; consequently, many drivers avoided it." The word "consequently" shows that the second idea is**

- A. a contrast with the first
- B. an example of the first
- C. a result of the first
- D. a condition for the first

**Answer: C.** "Consequently" signals a result: drivers avoided the road because it was dangerous.

**10. "All the students in the debate team are in SS3. Musa is in the debate team." Which conclusion must be true?**

- A. Musa is in SS3.
- B. All SS3 students are in the debate team.
- C. Musa is the team captain.
- D. Musa is a good speaker.

**Answer: A.** This is a deduction: if all team members are in SS3 and Musa is a member, Musa must be in SS3. B reverses the statement; C and D are not supported.
