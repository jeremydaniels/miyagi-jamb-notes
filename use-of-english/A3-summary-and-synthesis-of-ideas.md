# JAMB Use of English Notes: Summary and Synthesis of Ideas

## What JAMB expects

This topic is part of Section A of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Comprehension and Summary. It covers:

- Content item (ii)(e): **synthesis of ideas from the passages**.
- The syllabus NOTE: "Synthesis of ideas means the art of combining distinct or separate pieces of information to form a complete whole as summary."
- Objective i: **identify main points/topic sentences in passages**, used here for summary.
- Objective iv, in part: **deduce the writer's intention and opinion**, because a good summary keeps the writer's main purpose.

In the examination (Section D of the syllabus), there is **1 summary passage with 5 questions**. The passage is about 200 words long and may come from any discipline (agriculture, health, economics, history, science, law and so on). The questions are **multiple choice**: you do not write a summary yourself, but you must choose the option that best summarises a sentence, a paragraph or the whole passage, or that combines ideas correctly.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Explain what a summary is and what "synthesis of ideas" means.
2. Find the topic sentence and main point of each paragraph.
3. Tell main points apart from supporting details, examples and illustrations.
4. Combine (synthesise) separate points into one complete idea.
5. Choose the best summary, the best title and the main idea of a passage in JAMB multiple-choice questions.
6. Recognise and avoid the common wrong options JAMB uses as traps.

Comprehension skills in general are covered in `A2a-main-points-and-implied-meanings.md`, `A2b-words-phrases-and-expressions-in-passages.md` and `A2c-writers-intention-mood-and-attitude.md`. This file focuses on summary.

## What is a summary?

A **summary** is a short version of a longer text that keeps only its **main points**, in clear, simple words, without the details, examples, repetitions and decorations.

Think of a summary as the answer to the question: **"What is this passage really saying, in as few words as possible?"**

A good summary is:

- **Accurate:** it says exactly what the writer said, not more and not less.
- **Complete:** it covers all the main points the question asks for.
- **Brief:** it leaves out examples, illustrations, figures (unless they are the point), repetitions and the writer's decorations.
- **Objective:** it does not add your own opinion.
- **Clear:** it is easy to understand on its own.

### An everyday example

Your friend asks how the inter-house sports went. You do not describe every race. You say: "Blue House won overall, mainly because of their relay teams, and the event ended early because of rain." That is a summary. You picked the **main points** (who won, why, what happened at the end) and left out the details (who came third in the 100 metres, what colour the tents were).

## What is synthesis of ideas?

The syllabus defines synthesis of ideas as **"the art of combining distinct or separate pieces of information to form a complete whole as summary."**

In simple words: **synthesis means putting pieces together.** The ideas you need are often spread across different sentences, or even different paragraphs. You must:

1. **Find** each separate piece of information.
2. **See how they are connected** (cause and effect, problem and solution, contrast, sequence, general idea and examples).
3. **Join them** into one complete statement.

### Summary vs synthesis

| Feature | Summary | Synthesis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic meaning | Shortening a text to its main points | Combining separate pieces of information into one whole |
| What you work with | Usually one sentence, paragraph or passage | Ideas spread across several sentences or paragraphs (sometimes across passages) |
| Main skill | Selecting the important and leaving out the unimportant | Seeing connections and joining ideas |
| Result | A shorter version | A new, complete statement that no single sentence in the passage says on its own |
| How they relate | The end product | One of the main tools for producing a good summary |

The two are closely linked. To summarise a whole passage, you almost always need to synthesise: you take the main point of paragraph 1, the main point of paragraph 2 and the main point of paragraph 3, and you join them into one complete idea.

### A small example of synthesis

Read these three separate sentences from different parts of a passage:

- "Many farmers in Benue lose up to a third of their oranges after harvest."
- "Bad roads mean that trucks take days to reach the markets in Lagos and Port Harcourt."
- "There are few cold stores or processing factories near the farms."

None of the sentences says the whole idea. **Synthesised**, they become:

> **Poor roads and a lack of storage and processing facilities cause farmers to lose much of their fruit after harvest.**

Notice what happened:

- "Lose up to a third of their oranges" became "lose much of their fruit". The exact figure and the specific fruit were details.
- "Benue", "Lagos" and "Port Harcourt" disappeared. They were examples of places.
- The two causes (roads; storage and processing) were joined to the effect (losses).

## Main points vs supporting details

The key skill for summary is telling **main points** apart from **supporting details**.

| | Main point | Supporting detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | The central idea the writer wants you to accept or understand | Material that explains, proves or decorates the main point |
| Where it is usually found | The topic sentence (often, but not always, the first sentence of a paragraph) | The sentences that follow the topic sentence |
| Typical forms | A general statement, a claim, a conclusion | Examples, figures, dates, names, quotations, stories, comparisons, repetitions |
| Signal words | "The main reason…", "Above all…", "In short…", "Therefore…", "The problem is…" | "For example…", "for instance…", "such as…", "like…", "In 2019…", "according to…" |
| In a summary | Kept | Usually left out |
| If you remove it | The paragraph loses its meaning | The paragraph still makes sense, only less vivid |

**The "remove test":** cover a sentence with your finger. If the paragraph still makes its point without it, that sentence is a supporting detail. If the paragraph no longer makes sense, it is the main point.

### Finding the topic sentence

The **topic sentence** states the main idea of a paragraph. Writers place it in different positions:

| Position | How the paragraph works | Example signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beginning (most common) | Main idea first, then explanation and examples | "Rice farming in Kebbi State has grown rapidly." followed by reasons |
| End | Examples or reasons first, then a conclusion | "…For all these reasons, the policy failed." |
| Middle | An introduction or a question first, then the main idea, then examples | "Why do so many students fail Mathematics? The main cause is poor foundation." |
| Implied (not stated) | Every sentence points to an idea the writer never states directly | You must work it out yourself (see `A2a-main-points-and-implied-meanings.md`) |

### What to leave out of a summary

Leave out:

- **Examples and illustrations:** "such as Kano, Kaduna and Katsina"
- **Figures and statistics**, unless the figure itself is the point: "about 70 per cent", "in 2018"
- **Names of people quoted:** "according to Dr Adebayo"
- **Repetitions:** the same idea said again in different words
- **Rhetorical questions and decorations:** "Who would not want a cleaner city?"
- **Stories and anecdotes** used to introduce the topic
- **Your own opinion or knowledge**, even if true

## Step-by-step method for JAMB summary questions

Use these steps every time.

### Step 1: Read the questions first (quickly)

Look at the five questions before reading the passage. They tell you what to focus on: one paragraph? the whole passage? a title? Underline the key words: "paragraph 2", "the writer's main point", "best summarises".

### Step 2: Read the whole passage once for the general idea

Do not stop at hard words. Ask yourself: **What is the topic? What is the writer saying about it? Why did the writer write it (to inform, persuade, describe, narrate)?**

### Step 3: Find the topic sentence of each paragraph

Read each paragraph again. Underline or note the sentence that carries its main idea. If no sentence does, write the main idea in your own head in a few words.

### Step 4: Write a "skeleton" of the passage in your head

Put the main points together in order. For example: "Problem → causes → solution." This is your synthesis.

### Step 5: Read the question carefully

Is it asking about:

- **one sentence?** (Choose the option that keeps that sentence's meaning.)
- **one paragraph?** (Choose the option that matches that paragraph's topic sentence, not another paragraph's.)
- **the whole passage?** (Choose the option that covers all the main points, not just one paragraph.)
- **a title?** (Choose the option that names the central topic, neither too wide nor too narrow.)

### Step 6: Eliminate wrong options using the trap checklist

Cross out options that are:

1. **Too narrow:** only a detail or one part of the idea.
2. **Too wide (too general):** bigger than what the passage discusses.
3. **Distorted:** uses words from the passage but changes the meaning (for example, turns "may" into "will", "some" into "all", or reverses cause and effect).
4. **Not in the passage:** true in real life, but the writer did not say it.
5. **From the wrong part:** correct for a different paragraph.
6. **Contradicting the writer:** says the opposite of the writer's view.

### Step 7: Choose the option that is accurate AND complete

If two options seem correct, choose the one that covers **more of the main idea** without adding anything new. Then check it against the passage one last time.

## The common JAMB traps in detail

### Trap 1: The true-but-minor detail

The option is correct according to the passage, but it is only an example or a small point. It is **not** the main idea.

> Passage idea: "Regular exercise improves health in many ways. It strengthens the heart, helps control weight and reduces stress."
> Trap option: "Exercise helps control weight." (True, but only one detail.)
> Correct: "Regular exercise has many health benefits."

### Trap 2: Too wide

The option goes beyond the passage.

> Passage about the dangers of illegal mining in Zamfara.
> Trap: "Mining is dangerous everywhere in the world." (The passage did not say this.)

### Trap 3: Distortion by absolute words

Watch for **always, never, all, none, only, completely, every**. Writers usually use careful words like **often, many, some, may, can, tends to**. An option that turns "may" into "will" or "many" into "all" is distorted.

> Passage: "Some traders have begun to accept bank transfers."
> Trap: "All traders now accept bank transfers."

### Trap 4: Reversed cause and effect

> Passage: "Because the drainage was blocked, the street flooded."
> Trap: "Because the street flooded, the drainage was blocked."

### Trap 5: Outside knowledge

The option is a true fact you know from school or the news, but the passage does not mention it. JAMB tests **what the passage says**, not what you know.

### Trap 6: Copied words with a changed meaning

The option repeats exact phrases from the passage, which makes it look right, but it joins them in a way the writer did not. Always check the **meaning**, not the words.

### Trap 7: The writer's opinion vs someone else's

Many passages report what "some people" or "critics" say and then disagree. An option that gives the critics' view as the writer's main point is wrong.

## Choosing a title

A good title:

- names the **central topic** of the whole passage;
- is **neither too wide nor too narrow**;
- matches the writer's **angle** (a passage about the dangers of something should not be titled "The Benefits of…").

| Passage about | Too wide | Too narrow | Good |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How waste from Lagos markets blocks drains and causes flooding | "Lagos" | "Plastic bags at Oyingbo Market" | "Market Waste and Flooding in Lagos" |
| Why many Nigerian youths are learning coding | "Technology" | "A coding school in Yaba" | "The Rise of Coding Among Nigerian Youths" |
| The causes and prevention of malaria | "Diseases in Africa" | "Mosquito nets" | "Malaria: Causes and Prevention" |

## Worked example 1: a passage on agriculture

Read this original passage.

> **Passage A**
>
> (1) Cassava is one of the most important crops in Nigeria. It grows well even in poor soils, survives long dry periods and can be left in the ground for months until the farmer needs it. For these reasons, it is often called a "famine reserve" crop, one that families can depend on when other foods fail.
>
> (2) Yet many cassava farmers remain poor. Fresh roots begin to spoil within two or three days of harvest, so farmers who cannot sell quickly are forced to accept very low prices. Most farmers also lack the machines needed to turn the roots into longer-lasting products.
>
> (3) Processing offers a way out. When cassava is turned into garri, fufu flour, starch or high-quality flour for bread, it lasts much longer and sells for more. Where farmers' cooperatives have set up small processing centres, members can earn far more because they sell finished products instead of raw roots. If such centres were spread across the country, cassava could make farmers rich rather than merely keep them alive.

**Skeleton (synthesis):**

- Paragraph 1: Cassava is important because it is hardy and reliable.
- Paragraph 2: But farmers stay poor because fresh roots spoil quickly and farmers cannot process them.
- Paragraph 3: Processing, especially by cooperatives, can raise farmers' incomes.

**Whole passage in one sentence:** Although cassava is a hardy and important crop, farmers remain poor because the roots spoil quickly, and processing could greatly increase their income.

**Question 1. Which of the following best summarises paragraph 1?**

- A. Cassava can be left in the ground for months.
- B. Cassava is valuable because it is hardy and dependable.
- C. Cassava is the most important crop in Africa.
- D. Cassava prevents famine in every part of Nigeria.

**Working:** A is a true detail but too narrow (Trap 1). C is too wide and not in the passage ("Africa"; also "most important" is stronger than "one of the most important") (Traps 2 and 3). D is distorted: the passage says families can depend on it, not that it prevents famine everywhere (Trap 3). **Answer: B.**

**Question 2. The main problem of cassava farmers, according to the passage, is that**

- A. cassava does not grow well in poor soils
- B. cassava cannot survive dry seasons
- C. fresh cassava spoils quickly and they cannot process it
- D. the government does not buy their cassava

**Working:** A and B contradict paragraph 1 (Trap 6/contradiction). D is not mentioned (Trap 5). **Answer: C.** Notice that C synthesises two separate sentences of paragraph 2.

**Question 3. Which of the following is the writer's main solution?**

- A. Farmers should stop planting cassava.
- B. Farmers should sell their cassava within two days.
- C. Cassava processing should be expanded, for example through cooperatives.
- D. Farmers should sell their cassava only to cooperatives in other states.

**Answer: C.** B is a detail of the problem, not the solution. D is a distortion: the passage mentions cooperatives as owners of processing centres, not as buyers in other states.

**Question 4. A suitable title for the passage is**

- A. Crops in Nigeria
- B. Garri and Fufu
- C. Cassava: From Survival Crop to Source of Wealth
- D. The Dry Season in Nigeria

**Answer: C.** A is too wide; B is too narrow; D is off-topic.

## Worked example 2: a passage on health

> **Passage B**
>
> (1) Handwashing with soap is one of the cheapest ways to prevent disease. Germs that cause diarrhoea, cholera and many chest infections are often passed from dirty hands to food, water and the mouth.
>
> (2) Despite this, many people wash their hands only with water, or not at all, before eating. Some believe that hands that look clean are clean. Others say soap is too costly, even though a single bar can serve a household for weeks.
>
> (3) Schools can change these habits. When pupils are taught why handwashing matters and are given simple facilities, such as a bucket with a tap, they not only practise it themselves but also carry the habit home to their families. Health workers therefore see schools as one of the best places to begin.

**Skeleton:** Handwashing with soap is a cheap way to prevent disease (1) → but many people neglect it because of wrong beliefs and cost (2) → schools are a good place to change the habit (3).

**Question: Which of the following best combines the ideas in paragraphs 2 and 3?**

- A. Soap is too costly for most families.
- B. Although many people neglect handwashing with soap, schools can help to build the habit.
- C. Pupils should be given buckets with taps.
- D. Hands that look clean are always clean.

**Working:** A presents an excuse ("others say") as if it were the writer's view, and the writer actually disagrees (Trap 7). C is a detail. D is a belief the writer rejects. **Answer: B**, which synthesises the problem (paragraph 2) and the solution (paragraph 3).

## Worked example 3: a passage on technology

> **Passage C**
>
> (1) Twenty years ago, sending money from Lagos to a relative in a village in Kogi State could take days and involve a risky journey. Today it can be done in seconds on a mobile phone.
>
> (2) Mobile banking and payment apps have brought many people into the formal financial system for the first time. Small traders now receive payments from customers they have never met, and workers can send part of their wages home instantly.
>
> (3) However, the new system brings new dangers. Fraudsters send fake messages to trick people into revealing their PINs, and failed transfers can leave customers stranded for hours. Unless banks improve their services and users learn to protect themselves, trust in mobile money may decline.

**Question: The writer's attitude to mobile banking can best be described as**

- A. completely negative
- B. completely positive
- C. balanced: it sees both benefits and risks
- D. indifferent

**Answer: C.** Paragraph 2 gives benefits; paragraph 3 gives dangers. A full summary must include both. (See `A2c-writers-intention-mood-and-attitude.md` for attitude questions.)

**Question: Which of the following is NOT a point made in the passage?**

- A. Money transfer is now faster than before.
- B. Mobile banking has brought more people into formal finance.
- C. Fraud is a risk of mobile banking.
- D. Mobile banking has ended poverty in rural areas.

**Answer: D.** This is not stated anywhere (Trap 5, and also too wide).

## Summarising a single sentence

JAMB sometimes asks which option best expresses the meaning of **one sentence** from the passage. Method:

1. Find the sentence's **subject** (who or what it is about).
2. Find its **main verb and main claim** (what it says about the subject).
3. Remove the extras (examples, descriptions, phrases in commas or brackets).
4. Choose the option that keeps the subject and the claim, without changing the strength of the words.

> Sentence: "The new road, which took nearly six years and several contractors to complete, has cut the journey from Abuja to Kaduna by almost an hour."
>
> Core: The new road has shortened the journey.
>
> Best option: "The completed road has reduced travel time between the two cities."
>
> Trap: "The road took six years to build." (true detail, not the main claim)

## Summary of different passage types

The four passage types in the syllabus (description, narration, exposition, argumentation/persuasion; see `A1-types-of-passages.md`) are summarised differently.

| Passage type | What to keep in a summary | What to leave out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Description | The overall impression of the person, place or thing | Long lists of colours, sounds and small physical details |
| Narration | The key events in order and the outcome | Minor events, dialogue, scene-setting |
| Exposition | The main facts, causes, effects, steps or explanations | Examples, statistics, illustrations |
| Argumentation/persuasion | The writer's position (thesis) and the main reasons | Examples, anecdotes, rhetorical questions, emotional language |

## How summary questions are usually worded

Learn to recognise these question forms:

- "Which of the following best summarises paragraph 2?"
- "The main point of the passage is that…"
- "Which of the following sentences best expresses the central idea of the passage?"
- "In one sentence, the writer's argument in the last paragraph is that…"
- "Which of the following best combines the ideas in paragraphs 1 and 3?"
- "A suitable title for the passage is…"
- "Which of the following is NOT one of the reasons given by the writer?"
- "According to the passage, the two main causes of … are…"

Exam guides sometimes call questions like the last one "synthesis" questions and others "summary" questions; in JAMB papers the five summary-passage questions mix both kinds. Prepare for all of them.

## Practising summary on your own

You become good at summary by practice. Each day:

1. Pick a newspaper article, a textbook paragraph or an editorial.
2. Write down the topic sentence of each paragraph.
3. Join the main points into **one sentence** of no more than 25 words.
4. Give the passage a title of no more than six words.
5. Compare your sentence with the original: did you add anything? Did you leave out a main point?

This is also good practice for summary writing in WAEC/NECO English, where you must write your own summary sentences.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Summary | A short version of a text that keeps only its main points |
| Synthesis of ideas | Combining separate pieces of information to form one complete whole (syllabus definition) |
| Main point | The central idea that the writer wants to communicate |
| Topic sentence | The sentence that states the main idea of a paragraph |
| Supporting detail | An example, figure, fact or explanation that develops the main point |
| Implied main idea | A main idea that the writer does not state directly but that all the sentences point to |
| Distractor | A wrong option designed to look correct |
| Too narrow | An option that covers only part or a detail of the idea |
| Too wide | An option that goes beyond what the passage discusses |
| Distortion | An option that uses the writer's words but changes their meaning |
| Thesis | The main position or claim in an argumentative passage |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Answer from the passage, not from your knowledge.** A true fact that the writer does not mention is wrong.
- **Check the scope of the question.** "Paragraph 2" means only paragraph 2. "The passage" means the whole passage.
- **Beware the true-but-minor detail.** It is the most common trap in summary questions.
- **Watch absolute words.** "All", "always", "never", "only" in an option should make you check carefully.
- **Do not confuse the writer's view with views the writer reports.** Look for signals like "some argue", "critics claim", "however".
- **A good title is the topic plus the angle.** Not just the subject word.
- **When two options look right, choose the more complete one** that adds nothing new.
- **Do not spend too long.** Read the questions first, then the passage, and come back to the passage for each question.

## Quick summary

1. A summary keeps only the main points of a text, in brief and accurate form, without examples, repetitions or your own opinion.
2. Synthesis of ideas means combining separate pieces of information into one complete whole; it is the main tool for summarising a passage.
3. The topic sentence carries the main idea of a paragraph; it is often first but can be last, in the middle or implied.
4. Supporting details (examples, figures, names, stories) are left out of a summary; use the "remove test" to spot them.
5. Answer JAMB summary questions by reading the questions first, building a skeleton of main points, and eliminating trap options.
6. The main traps are: true-but-minor detail, too wide, distorted (absolute words or reversed cause and effect), outside knowledge, wrong paragraph and the reported view.
7. A good title names the central topic and the writer's angle, neither too wide nor too narrow.

## Practice questions

Questions 1 to 5 are based on the passage below.

> (1) For many years, the Lagos lagoon supported thousands of fishermen who supplied fresh fish to markets across the city. Their canoes were a familiar sight at dawn, and fishing was a skill passed from father to son.
>
> (2) In recent decades, however, catches have fallen sharply. Waste from homes, markets and factories flows into the lagoon through open drains, and sand dredging for building projects destroys the shallow areas where young fish grow. As a result, many fishermen now return home with nearly empty nets.
>
> (3) Some experts believe the lagoon can still recover. They argue that if waste were treated before it reached the water, and if dredging were controlled, fish stocks could grow again within a few years. Such a recovery would protect not only the fishermen but also the many traders and families who depend on them.

**1. Which of the following best summarises paragraph 1?**

- A. Fishermen use canoes at dawn.
- B. The Lagos lagoon was once an important source of fish and livelihood.
- C. Fishing is the oldest occupation in Lagos.
- D. Fathers no longer teach their sons to fish.

**Answer: B.** A is a detail; C is not stated; D is not stated in paragraph 1.

**2. According to the passage, the two main causes of falling catches are**

- A. overfishing and bad weather
- B. pollution from waste and sand dredging
- C. lack of canoes and lack of markets
- D. high prices and poor roads

**Answer: B.** Paragraph 2 names waste flowing in through drains and sand dredging. The other options are not mentioned.

**3. Which of the following best combines the ideas in paragraphs 2 and 3?**

- A. The lagoon has been damaged by waste and dredging, but it could recover if both are controlled.
- B. Dredging is necessary for building projects in Lagos.
- C. Fishermen should look for other jobs.
- D. The lagoon will never recover from pollution.

**Answer: A.** It joins the problem (paragraph 2) and the possible solution (paragraph 3). D contradicts paragraph 3.

**4. The phrase "Some experts believe" in paragraph 3 shows that the recovery of the lagoon is**

- A. certain
- B. impossible
- C. possible, according to some experts
- D. already happening

**Answer: C.** "Believe" and "could" show possibility, not certainty. A turns a possibility into a certainty (distortion).

**5. A suitable title for the passage is**

- A. Lagos State
- B. Canoes at Dawn
- C. The Decline and Possible Recovery of Lagos Lagoon Fishing
- D. Building Projects in Nigeria

**Answer: C.** A is too wide, B is too narrow, D is off the main topic.

**6. "Synthesis of ideas", as defined in the JAMB syllabus, means**

- A. copying the first sentence of each paragraph
- B. combining separate pieces of information to form a complete whole
- C. explaining the meaning of difficult words
- D. writing your own opinion about a passage

**Answer: B.** This is the syllabus definition.

**7. Which of the following should normally be LEFT OUT of a summary?**

- A. The writer's main argument
- B. The topic sentence of each paragraph
- C. Examples and statistics used to illustrate a point
- D. The main cause of the problem discussed

**Answer: C.** Examples and figures are supporting details.

**8. "Some farmers in the north have started to use solar-powered pumps." Which option distorts this sentence?**

- A. A number of northern farmers now use solar pumps.
- B. Solar-powered pumps are being used by some northern farmers.
- C. All farmers in the north now use solar-powered pumps.
- D. Some farmers in the north have begun using solar pumps.

**Answer: C.** "Some" has been changed to "all". The others keep the original meaning.

**9. In a paragraph, the topic sentence is**

- A. always the first sentence
- B. always the last sentence
- C. the sentence that states the main idea, wherever it appears
- D. the longest sentence

**Answer: C.** The topic sentence is often first, but can be anywhere, or implied.

**10. A summary of an argumentative passage should mainly contain**

- A. the writer's position and main reasons
- B. all the examples the writer gives
- C. the reader's opinion on the issue
- D. the rhetorical questions in the passage

**Answer: A.** Examples, rhetorical questions and your own opinion are left out.
