# JAMB Use of English Notes: Synonyms

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Lexis and Structure. It covers:

- Content item (a): **synonyms**.
- Objective ii: **determine similar and opposite meanings of words** (the "similar" half; opposites are in `B2-antonyms.md`).
- Objective i: **identify words and expressions in their ordinary, figurative and idiomatic contexts**, because JAMB tests words in sentences, and the context decides which meaning is intended.

In the examination (Section D of the syllabus), there are **5 synonym questions**. Synonym skills also help you in the cloze test (10 questions), comprehension (vocabulary questions) and sentence interpretation.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Explain what synonyms are, and the difference between exact and near synonyms.
2. Use context to decide which meaning of a word is intended.
3. Account for connotation, register, formality and part of speech when choosing a synonym.
4. Find the meaning of unfamiliar words using roots, prefixes and suffixes.
5. Choose the option "nearest in meaning" to a word or expression in a sentence, using a step-by-step method.
6. Avoid the common JAMB traps in synonym questions.

## What is a synonym?

A **synonym** is a word or expression that has the **same or nearly the same meaning** as another word or expression **in a particular context**.

The word comes from Greek: *syn* (together, same) + *onyma* (name). So synonyms are "same names" for an idea.

Examples:

| Word | Synonym |
| --- | --- |
| begin | start, commence |
| big | large |
| buy | purchase |
| quick | fast, rapid |
| brave | courageous, bold |
| ask | enquire (also spelt inquire) |
| end | finish, conclude |
| help | assist, aid |

### Exact (absolute) synonyms vs near synonyms

Most linguists agree that **exact synonyms are very rare**. Two words almost always differ a little in meaning, feeling, formality or how they are used. Most synonyms are **near synonyms**.

| | Exact (absolute) synonyms | Near synonyms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Identical in every context | Similar, but not identical in every context |
| Can always replace each other? | Yes | No, only in some contexts |
| How common | Very rare | Very common; most synonyms are like this |
| Example | "Sofa" and "settee" are often given as close to exact | "Big" and "large": "a large size" ✓, "a big brother" (older brother) ≠ "a large brother" |

This is why JAMB asks for the option **"nearest in meaning"**, not "the same meaning". You are choosing the **best** match in that sentence, not a perfect one.

## Why context matters

Many English words have **more than one meaning**. The sentence decides which meaning is intended.

Consider the word **"sound"**:

| Sentence | Meaning of "sound" | Synonym |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The doctor said the patient was of **sound** mind. | healthy, not damaged | sane, healthy |
| The bridge is **sound**; it will not collapse. | strong, in good condition | solid, sturdy |
| She gave us **sound** advice. | sensible, reliable | wise, good |
| The child fell into a **sound** sleep. | deep | deep |
| We heard a strange **sound**. | noise | noise |

Now consider **"fair"**:

| Sentence | Meaning | Synonym |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The referee was **fair** to both teams. | just, impartial | just, impartial |
| She has a **fair** complexion. | light-coloured | light |
| His performance was only **fair**. | average | moderate, average |
| They went to the trade **fair**. | exhibition | exhibition, show |

**Rule:** never choose a synonym from memory alone. **Put the option into the sentence** and check that it keeps the sentence's meaning.

## Things to consider when choosing a synonym

### 1. Part of speech

A synonym must normally be the **same part of speech** as the word in the sentence (noun for noun, verb for verb, and so on), and it should fit the sentence grammatically.

> The manager **abandoned** the project.
> "abandoned" is a past-tense verb → choose a past-tense verb: **gave up** / **deserted** / **left**, not "abandonment" (noun).

### 2. Connotation (the feeling a word carries)

Words with similar dictionary meanings (their **denotation**) can carry different feelings (their **connotation**).

| Neutral | Positive connotation | Negative connotation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| thin | slim, slender | skinny, bony, scrawny |
| careful with money | thrifty, economical | stingy, mean, miserly |
| confident | self-assured | arrogant, cocky |
| firm | determined, resolute | stubborn, obstinate |
| curious | inquisitive | nosy |
| old | elderly, mature | aged, decrepit |
| smell | fragrance, aroma (pleasant) | stench, odour (unpleasant) |

If the sentence praises someone, choose a synonym with a positive connotation.

> The teacher praised the **thrifty** student who saved her pocket money.
> Best: **economical** (positive), not "stingy" (negative).

### 3. Register and formality

Some words are **formal**, some **informal** (colloquial or slang). JAMB prefers formal Standard British English.

| Informal | Neutral | Formal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kid | child | offspring, minor |
| guy | man | gentleman |
| get | obtain | acquire |
| buy | buy | purchase |
| ask | ask | enquire |
| end | end | terminate, conclude |
| help | help | assist |
| show | show | demonstrate |

### 4. Collocation

The synonym must sit naturally with the surrounding words. "**Heavy** rain" → "torrential rain" ✓, but not "weighty rain" ✗, even though "heavy" and "weighty" are synonyms in other contexts.

### 5. Literal vs figurative meaning

A word may be used **figuratively** (not in its basic sense). Then its synonym must match the figurative meaning (objective i).

> The news was a **bombshell** to the family.
> Literal: an explosive bomb. Figurative here: **a great shock / a big surprise**.

> The minister **dodged** the journalist's question.
> Literal: moved quickly aside. Figurative here: **avoided / evaded**.

## How JAMB sets synonym questions

The instruction is usually like this (in spirit):

> In each of the questions below, choose the option nearest in meaning to the word or phrase in italics.

The word or phrase is printed in *italics* in a sentence. You choose from four options, A to D. Sometimes the italicised part is a **phrase** or **idiom**, not a single word.

In these notes, the tested word is shown in ***bold italics***.

## Step-by-step method for synonym questions

### Step 1: Read the whole sentence

Do not look only at the italicised word. Understand what the sentence is saying.

### Step 2: Identify the part of speech and the context meaning

Ask: Is the word a noun, verb, adjective or adverb? What does it mean **in this sentence**? Is it literal or figurative? Positive or negative?

### Step 3: Predict a synonym in your own words

Before looking at the options, replace the italicised word with your own simpler word. For example: "The judge was ***impartial***" → "fair, not taking sides".

### Step 4: Compare with the options

Find the option closest to your prediction.

### Step 5: Substitute and test

Put each remaining option into the sentence. The right answer keeps the **meaning, tone and grammar** of the original.

### Step 6: Eliminate the traps

Cross out options that are:

- **antonyms** (opposite meanings) placed to catch careless readers;
- **sound-alike or look-alike** words;
- **related but not equivalent** words (same topic, different meaning);
- **another meaning of the word** that does not fit this context;
- **wrong part of speech**.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** The new principal is a ***meticulous*** woman who checks every detail.

- A. careless
- B. careful
- C. talkative
- D. strict

**Working:** "checks every detail" tells us the meaning: extremely careful about details. A is the opposite (antonym trap). D (strict) is related but not the same: a strict person enforces rules; a meticulous person pays attention to detail. **Answer: B.**

**Example 2.** The governor's speech was full of ***ambiguous*** statements, and nobody knew what he meant.

- A. clear
- B. long
- C. unclear
- D. ambitious

**Working:** "nobody knew what he meant" shows the statements could be understood in more than one way. A is the antonym. D is a look-alike trap (ambiguous / ambitious). **Answer: C.**

**Example 3.** After weeks of argument, the two communities finally ***buried the hatchet***.

- A. went to war
- B. made peace
- C. held a funeral
- D. hid their weapons

**Working:** This is an idiom: to stop quarrelling and become friendly again. C and D are literal interpretation traps. A is the opposite. **Answer: B.** (Idioms: see `B7b-idiomatic-usage.md`.)

**Example 4.** The lecturer's explanation was so ***lucid*** that even the weakest students understood.

- A. clear
- B. lengthy
- C. confusing
- D. loud

**Working:** "even the weakest students understood" → clear. C is the antonym. **Answer: A.**

**Example 5.** The drought ***aggravated*** the food shortage in the region.

- A. reduced
- B. worsened
- C. caused
- D. annoyed

**Working:** "Aggravate" means to make worse. A is the opposite. C is related but different: the drought made an existing shortage worse; it did not create it. D is another meaning of "aggravate" used in informal English ("to annoy"); it does not fit this context (a shortage cannot be annoyed). **Answer: B.**

**Note:** Some usage guides object to "aggravate" meaning "annoy"; others accept it as informal. In JAMB, the safe meaning is "make worse".

**Example 6.** The athlete's ***phenomenal*** speed amazed the spectators at the National Stadium.

- A. ordinary
- B. extraordinary
- C. dangerous
- D. steady

**Answer: B.** "Amazed the spectators" shows it was exceptional. A is the antonym.

**Example 7.** The suspect was ***reticent*** when the police questioned him.

- A. talkative
- B. angry
- C. unwilling to speak
- D. restless

**Answer: C.** "Reticent" means reserved, not revealing one's thoughts. A is the antonym. D is a look-alike trap (some confuse it with "restless").

## Using word parts to work out meanings

Many English words are built from **roots** (often from Latin or Greek), **prefixes** (at the front) and **suffixes** (at the end). Knowing these can help you guess the meaning of an unfamiliar word. Use this as a **guide**, not a guarantee, because some words have changed meaning over time.

### Common prefixes

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bene- | good, well | benefit, benevolent (kind), benefactor |
| mal- | bad, badly | malpractice, malnutrition, malicious |
| pre- | before | predict, precede, premature |
| post- | after | postpone, postwar, posthumous |
| re- | again, back | rebuild, return, revive |
| sub- | under, below | submarine, subordinate, submerge |
| super- | above, beyond | supervise, superior, superfluous |
| inter- | between | international, interrupt, intervene |
| intra- | within | intravenous, intramural |
| trans- | across | transport, transfer, transparent |
| circum- | around | circumference, circumvent, circumspect |
| omni- | all | omnipotent (all-powerful), omniscient (all-knowing) |
| ante- | before | antenatal, antecedent |
| anti- | against | antibiotic, antisocial |
| co-/con-/com- | with, together | cooperate, congregate, compile |
| mis- | wrongly | misunderstand, mislead |

Negative prefixes (un-, in-, dis-, non- and others) are treated in detail in `B2-antonyms.md`.

### Common roots

| Root | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| aud | hear | audible, audience, audition |
| vis/vid | see | visible, vision, video |
| dict | say | predict, dictate, contradict |
| scrib/script | write | describe, manuscript, inscription |
| port | carry | transport, export, portable |
| ject | throw | reject, inject, project |
| spect | look | inspect, spectator, prospect |
| voc/vok | call, voice | vocal, invoke, advocate |
| cred | believe | credible, incredible, credit |
| chron | time | chronological, chronic, synchronise |
| loqu/locu | speak | eloquent, loquacious (talkative), elocution |
| bio | life | biology, biography |
| phil | love | philanthropist, philosophy |
| mort | death | mortal, mortuary |
| fid | faith, trust | fidelity, confide |

### Common suffixes

| Suffix | Meaning or use | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -able/-ible | can be | readable, visible |
| -ous/-ious | full of, having | dangerous, courageous |
| -ful | full of | hopeful, careful |
| -less | without | hopeless, careless |
| -ify/-ise | to make | simplify, modernise |
| -ment/-tion/-ness | noun of action or state | development, education, kindness |
| -ist/-er/-or | person who | scientist, teacher, inventor |

**Worked example:** What does ***benevolent*** mean? bene (good) + vol (wish) + -ent (adjective). So "wishing good" → **kind, generous**.

## Common traps in synonym questions

### Trap 1: The antonym among the options

JAMB often includes the opposite meaning as an option, because a candidate who half-remembers a word may confuse the two. **Always check the direction** (positive or negative, more or less).

### Trap 2: Look-alike and sound-alike words

| Tested word | Look-alike trap | Correct meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| eminent | imminent | famous, distinguished |
| imminent | eminent | about to happen |
| prodigy | prodigal | a very talented young person |
| prodigal | prodigy | wasteful with money |
| credible | credulous | believable |
| credulous | credible | too ready to believe |
| ingenious | ingenuous | clever, inventive |
| ingenuous | ingenious | innocent, naive |
| complacent | complaisant | self-satisfied, unaware of danger |
| official | officious | (officious = too eager to give orders) |
| venal | venial | (venal = open to bribery; venial = pardonable) |

### Trap 3: A real meaning of the word that does not fit the context

As with "aggravate" (Example 5), or "fair" and "sound" above. Test each option in the sentence.

### Trap 4: A related word from the same topic

> The farmer's ***yield*** was poor this year.
> Trap: "crop" (related) or "farm" (related). Better: **harvest / output**.

### Trap 5: Wrong part of speech

> She spoke ***eloquently***.
> Options include "eloquence" (noun) and "fluently" (adverb). The adverb is the match.

### Trap 6: Literal reading of an idiom or figurative expression

See Example 3: "buried the hatchet" does not mean "hid their weapons".

### Trap 7: Overstated or understated meaning

> The noise was ***annoying***.
> "Infuriating" is too strong; "irritating" is a closer match.

## Building your vocabulary

1. **Read widely:** newspapers (editorials and features), novels, magazines.
2. **Keep a vocabulary notebook:** word, meaning, synonym, antonym, an example sentence of your own.
3. **Learn words in families:** "diligent, diligence, diligently".
4. **Use a good dictionary** (for example, an Oxford or Cambridge learner's dictionary); check meaning, part of speech and an example.
5. **Learn synonyms and antonyms together** (see `B2-antonyms.md`).
6. **Practise past-question patterns** daily: five synonym questions a day adds up quickly.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Synonym | A word or expression with the same or nearly the same meaning as another in a given context |
| Near synonym | A word similar in meaning but not interchangeable in every context |
| Exact (absolute) synonym | A word identical in meaning in every context (very rare) |
| Context | The surrounding words and situation that decide which meaning a word has |
| Denotation | The basic dictionary meaning of a word |
| Connotation | The feelings or associations a word carries (positive or negative) |
| Register | The level or field of language (formal, informal, technical) |
| Collocation | Words that naturally go together |
| Literal meaning | The basic, ordinary sense of a word |
| Figurative meaning | A non-literal, extended sense of a word |
| Root | The core part of a word that carries its basic meaning |
| Prefix / suffix | A part added to the front / end of a word to change its meaning or class |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Read the whole sentence**, not just the italicised word. Context decides the meaning.
- **Predict first**, then look at the options.
- **Substitute each option** into the sentence; the right one keeps the meaning, tone and grammar.
- **Watch out for the antonym option**. It is often there on purpose.
- **Do not be fooled by look-alike words** (eminent/imminent, ingenious/ingenuous).
- **Match the part of speech.**
- **For idioms, think of the whole expression.** Never interpret word by word.
- **Choose the "nearest" meaning.** If no option is perfect, pick the best fit.
- **Use British English meanings and spellings** (enquire/inquire, programme, colour).

## Quick summary

1. A synonym is a word or expression with the same or nearly the same meaning as another in a given context; JAMB sets 5 synonym questions.
2. Exact synonyms are rare; most are near synonyms, which is why JAMB asks for the option "nearest in meaning".
3. Context decides meaning: many words (sound, fair, aggravate) have several meanings, and the synonym must fit the sentence.
4. Consider part of speech, connotation, register, collocation and whether the use is literal or figurative.
5. Method: read the sentence, identify the meaning in context, predict, compare, substitute and eliminate traps.
6. Common traps: the antonym option, look-alike words, another meaning of the word, related words, wrong part of speech and literal reading of idioms.
7. Roots, prefixes and suffixes can help you work out unfamiliar words.

## Practice questions

In each question, choose the option nearest in meaning to the word or phrase in italics.

**1. The chairman's speech was so _verbose_ that many guests fell asleep.**

- A. brief
- B. wordy
- C. loud
- D. interesting

**Answer: B.** "Verbose" means using more words than needed. A is the antonym. The guests falling asleep supports "wordy".

**2. The flood victims received _succour_ from the Red Cross.**

- A. help
- B. food
- C. money
- D. sympathy

**Answer: A.** "Succour" means help or assistance in time of difficulty. Food and money are specific kinds of help (too narrow); sympathy is a feeling, not active help.

**3. The new policy is meant to _curb_ the rising cost of food.**

- A. increase
- B. restrain
- C. explain
- D. announce

**Answer: B.** "Curb" (verb) means to restrain or keep in check. A is the opposite.

**4. Her _candid_ remarks about the school's problems surprised the visitors.**

- A. rude
- B. frank
- C. secret
- D. foolish

**Answer: B.** "Candid" means honest and direct. "Rude" is a trap: frankness is not necessarily rude.

**5. The suspect was _exonerated_ after a long trial.**

- A. imprisoned
- B. cleared of blame
- C. sentenced to death
- D. released on bail

**Answer: B.** "Exonerate" means to declare free from blame. D is a trap: bail is temporary release before trial ends, not a declaration of innocence.

**6. The two brothers have been _at daggers drawn_ since their father's death.**

- A. very friendly
- B. bitterly hostile to each other
- C. fighting with knives
- D. sad and depressed

**Answer: B.** The idiom means in a state of bitter hostility. C is a literal trap; A is the opposite.

**7. The _imminent_ arrival of the President caused tight security on the road.**

- A. famous
- B. about to happen
- C. unexpected
- D. delayed

**Answer: B.** "Imminent" means about to happen. A is the meaning of the look-alike "eminent".

**8. The scientist made an _ingenious_ device for purifying water.**

- A. innocent
- B. expensive
- C. clever and inventive
- D. simple

**Answer: C.** "Ingenious" means clever and original. A is the meaning of the look-alike "ingenuous".

**9. The mechanic's explanation was quite _lucid_.**

- A. long
- B. clear
- C. confusing
- D. technical

**Answer: B.** "Lucid" means clear and easy to understand. C is the antonym.

**10. There is a _dearth_ of qualified teachers in many rural schools.**

- A. shortage
- B. plenty
- C. death
- D. training

**Answer: A.** "Dearth" means a scarcity or lack. B is the antonym; C is a look-alike/sound-alike trap.
