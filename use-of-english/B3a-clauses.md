# JAMB Use of English Notes: Clauses

## What JAMB expects

This topic is Section B of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Lexis and Structure, content item **(c) clause and sentence patterns**. These notes cover **clauses**. Sentence patterns (how clauses and other parts combine into sentences) are in `B3b-sentence-patterns.md`.

It covers these objectives:

- **Section B, objective iv:** identify various grammatical patterns in use.
- **Section B, objective v:** interpret information conveyed in sentences (you cannot interpret a long sentence without seeing its clauses).
- **Section A, objective iii:** identify the grammatical functions of words, phrases, **clauses** and figurative/idiomatic expressions. JAMB comprehension passages often ask: "The clause *'...'* as used in the passage is a/an ...". The skills in these notes answer those questions too.

**How many questions?** Clauses are part of "Basic Grammar", which has **10 questions** in Section B. Clause questions also appear among the 5 comprehension questions in Section A.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Tell the difference between a word, a phrase, a clause and a sentence.
2. Identify main (independent) clauses and subordinate (dependent) clauses.
3. Tell finite clauses from non-finite clauses.
4. Identify noun clauses and state their functions in a sentence.
5. Identify adjective (relative) clauses, including defining and non-defining clauses.
6. Identify the different kinds of adverbial clauses, including conditional clauses.
7. Answer JAMB questions of the type "The clause in italics is a/an ..." and "The clause functions as ...".

**A note on how clauses are marked:** in the real JAMB paper, the tested clause is usually **underlined** or printed in *italics*. In these notes, the tested clause is always printed in *italics*.

## Building blocks: word, phrase, clause and sentence

English grammar works in layers, from small units to big ones:

**word → phrase → clause → sentence**

| Unit | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word | The smallest unit that can stand alone with a meaning | *market* |
| Phrase | A group of words that works as a unit but does **not** have a subject and a finite verb together | *at the busy market* |
| Clause | A group of words that has a **subject** and a **verb** (usually a finite verb) | *my mother sells pepper at the busy market* |
| Sentence | One or more clauses that express a complete thought; written with a capital letter and a full stop (or ? or !) | *My mother sells pepper at the busy market because she wants to pay our school fees.* |

### Phrase vs clause: the key difference

A **phrase** has no subject-plus-finite-verb combination. A **clause** has one.

| Phrase | Clause |
| --- | --- |
| *after the rain* | *after the rain stopped* |
| *in the morning* | *when the morning came* |
| *because of the strike* | *because the doctors went on strike* |
| *the boy in the red shirt* | *the boy who is wearing the red shirt* |

**Test:** look for a verb that shows tense and agrees with a subject. *Stopped*, *came*, *went*, *is wearing* all have subjects (*the rain*, *the morning*, *the doctors*, *who*). The phrases on the left have no such verb.

**Note on terms:** some grammar books (especially modern ones) also call groups like *to win the match* or *having finished his work* "non-finite clauses" (see below). Older Nigerian school books often call these "phrases" (infinitive phrases, participial phrases). Both names are used. In JAMB, if the options include "phrase" and the group has no finite verb, the answer expected is usually a **phrase** type. Read all the options carefully.

## Main clauses and subordinate clauses

### Main (independent) clause

A **main clause** can stand alone as a complete sentence. It makes full sense on its own.

- *Tunde passed the exam.*
- *The rain fell heavily.*
- *We stayed at home.*

Other names: **independent clause**, **principal clause**.

### Subordinate (dependent) clause

A **subordinate clause** cannot stand alone. It depends on a main clause to complete its meaning. It usually begins with a **subordinator** (a subordinating conjunction or a relative word).

- *because he studied hard* (Because he studied hard ... what?)
- *when the rain fell* (When the rain fell ... what happened?)
- *who lives next door* (Who lives next door ... what about them?)

Put them together with a main clause:

- **Tunde passed the exam** *because he studied hard*.
- *When the rain fell*, **we stayed at home**.
- **The woman** *who lives next door* **is a nurse**.

Other names: **dependent clause**, **subordinate clause**.

### Comparison table

| Feature | Main (independent) clause | Subordinate (dependent) clause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can it stand alone as a sentence? | Yes | No |
| Usually begins with | The subject (or a coordinating conjunction in compound sentences) | A subordinator: *because, when, if, although, that, who, which, whether*, etc. |
| Makes complete sense? | Yes | No, it leaves the reader waiting |
| Example | *The bus left.* | *before we arrived* |

### Coordinate clauses

When two main clauses are joined by a **coordinating conjunction** (*and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet*), each is called a **coordinate clause**. Neither depends on the other.

- *Ngozi cooked the rice* **and** *Emeka fried the plantain.*
- *I called him twice*, **but** *he did not answer.*
- *You can pay now* **or** *you can pay at the end of the month.*

Each coordinate clause could stand alone. This is different from a subordinate clause.

## Finite and non-finite clauses

### Finite clauses

A **finite clause** has a **finite verb**: a verb that shows **tense** (past or present) and usually **agrees** with its subject.

- *She **sells** tomatoes.* (present tense, agrees with *she*)
- *She **sold** tomatoes.* (past tense)
- *They **are** waiting.* (present, plural)

Main clauses are always finite. Most subordinate clauses in JAMB questions are finite.

### Non-finite clauses

A **non-finite clause** has a verb that does **not** show tense or agreement. There are three main forms:

| Type | Verb form | Example (non-finite clause in italics) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *to*-infinitive | *to* + base form | *To pass JAMB*, you must practise every day. |
| *-ing* participle | verb + *-ing* | *Walking along the beach*, we saw a fishing boat. |
| *-ed* (past) participle | verb + *-ed*, *-en*, etc. | *Built in 1960*, the hall needs repairs. |

Compare:

| Finite | Non-finite |
| --- | --- |
| *When he finished his homework*, he went to bed. | *Having finished his homework*, he went to bed. |
| *Because she was tired*, she slept early. | *Being tired*, she slept early. |
| *If it is used carefully*, this machine will last long. | *Used carefully*, this machine will last long. |

**Terminology difference:** as noted above, modern grammars (such as Quirk and colleagues' *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*) call these **non-finite clauses**. Many Nigerian school textbooks call the same groups **infinitive phrases** and **participial phrases**. You should recognise both names.

### The dangling (misrelated) participle

A non-finite clause at the start of a sentence must refer to the **subject** of the main clause. If it does not, the sentence is wrong (or funny).

- ✗ *Walking to school, the rain started.* (This says the rain was walking to school.)
- ✓ *Walking to school, we were caught in the rain.*
- ✗ *Having eaten the food, the plates were washed.* (The plates did not eat the food.)
- ✓ *Having eaten the food, Amaka washed the plates.*

JAMB likes to test this in "choose the correct sentence" questions.

## The three types of subordinate clause

Subordinate clauses are named by the **job they do** in the sentence. They do the same jobs as single words:

| Clause type | Does the job of a ... | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun clause | noun | I know *that she is honest*. (compare: I know *the truth*.) |
| Adjective (relative) clause | adjective | The boy *who won the prize* is my cousin. (compare: The *clever* boy is my cousin.) |
| Adverbial clause | adverb | We left *when the meeting ended*. (compare: We left *early*.) |

**Key idea:** the type of a clause depends on its **function** in the sentence, not on the word that begins it. The same word (*that*, *when*, *where*) can begin different kinds of clause. You must look at what job the clause is doing.

## Noun clauses

A **noun clause** is a subordinate clause that does the work of a **noun**. If you can replace the clause with a noun or pronoun (*it*, *something*, *the fact*), it is probably a noun clause.

### Words that often begin noun clauses

*that, whether, if* (meaning "whether"), *what, whatever, who, whoever, whom, whose, which, when, where, why, how*

Some noun clauses have no introducing word: *I think* *(that) he is right*. Here *that* has been left out.

### Functions of the noun clause

JAMB often asks: "The clause in italics functions as the ...". These are the main functions:

#### 1. Subject of a verb

The noun clause comes before the main verb and is what the sentence is about.

- *What she said* surprised everyone. (verb: *surprised*)
- *That the governor will visit our school* is now certain.
- *Whoever wins the election* will face many problems.
- *How the thief escaped* remains a mystery.

**Test:** ask "What surprised everyone?" Answer: *what she said*. So it is the subject.

#### 2. Object of a verb

The noun clause comes after a transitive verb and receives the action.

- I know *that you are tired*.
- The teacher asked *why we were late*.
- Nobody knows *where the money went*.
- She wondered *whether the shop was open*.

**Test:** ask "Know what?" "Asked what?" The answer is the clause.

#### 3. Complement of a verb (subject complement)

The noun clause comes after a linking verb such as *be*, *seem*, *become*, and tells us what the subject is.

- The problem is *that we have no money*.
- My fear was *that the bridge would collapse*.
- This is *what I wanted*.

**Test:** the clause renames the subject: "The problem = that we have no money."

#### 4. Object of a preposition

The noun clause comes after a preposition (*about, of, on, for, to, in*, etc.).

- We talked about *what we would do after secondary school*.
- She was worried about *whether she would pass*.
- Give the book to *whoever asks for it*.
- It depends on *how much it costs*.

#### 5. In apposition to a noun

The noun clause comes after a noun (often *fact, news, idea, belief, hope, rumour, claim*) and explains what that noun is.

- The news *that the strike was over* made everyone happy. (The news = the strike was over.)
- The fact *that he lied* is unforgivable.
- Her belief *that hard work pays* kept her going.

**Some books** call this a "noun clause in apposition"; others call it an "appositive clause" or a "complement of the noun". It is the same thing.

#### 6. Extraposed subject (after "It")

English often moves a long noun-clause subject to the end and puts *it* in its place:

- *That he failed* is surprising. → It is surprising *that he failed*.
- *Whether we go or not* doesn't matter. → It doesn't matter *whether we go or not*.

In JAMB, the clause *that he failed* in "It is surprising that he failed" is still a **noun clause**. Books differ on how to name its function: some say "subject" (because it is the real subject), others say "in apposition to *it*" or "extraposed subject". If the options include "subject of the verb" and nothing better, choose it.

### Noun clause or adjective clause? The "that" test

Both noun clauses and adjective clauses can begin with *that*. Here's how to tell them apart:

| | Noun clause (appositive) | Adjective (relative) clause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Example | The news *that he won the prize* pleased us. | The news *that he brought* pleased us. |
| Can you replace *that* with *which*? | No: "The news which he won the prize" makes no sense | Yes: "The news which he brought" |
| Is the clause complete without the noun? | Yes: "he won the prize" is a full idea | No: "he brought" — brought what? Something is missing |
| What does the clause do? | Says **what** the news was | Says **which** news it was |

**Simple test:** if *that* can be replaced by *which* (or *who*), it is a **relative (adjective) clause**. If not, it is a **noun clause**.

## Adjective (relative) clauses

An **adjective clause** is a subordinate clause that does the work of an **adjective**: it describes or identifies a noun or pronoun. It usually comes right after the noun it describes.

Other names: **relative clause**, **adjectival clause**. All three names mean the same thing.

### Relative words that begin adjective clauses

| Relative word | Used for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *who* | people (subject) | The nurse *who treated me* was kind. |
| *whom* | people (object) | The nurse *whom I met* was kind. |
| *whose* | possession (people and things) | The girl *whose bag was stolen* reported to the police. |
| *which* | things, animals | The car *which he bought* is a Toyota. |
| *that* | people or things (in defining clauses) | The car *that he bought* is a Toyota. |
| *where* | places | This is the village *where my father was born*. |
| *when* | times | I remember the day *when we moved to Abuja*. |
| *why* | reasons (after *reason*) | That is the reason *why she left*. |

Sometimes the relative word is left out when it is the **object** of the clause:

- The book *(that) I borrowed* is on the table.
- The man *(whom) we saw* is a pilot.

These are called **contact clauses** or **zero relative clauses** in some books.

### Who or whom?

- Use **who** when it is the **subject** of the clause: The man *who called you* is outside. (*who* called you)
- Use **whom** when it is the **object** of the clause or after a preposition: The man *whom you called* is outside. (you called *him*); The man *to whom I spoke* is outside.

**Test:** answer the clause with *he/him*. If *he* fits, use *who*. If *him* fits, use *whom*.

- "The candidate (who/whom) we believe will win ..." → *we believe **he** will win* → **who**.
- "The candidate (who/whom) we elected ..." → *we elected **him*** → **whom**.

**Usage note:** in everyday speech, many native speakers use *who* for both. Grammar books agree that *whom* is formal and is still expected in formal writing. JAMB tests formal Standard British English, so use *whom* for objects.

### Defining and non-defining relative clauses

This is a very common exam area.

#### Defining (restrictive) relative clauses

A **defining clause** tells us **which** person or thing we mean. Without it, the meaning is incomplete or changes. **No commas.**

- The students *who failed the test* will write it again. (Only the students who failed, not all students.)
- The phone *that I bought last week* has stopped working.

#### Non-defining (non-restrictive) relative clauses

A **non-defining clause** gives **extra information** about a noun that is already clear. You could remove it and the main meaning stays. **Commas** go around it. You **cannot** use *that* in a non-defining clause.

- My father, *who is a farmer*, lives in Ogbomoso. (I have only one father; the clause just adds information.)
- Lagos, *which is Nigeria's largest city*, is very crowded.
- ✗ Lagos, *that is Nigeria's largest city*, is very crowded. (Wrong: no *that* with commas.)

#### Comparison

| Feature | Defining clause | Non-defining clause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job | Identifies which one | Adds extra information |
| Commas | No | Yes |
| Can use *that*? | Yes | No |
| Can the relative word be left out? | Yes, if it is the object | No |
| Removing it | Changes or breaks the meaning | Main meaning stays |

#### How commas change meaning (interpretation questions)

- (a) *The students who were tired went home.* → Only the tired students went home. Some students stayed.
- (b) *The students, who were tired, went home.* → All the students went home, and all of them were tired.

JAMB's "sentence interpretation" questions sometimes test exactly this difference (see `B8-sentence-interpretation.md`).

### Relative clause or noun clause? Watch "who", "where" and "when"

The same word can begin a noun clause or an adjective clause.

| Sentence | Clause type | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I know the house *where she lives*. | Adjective clause | It describes the noun *house* |
| I know *where she lives*. | Noun clause | It is the object of *know*; there is no noun before it to describe |
| The day *when he arrived* was sunny. | Adjective clause | Describes *day* |
| Tell me *when he arrived*. | Noun clause | Object of *tell* (the thing told) |
| We left *when he arrived*. | Adverbial clause (time) | Tells us when we left; modifies the verb *left* |
| The man *who called* is my uncle. | Adjective clause | Describes *man* |
| I don't know *who called*. | Noun clause | Object of *know* |

**Rule of thumb:**

- If the clause comes **right after a noun** and tells you **which** one → adjective clause.
- If the clause can be replaced by **"it" or "something"** → noun clause.
- If the clause answers **when? where? why? how? on what condition? in spite of what?** about the action of the verb → adverbial clause.

## Adverbial clauses

An **adverbial clause** is a subordinate clause that does the work of an **adverb**. It tells us **when, where, why, how, on what condition, in spite of what, with what result** or **to what degree** something happens. It usually modifies the verb of the main clause.

Adverbial clauses can usually move to the front or end of the sentence:

- We stayed indoors *because it was raining*.
- *Because it was raining*, we stayed indoors.

When the adverbial clause comes first, it is usually followed by a comma.

### Kinds of adverbial clauses

JAMB questions often ask for the **kind**: "The clause in italics is an adverbial clause of ...". Learn the main kinds and their subordinators.

| Kind | Answers the question | Common subordinators | Example (clause in italics) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time | When? | *when, while, as, before, after, since, until/till, as soon as, once, whenever* | *As soon as the bell rang*, the pupils rushed out. |
| Place | Where? | *where, wherever* | Sit *wherever you like*. |
| Reason (cause) | Why? | *because, as, since, now that, seeing that* | *Since you are here*, help me carry this bag. |
| Purpose | For what purpose? | *so that, in order that, lest* | She saved money *so that she could pay her fees*. |
| Result (consequence) | With what result? | *so ... that, such ... that, so that* | He was *so* weak *that he could not stand*. |
| Condition | On what condition? | *if, unless, provided (that), providing, as long as, in case, on condition that* | *If it rains*, the match will be postponed. |
| Concession (contrast) | In spite of what? | *although, though, even though, even if, while, whereas, however* (+ adjective), *no matter how* | *Although he was sick*, he wrote the exam. |
| Manner | How? | *as, as if, as though, the way* | She speaks *as if she were the principal*. |
| Comparison (degree) | Compared with what? To what degree? | *than, as ... as, so ... as* | Chidi is taller *than his brother is*. |

### Tricky subordinators: the same word, different kinds

| Word | Kind | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *since* | Time | I have lived here *since I was born*. |
| *since* | Reason | *Since you won't listen*, I'll stop talking. |
| *as* | Time | *As I was leaving*, the phone rang. |
| *as* | Reason | *As it was late*, we took a taxi. |
| *as* | Manner | Do it *as I showed you*. |
| *as* | Comparison | She is as tall *as her mother is*. |
| *while* | Time | *While we were eating*, NEPA took the light. |
| *while* | Concession/contrast | *While I agree with you*, I can't support the plan. |
| *so that* | Purpose | He woke early *so that he could catch the first bus*. (aim) |
| *so ... that* | Result | He woke *so* early *that he caught the first bus*. (what happened) |

**Purpose vs result:** a purpose clause gives the **aim** (often with *can, could, may, might, will, would* inside it). A result clause gives the **outcome** that actually happened (often after *so* + adjective/adverb or *such* + noun).

- Purpose: *She studied hard so that she would pass.* (aim — we don't know if she passed)
- Result: *She studied so hard that she passed.* (outcome — she passed)

**A note on "NEPA took the light":** this is a common Nigerian expression (NEPA was the former name of the electricity authority). It is fine as an example sentence for clauses, but in formal written English you would write "there was a power cut".

### Conditional clauses in more detail

Conditional clauses are adverbial clauses of condition. Grammar books usually describe these main types. (The numbering "first, second, third" is common in British teaching books; some Nigerian texts call them "open", "hypothetical/improbable" and "impossible/unfulfilled" conditions. These are the same types.)

| Type | Meaning | If-clause | Main clause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | General truths, facts | present simple | present simple | *If you heat ice*, it melts. |
| First (open) | Real, possible future | present simple | *will* + base verb | *If it rains tomorrow*, we will stay at home. |
| Second (hypothetical) | Unreal or unlikely present/future | past simple | *would* + base verb | *If I had a car*, I would drive to Abuja. |
| Third (unfulfilled) | Unreal past; it did not happen | past perfect (*had* + past participle) | *would have* + past participle | *If he had studied*, he would have passed. |

**Common errors:**

- ✗ *If I will see him, I will tell him.* → ✓ *If I see him, I will tell him.* (No *will* in the if-clause of a first conditional, in normal use.)
- ✗ *If he would have come, we would have finished.* → ✓ *If he had come, we would have finished.*
- ✗ *If I was you, I would go.* → In formal English: ✓ *If I were you, I would go.* (Many speakers use *was* informally, and some books accept it. In JAMB, choose *were* in "If I/he/she were ..." for hypothetical conditions. More on this in `B5a-mood-tense-and-aspect.md`.)

**Inverted conditionals** (formal) have no *if*:

- *Had I known*, I would have come. (= If I had known)
- *Were she to apply*, she would get the job. (= If she were to apply)
- *Should you need help*, call me. (= If you should need help)

These are still conditional clauses.

**Unless** means "if ... not":

- *Unless you hurry*, you will miss the bus. = If you do not hurry, you will miss the bus.
- ✗ *Unless you don't hurry, you will miss the bus.* (double negative: wrong)

## How to identify a clause in a JAMB question: step by step

**Step 1. Find the verb in the italic group.** Is there a finite verb with a subject? If not, it may be a phrase (or a non-finite clause). Check the options.

**Step 2. Look at the word that begins it.** Note it, but don't decide yet — *that, when, where, who* can begin more than one type.

**Step 3. Ask what job the clause does.**

- Can I replace it with **"it"** or **"something"**? → **noun clause**. Then ask: is it the subject, object, complement, object of a preposition or in apposition?
- Does it come **right after a noun** and tell me **which one** or more about it? → **adjective (relative) clause**.
- Does it tell me **when, where, why, how, on what condition, despite what, with what result, for what purpose**? → **adverbial clause** of that kind.

**Step 4. Check the options for the exact name.** JAMB options look like:

- A. adverbial clause of reason
- B. adverbial clause of purpose
- C. noun clause, object of the verb
- D. adjectival clause qualifying "boy"

**Step 5. Test your answer.** Move the clause (adverbial clauses can usually move), replace it with "it" (noun clauses), or check that it follows and describes a noun (adjective clauses).

### Worked example 1

> *Whatever you decide* will be acceptable to us.
>
> The clause in italics is a/an
>
> - A. adverbial clause of concession
> - B. noun clause, subject of the verb "will be"
> - C. adjectival clause qualifying "us"
> - D. noun clause, object of "decide"

- Step 1: verb *decide*, subject *you*. It's a clause.
- Step 3: replace with "it": "*It* will be acceptable to us." Works → noun clause. It comes before the verb *will be* and is what the sentence is about → subject.
- **Answer: B.** Option A is a trap: "whatever" can begin a concession clause ("*Whatever you decide*, I'll support you"), but here the clause is the subject.

### Worked example 2

> The farmer, *whose crops were destroyed by the flood*, received help from the government.
>
> - A. noun clause in apposition to "farmer"
> - B. adverbial clause of reason
> - C. adjectival clause qualifying "farmer"
> - D. adjectival clause qualifying "crops"

- The clause comes right after *farmer* and gives extra information about him → adjective (relative) clause, non-defining (commas).
- It describes *farmer*, not *crops* (*crops* is inside the clause).
- **Answer: C.**

### Worked example 3

> We left the hall early *so that we could beat the traffic on Third Mainland Bridge*.
>
> - A. adverbial clause of result
> - B. adverbial clause of purpose
> - C. adverbial clause of reason
> - D. noun clause, object of "left"

- The clause gives the **aim** of leaving early (*could* inside the clause). → purpose.
- **Answer: B.** A is the trap: result would be "We left *so* early *that we beat the traffic*."

### Worked example 4

> The rumour *that the school would close* spread quickly.
>
> - A. adjectival clause qualifying "rumour"
> - B. noun clause in apposition to "rumour"
> - C. adverbial clause of result
> - D. noun clause, object of "spread"

- *That* test: "The rumour which the school would close" — makes no sense. So it is not a relative clause.
- The clause says what the rumour was → noun clause in apposition.
- **Answer: B.**

### Worked example 5

> *Had the driver been more careful*, the accident would not have happened.
>
> - A. adverbial clause of time
> - B. adverbial clause of condition
> - C. adverbial clause of reason
> - D. noun clause, subject of the sentence

- *Had the driver been* = *If the driver had been*. It's an inverted third conditional.
- **Answer: B.**

## Clauses and punctuation (link to mechanics)

- A **non-defining** relative clause has commas around it. A **defining** one has none.
- An **adverbial clause at the start** of a sentence is usually followed by a comma: *When the rain stopped, we went out.* At the end, a comma is usually not needed: *We went out when the rain stopped.*
- Two **main clauses** joined without a conjunction need a full stop or a semicolon, not a comma: ✗ *I was tired, I went to bed.* ✓ *I was tired; I went to bed.* ✓ *I was tired, so I went to bed.* (A comma alone between two main clauses is called a **comma splice**.)
- A **noun clause** used as a subject or object is **not** separated from its verb by a comma: ✗ *What he said, was true.* ✓ *What he said was true.*

More on punctuation in `B6b-punctuation-and-capitalisation.md`.

## Common errors involving clauses

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The man whom I think is guilty has fled. | The man **who** I think is guilty has fled. | *who* is the subject of *is guilty* ("I think he is guilty") |
| This is the house which I live. | This is the house **in which** I live / **where** I live / **which I live in**. | *live* needs a preposition before a place |
| My brother who lives in Kaduna, is a soldier. | My brother, who lives in Kaduna, is a soldier. (if I have one brother) | Non-defining clauses need two commas |
| Unless you don't work hard, you will fail. | Unless you work hard, you will fail. | *unless* already means "if not" |
| Although he was rich, but he was not happy. | Although he was rich, he was not happy. / He was rich, but he was not happy. | Use *although* or *but*, not both |
| Because he was late, so he was punished. | Because he was late, he was punished. / He was late, so he was punished. | Use *because* or *so*, not both |
| The reason why he failed is because he was lazy. | The reason he failed is **that** he was lazy. | *the reason ... is because* is a repetition; formal English prefers *the reason ... is that*. (Some modern dictionaries accept *because* here in informal use.) |
| Being a rainy day, we stayed home. | It being a rainy day, we stayed home. / As it was a rainy day, we stayed home. | Dangling participle: *we* were not a rainy day |
| If I will see him, I will tell him. | If I see him, I will tell him. | First conditional: present tense in the if-clause |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Clause | A group of words with a subject and a verb |
| Phrase | A group of words without a subject-plus-finite-verb, working as a unit |
| Main (independent) clause | A clause that can stand alone as a sentence |
| Subordinate (dependent) clause | A clause that cannot stand alone; it depends on a main clause |
| Coordinate clause | One of two or more main clauses joined by *and, but, or*, etc. |
| Subordinator | A word that introduces a subordinate clause, e.g. *because, if, that, who* |
| Finite verb | A verb that shows tense and agrees with its subject, e.g. *goes, went* |
| Non-finite clause | A clause whose verb shows no tense: *to*-infinitive, *-ing* or *-ed* participle |
| Noun clause | A subordinate clause that does the job of a noun |
| Adjective (relative) clause | A subordinate clause that describes a noun or pronoun |
| Defining clause | A relative clause that identifies which person or thing is meant; no commas |
| Non-defining clause | A relative clause that adds extra information; set off by commas |
| Adverbial clause | A subordinate clause that does the job of an adverb (time, reason, condition, etc.) |
| Apposition | When a word, phrase or clause renames or explains the noun next to it |
| Dangling participle | A participle clause that does not refer to the subject of the main clause |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Function decides the type, not the first word.** *When*, *where*, *that* and *who* can each begin more than one kind of clause.
- **The "it" test:** if the clause can be replaced by *it* or *something*, it is a noun clause.
- **The "which" test:** if *that* can be replaced by *which* or *who*, the clause is relative (adjective); if not, it may be a noun clause in apposition.
- **Adjective clauses follow their noun.** Name the noun they qualify correctly — JAMB options sometimes name the wrong noun.
- **Purpose vs result:** *so that* + *could/would/might* usually = purpose; *so* + adjective + *that* = result.
- ***Since* and *as*** can show time or reason; read the meaning.
- **Inverted conditionals** (*Had I known ...*, *Were he to ...*, *Should you ...*) are clauses of condition.
- **Non-defining clauses:** two commas, never *that*.
- **Don't double up conjunctions:** not *although ... but*, not *because ... so*.
- **Who vs whom:** use the *he/him* test.

## Quick summary

1. A clause has a subject and a verb; a phrase does not have a subject-plus-finite-verb combination.
2. A main clause can stand alone; a subordinate clause cannot and is usually introduced by a subordinator.
3. Finite clauses have tensed verbs; non-finite clauses use *to*-infinitives, *-ing* or *-ed* participles (many Nigerian textbooks call these "phrases").
4. Noun clauses work as subject, object, complement, object of a preposition, or in apposition to a noun.
5. Adjective (relative) clauses describe a noun; defining clauses have no commas, non-defining clauses have commas and never use *that*.
6. Adverbial clauses show time, place, reason, purpose, result, condition, concession, manner or comparison; *since*, *as* and *while* can show more than one.
7. To name a clause, ask what job it does: replace with "it" (noun), check whether it follows and describes a noun (adjective), or ask when/why/how/on what condition (adverbial).

## Practice questions

In questions 1 to 8, choose the option that best describes the clause in *italics*.

**1. *What the principal announced at the assembly* shocked the students.**

- A. adverbial clause of reason
- B. noun clause, subject of the verb "shocked"
- C. adjectival clause qualifying "principal"
- D. noun clause, object of the verb "announced"

**Answer: B.** The clause can be replaced by "it" ("It shocked the students"), and it comes before the verb *shocked* as its subject.

**2. The woman *whose shop was burgled* has reported to the police.**

- A. adjectival clause qualifying "woman"
- B. adjectival clause qualifying "shop"
- C. noun clause in apposition to "woman"
- D. adverbial clause of reason

**Answer: A.** The clause follows *woman* and tells us which woman. *Shop* is inside the clause, so B is wrong.

**3. Ade worked overtime *so that he could buy a new phone*.**

- A. adverbial clause of result
- B. adverbial clause of reason
- C. adverbial clause of purpose
- D. noun clause, object of "worked"

**Answer: C.** The clause states Ade's aim, with *could* inside it. A result clause would be "He worked *so* hard *that he bought a new phone*".

**4. *Although the road was flooded*, the bus driver continued the journey.**

- A. adverbial clause of condition
- B. adverbial clause of concession
- C. adverbial clause of time
- D. adverbial clause of reason

**Answer: B.** *Although* introduces a contrast: the driver continued in spite of the flood. That is concession.

**5. The fact *that he apologised* did not reduce his punishment.**

- A. adjectival clause qualifying "fact"
- B. noun clause in apposition to "fact"
- C. noun clause, object of "apologised"
- D. adverbial clause of manner

**Answer: B.** *That* cannot be replaced by *which* here ("the fact which he apologised" makes no sense). The clause states what the fact was.

**6. We shall not start the meeting *until the chairman arrives*.**

- A. adverbial clause of condition
- B. adverbial clause of time
- C. noun clause, object of "start"
- D. adverbial clause of place

**Answer: B.** *Until* shows when the meeting will start. It answers "When?".

**7. She is worried about *whether her result will be released*.**

- A. noun clause, object of the preposition "about"
- B. adverbial clause of condition
- C. noun clause, complement of "is"
- D. adjectival clause qualifying "result"

**Answer: A.** The clause comes after the preposition *about* and can be replaced by "it" ("worried about it").

**8. *Had she left home earlier*, she would not have missed the flight.**

- A. adverbial clause of time
- B. adverbial clause of reason
- C. adverbial clause of condition
- D. noun clause, subject of the sentence

**Answer: C.** It means "If she had left home earlier". It is an inverted third conditional.

**9. Choose the option that best completes the gap: The candidate ______ we thought would win came third.**

- A. whom
- B. who
- C. which
- D. whose

**Answer: B.** Inside the clause, the relative word is the subject of *would win* ("we thought **he** would win"). So *who* is correct, not *whom*.

**10. Choose the correct sentence.**

- A. Lagos, that is a coastal city, has many beaches.
- B. Lagos, which is a coastal city, has many beaches.
- C. Lagos which is a coastal city, has many beaches.
- D. Lagos that is a coastal city has many beaches.

**Answer: B.** The clause is non-defining (there is only one Lagos), so it needs two commas and must use *which*, not *that*.
