# JAMB Use of English Notes: Adjectives and Adverbs

## What JAMB expects

This topic is part of Section B of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Lexis and Structure, content item (d) "word classes and their functions". It is the third of four files on word classes:

- B4a: nouns and pronouns
- B4b: verbs
- **B4c: adjectives and adverbs (this file)**
- B4d: prepositions, conjunctions, determiners and interjections

Degree (positive, comparative and superlative), which is part of content item (e), is covered fully in B5c. This file introduces it only where needed.

The objectives this file serves are:

- **Objective iv:** identify various grammatical patterns in use (word order, adjective order, inversion after negative adverbs).
- **Objective v:** interpret information conveyed in sentences (the position of words like "only", "even", "hardly" changes the meaning).
- **Objective i:** identify words and expressions in their ordinary, figurative and idiomatic contexts.

In the exam, adjectives and adverbs are tested mainly in **Basic Grammar** (10 questions, gap-filling), and in **Sentence interpretation** (5 questions). Adverbs like "hardly", "scarcely" and "no sooner" are among the most common traps in the whole paper.

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Say what adjectives and adverbs are and what jobs they do.
2. Name the kinds of adjectives and use them correctly in attributive, predicative and postpositive positions.
3. Put several adjectives in the correct order before a noun.
4. Use noun modifiers and compound adjectives correctly ("a five-year-old boy").
5. Use the right preposition after common adjectives.
6. Name the kinds of adverbs and form them correctly.
7. Choose correctly between adjective and adverb (good/well, hard/hardly, late/lately).
8. Place adverbs correctly, and use inversion after negative adverbs (Hardly … when, No sooner … than).

## Part 1: Adjectives

### What is an adjective?

An adjective is a word that **describes** or **gives more information about** a noun or pronoun. It answers questions such as: What kind? Which one? How many? Whose?

- a **tall** palm tree (what kind?)
- **this** book (which one?)
- **three** goats (how many?)
- **my** bag (whose?)
- She is **intelligent**. (describes the pronoun "she")

**Note on classification:** traditional Nigerian school grammars treat words like "this", "my", "three", "some" and "every" as kinds of adjectives (demonstrative, possessive, numeral, quantitative and distributive adjectives). Many modern grammars (for example, Quirk and colleagues, and Swan's *Practical English Usage*) put these words in a separate class called **determiners**, because they behave differently from true describing words: they come first in the noun phrase, you cannot compare them ("more this" is impossible), and you cannot use most of them after "be" ("The book is my" is impossible). Both approaches appear in JAMB textbooks. This file explains them under the traditional names, and B4d explains the determiner approach. In the exam, the label rarely matters; correct use does.

### Where adjectives go: three positions

| Position | What it means | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Attributive** | Before the noun | a **clever** student, **fresh** fish, the **red** car |
| **Predicative** | After a linking verb (be, seem, become, look, feel …) | The student is **clever**. The fish smells **fresh**. |
| **Postpositive** | Immediately after the noun or pronoun | something **new**, someone **important**, the people **present**, the Attorney **General**, the members **concerned** |

Most adjectives can go in both attributive and predicative positions. Some cannot.

**Predicative only (the "a-" adjectives and a few others):** afraid, alive, alone, asleep, awake, alike, aware, ashamed, ablaze, content, glad (mostly), well (= healthy)

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| the asleep baby | the **sleeping** baby / The baby is **asleep**. |
| an afraid boy | a **frightened** boy / The boy is **afraid**. |
| the alive patients | the **living** patients / The patients are **alive**. |
| the alone woman | the **lonely** woman / The woman is **alone**. (Note: alone and lonely do not mean the same: "alone" = without others; "lonely" = sad because of being alone.) |

**Attributive only:** main, chief, principal, mere, sheer, utter, former, elder, eldest, upper, inner, outer, very (as in "the very man")

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| The reason is main. | That is the **main** reason. |
| My brother is elder than me. | My brother is **older** than me. / He is my **elder** brother. |

**elder vs older:** "elder" and "eldest" are used only before a noun for family members (my **elder** sister, his **eldest** son). For comparison with "than", use "older": She is **older than** I am. "Elder than" is wrong.

**Postpositive adjectives** are used:

- after indefinite pronouns: something **strange**, nobody **else**, anyone **interested**
- in fixed titles and phrases: Secretary **General**, Attorney **General**, Governor-**elect**, heir **apparent**, court **martial**, the Princess **Royal**
- with some -able/-ible adjectives after superlatives: the best room **available**, the only seats **possible**
- when the adjective has its own phrase after it: a basket **full of oranges**, a man **proud of his son**

### Kinds of adjectives (traditional classification)

| Kind | What it does | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Descriptive (qualitative)** | Tells the quality or kind | beautiful, heavy, honest, Nigerian, wooden |
| **Proper** | Formed from a proper noun; always capitalised | Nigerian music, Yoruba culture, Igbo traditions, Hausa language, African art, Christian festivals |
| **Quantitative** | Tells how much | some rice, much water, little time, enough food, all the money |
| **Numeral** | Tells the number or order | **cardinal**: one, two, fifty; **ordinal**: first, second, fiftieth; **multiplicative**: single, double, triple |
| **Demonstrative** | Points out which | this, that, these, those, such |
| **Possessive** | Shows ownership | my, your, his, her, its, our, their |
| **Interrogative** | Asks a question | **Which** road? **What** time? **Whose** pen? |
| **Distributive** | Refers to members one at a time | each, every, either, neither |
| **Emphasising** | Adds emphasis | the **very** man, his **own** house |
| **Exclamatory** | Expresses surprise | **What** a game! **What** rubbish! |

**Possessive adjective vs possessive pronoun.** A possessive adjective (determiner) comes **before** a noun; a possessive pronoun stands **alone**.

- This is **my** book. (adjective) / This book is **mine**. (pronoun)
- **Its** wing is broken. (adjective; no apostrophe) / **It's** = it is. "It's raining."

### Order of adjectives

When two or more describing adjectives come before a noun, English follows a usual order. A common memory aid is **OSASCOMP**:

| Order | Category | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **O**pinion | beautiful, ugly, lovely, delicious, expensive |
| 2 | **S**ize | big, small, tall, long, tiny |
| 3 | **A**ge | old, new, young, ancient |
| 4 | **S**hape | round, square, flat, oval |
| 5 | **C**olour | red, black, green, brown |
| 6 | **O**rigin | Nigerian, Chinese, Igbo, Ghanaian |
| 7 | **M**aterial | wooden, leather, cotton, gold, plastic |
| 8 | **P**urpose (or type) | sleeping (bag), cooking (pot), wedding (dress), football (boots) |

Determiners (a, the, my, this, two) come before all of these.

Examples:

- a **beautiful old** house (opinion, age)
- a **big round wooden** table (size, shape, material)
- **two small black** goats (number, size, colour)
- a **lovely new red** Ankara dress (opinion, age, colour, material/type)
- an **expensive Italian leather** handbag (opinion, origin, material)
- a **tall young** man (size, age)
- an **old Hausa** saying (age, origin)

**Note on variation:** grammar books do not all use the same list. Some put shape before age, some split "size" and "shape" into "size", "shape" and "condition", and some use other memory aids (for example, "OPSHACOM" or "DOSASCOMP" with D for determiner). They all agree on the main pattern: **opinion before facts**, and **colour, origin and material close to the noun**, with **purpose/type right next to the noun**. JAMB options usually differ in obvious ways, so this pattern is enough.

**How to answer an order question, step by step:**

1. Label each adjective with its category (opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose).
2. Put the opinion adjective first.
3. Put purpose/type last (right before the noun).
4. Fill in the rest in order: size, age, shape, colour, origin, material.

**Example:** Choose the best order: "She bought a ______ bag."

- A. leather black beautiful
- B. beautiful black leather
- C. black beautiful leather
- D. beautiful leather black

Beautiful (opinion), black (colour), leather (material). Order: opinion → colour → material. **Answer: B.**

**Commas between adjectives.** Use a comma (or "and") between adjectives of the **same** category (coordinate adjectives): a **long, boring** speech; a **kind, honest** man. Do not use a comma between adjectives of different categories: a **big black** dog (not "a big, black dog" in careful British usage, though some writers use it). Test: if you can put "and" between them naturally, use a comma.

### Nouns used as adjectives (noun modifiers)

A noun can come before another noun and act like an adjective: a **school** bus, a **gold** ring, a **football** match, a **kitchen** table, a **traffic** jam.

**Rule:** the modifying noun is normally **singular**, even if the meaning is plural.

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| a shoes shop | a **shoe** shop |
| a books store | a **book** store / bookshop |
| a teeth brush | a **toothbrush** |
| a five years old boy | a **five-year-old** boy |
| a ten naira note / a ten nairas note | a **ten-naira** note |
| a two weeks holiday | a **two-week** holiday (or **two weeks'** holiday) |
| a three men committee | a **three-man** committee |
| a 200 metres race | a **200-metre** race |

**But:** after the noun, the plural is used normally: The boy is **five years old**. The holiday lasted **two weeks**. The race is **200 metres** long.

**Possessive alternative for time:** "a **week's** holiday", "**two weeks'** notice", "a **day's** work". These are correct too (note the apostrophe after the s for plurals: weeks').

There are a few fixed exceptions where the plural stays, for example: **sports** car, **arms** race, **clothes** shop, **savings** account, **sales** department, **customs** officer, **goods** train. These are learnt one by one.

### Compound adjectives and the hyphen

A compound adjective is two or more words joined to act as one adjective before a noun. They are usually **hyphenated** before the noun.

- a **well-known** writer (but: The writer is **well known**.)
- a **good-looking** man
- a **long-term** plan
- a **ten-storey** building
- an **English-speaking** country
- a **world-famous** musician
- a **last-minute** decision
- a **first-class** result
- a **hard-working** student

**Rule of thumb:** use a hyphen when the compound comes **before** the noun. After the noun (predicative position), many compounds drop the hyphen: "a **well-known** fact" but "the fact is **well known**". Style guides differ on some words, and dictionaries sometimes record "hardworking" as one word. Do not hyphenate an **-ly adverb + adjective**: "a **highly respected** chief" (not "highly-respected"), because "-ly" already shows the first word is an adverb.

### Adjectives used as nouns

Some adjectives can be used with "the" to mean **a whole group of people**. They take a **plural verb** and **no -s**.

- **The rich** are getting richer, and **the poor** are getting poorer.
- **The blind** need our support.
- **The young** should respect **the old**.
- **The wounded** were taken to the hospital.
- **The unemployed** have formed an association.

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| The poors need help. | **The poor** need help. |
| The rich is selfish. | **The rich** are selfish. (meaning rich people in general) |
| A blind was crossing the road. | **A blind man/woman/person** was crossing the road. |

Some nationality words work the same way: **the English**, **the French**, **the Dutch**, **the Swiss**, **the Chinese**, **the Japanese** (all plural). Others have a noun form with -s: the Nigerians, the Ghanaians, the Americans.

### -ing and -ed adjectives

These are participle adjectives. (There is more on them in B4b.)

- **-ing** describes the thing that **causes** the feeling: an **interesting** book, a **tiring** journey, **disappointing** results.
- **-ed** describes the person who **has** the feeling: an **interested** reader, a **tired** traveller, **disappointed** parents.

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I am very interesting in science. | I am very **interested** in science. |
| The film was bored. | The film was **boring**. |
| We were exciting about the trip. | We were **excited** about the trip. |

### Adjectives and their prepositions

Many adjectives are followed by a fixed preposition. JAMB tests these often in gap-fill questions.

| Adjective | Preposition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| afraid | of | She is **afraid of** snakes. |
| fond | of | He is **fond of** pounded yam. |
| proud | of | Her parents are **proud of** her. |
| capable | of | He is **capable of** winning. |
| aware | of | Are you **aware of** the new rule? |
| ashamed | of | He was **ashamed of** his behaviour. |
| full | of | The basket is **full of** oranges. |
| jealous | of | She was **jealous of** her sister. |
| guilty | of | He was found **guilty of** theft. |
| accused | of | He was **accused of** fraud. |
| independent | of | Nigeria became **independent of** Britain in 1960. |
| dependent | on | The town is **dependent on** fishing. |
| keen | on | She is **keen on** football. |
| interested | in | I am **interested in** chemistry. |
| rich | in | Nigeria is **rich in** natural resources. |
| good / bad | at (a skill) | She is **good at** mathematics. |
| good / kind | to (a person) | He was **good to** me. |
| surprised | at / by | We were **surprised at** (or **by**) the result. |
| angry | with (a person); about (a thing) | She was **angry with** him **about** the delay. |
| annoyed | with (a person); about (a thing) | The teacher was **annoyed with** the class. |
| disappointed | with / in (a person); with / at / about (a thing) | I'm **disappointed in** you. They were **disappointed with** the result. |
| similar | to | Your answer is **similar to** mine. |
| married | to | She is **married to** a doctor. (not "married with") |
| responsible | for | Who is **responsible for** this mess? |
| famous | for | Kano is **famous for** its dyeing pits. |
| suitable | for | This dress is **suitable for** the occasion. |
| sorry | for (a person / an action); about (a situation) | I'm **sorry for** shouting. I'm **sorry about** the noise. |
| superior / inferior | to | This brand is **superior to** that one. (not "superior than") |
| senior / junior | to | He is **senior to** me. (not "senior than") |
| prior | to | **Prior to** the exam, read the rules. |
| identical | to / with | The twins' bags are **identical to** each other. (Both "to" and "with" are accepted.) |
| absent | from | He was **absent from** school. |
| different | from (British, preferred) / to (British, informal) / than (mainly American) | My answer is **different from** yours. |

**Different from / to / than.** This is a well-known area of disagreement:

- **different from** is accepted everywhere and is the safest choice, especially in formal British English.
- **different to** is common in everyday British English, but some traditional grammarians criticise it.
- **different than** is common in American English, and British grammars often treat it as less acceptable (though it is useful before a clause: "different than I expected").

For JAMB, choose **different from** when it is offered.

**superior to, inferior to, senior to, junior to, prior to, preferable to** never take "than", because they come from Latin comparatives that already contain the idea of "more". Wrong: "superior than", "more superior to", "more preferable".

## Part 2: Adverbs

### What is an adverb?

An adverb is a word that **modifies** (gives more information about) a **verb**, an **adjective**, **another adverb**, or a **whole sentence**. It usually answers: How? When? Where? How often? To what degree? Why?

| What it modifies | Example | Adverb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A verb | She sang **beautifully**. | beautifully (how she sang) |
| An adjective | The exam was **very** difficult. | very (how difficult) |
| Another adverb | He drove **too** fast. | too (how fast) |
| A whole sentence | **Fortunately**, nobody was hurt. | fortunately (comment on the whole event) |

**Note:** grammar books differ on whether words like "very" and "too" are adverbs or a separate class called **intensifiers** or **degree modifiers**. Most Nigerian school grammars call them adverbs of degree, which is what this file does.

### Kinds of adverbs

| Kind | Answers | Examples | Sentence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Manner** | How? | quickly, carefully, well, badly, fast, loudly | She speaks **softly**. |
| **Place** | Where? | here, there, everywhere, outside, abroad, upstairs, nearby | The children are playing **outside**. |
| **Time** | When? | now, today, yesterday, soon, later, already, yet, recently, then | He arrived **yesterday**. |
| **Frequency** | How often? | always, usually, often, sometimes, seldom, rarely, never, ever, daily | I **always** pray before meals. |
| **Degree** | To what extent? | very, quite, too, rather, fairly, extremely, almost, nearly, hardly, enough | It is **too** hot. |
| **Reason / purpose** | Why? | therefore, hence, so, consequently | He was ill; **therefore**, he stayed at home. |
| **Affirmation / negation** | Yes or no? | certainly, surely, indeed, not, never | I will **certainly** come. |
| **Sentence (comment) adverbs** | The speaker's attitude | fortunately, unfortunately, honestly, clearly, obviously, frankly, hopefully | **Clearly**, he was wrong. |
| **Interrogative** | Ask a question | when, where, why, how | **Where** do you live? |
| **Relative** | Introduce a relative clause | when, where, why | This is the house **where** I was born. |
| **Conjunctive** (linking adverbs) | Link ideas between clauses | however, moreover, therefore, nevertheless, furthermore, otherwise, consequently | It rained; **however**, the match went on. |

**Hopefully as a sentence adverb.** "**Hopefully**, the results will be out soon" (= I hope that…) is now standard in most dictionaries, but some traditional grammarians criticise it and insist "hopefully" should only mean "in a hopeful way" ("She looked at him **hopefully**"). Both uses appear in good modern English.

**Conjunctive adverbs and punctuation.** A conjunctive adverb (however, therefore, moreover) does **not** join two clauses on its own the way "but" or "and" does. Between two full clauses, use a **semicolon** (or a full stop) before it, and usually a comma after it.

- Correct: The road was bad; **however**, we arrived on time.
- Correct: The road was bad. **However**, we arrived on time.
- Wrong: The road was bad, however we arrived on time. (comma splice)

See B6b for punctuation.

### Forming adverbs from adjectives

Most adverbs of manner are formed by adding **-ly** to an adjective. Watch the spelling (also see B6a):

| Rule | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Most adjectives: add -ly | quick → quickly, careful → carefully (note: two l's, because "careful" + "ly") |
| Consonant + y: change y to i, add -ly | happy → happily, easy → easily, busy → busily |
| Adjective ends in -le after a consonant: drop e, add -y | gentle → gently, simple → simply, possible → possibly, terrible → terribly |
| Adjective ends in -ic: add -ally | basic → basically, tragic → tragically, scientific → scientifically. **Exception:** public → **publicly** |
| Adjective ends in -ll: add -y only | full → fully, dull → dully |
| Adjective ends in -ue: drop e | true → **truly**, due → **duly** (not "truely", "duely") |
| Adjective ends in -e (most others): keep e | sincere → sincerely, extreme → extremely, complete → completely. **Exceptions:** whole → **wholly**, true → truly |

Common misspellings to avoid: truely, wholely, publically, basicly, sincerly, definately (correct: **definitely**).

### Adjectives that end in -ly (not adverbs)

Some words ending in -ly are **adjectives**, not adverbs: friendly, lovely, lonely, lively, costly, elderly, ugly, silly, cowardly, likely, motherly, deadly, kindly (also an adverb).

You cannot add another -ly to them. Use a phrase instead:

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| She spoke to me friendly. / friendlily | She spoke to me **in a friendly way** (or **manner**). |
| He behaved cowardly. | He behaved **in a cowardly way**. / He behaved like a coward. |
| They sang lovely. | They sang **beautifully** / **wonderfully**. |

### Flat adverbs: same form as the adjective

Some adverbs have the same form as their adjective: **fast, hard, late, early, high, low, straight, far, near, daily, weekly, long, right, wrong, deep, free**.

- He is a **fast** runner. (adjective) / He runs **fast**. (adverb; "fastly" does not exist)
- She is a **hard** worker. (adjective) / She works **hard**. (adverb)
- The train was **late**. (adjective) / The train arrived **late**. (adverb)

### Pairs with different meanings: hard/hardly, late/lately and others

Some flat adverbs also have an -ly form with a **completely different meaning**. JAMB tests these constantly.

| Flat adverb | Meaning and example | -ly adverb | Meaning and example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **hard** | with effort: She worked **hard**. (She made a big effort.) | **hardly** | almost not: She **hardly** worked. (She did almost no work.) |
| **late** | not on time: He came **late**. | **lately** | recently: Have you seen him **lately**? |
| **high** | at a great height: The eagle flew **high**. | **highly** | very much: He is **highly** respected. |
| **near** | close by: Come **near**. | **nearly** | almost: I **nearly** fell. |
| **free** | without payment: Children travel **free**. | **freely** | without restriction: You may speak **freely**. |
| **short** | suddenly, before the end: He stopped **short**. | **shortly** | soon: The bus will arrive **shortly**. |
| **most** | to the greatest degree: What I want **most** is peace. | **mostly** | mainly: The audience was **mostly** students. |
| **deep** | far down: They dug **deep**. | **deeply** | very much (feeling): She was **deeply** hurt. |
| **just** | very recently; only: He has **just** left. | **justly** | fairly: The judge acted **justly**. |
| **wide** | fully: Open your mouth **wide**. | **widely** | in many places: The book is **widely** read. |

### Good vs well

| Word | Class | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **good** | adjective | describes a noun; after linking verbs | She is a **good** singer. The food tastes **good**. |
| **well** | adverb | describes how an action is done | She sings **well**. (not "sings good") |
| **well** | adjective (= healthy) | after "be", "feel", "look" for health | I was ill, but now I am **well**. You don't look **well**. |

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| He plays the guitar good. | He plays the guitar **well**. |
| The team did good. | The team did **well**. |
| She performed very good in WAEC. | She performed very **well** in WAEC. |

### Adjective or adverb after linking verbs

After a linking verb (be, seem, look, feel, sound, smell, taste, appear, become, grow, remain, turn), use an **adjective**, because it describes the subject. When the same verb describes an **action**, use an **adverb**.

| Linking verb + adjective (describes subject) | Action verb + adverb (describes action) |
| --- | --- |
| The soup **smells good**. | The dog **smelled** the food **carefully**. |
| She **looked angry**. | She **looked angrily** at the boy. |
| The music **sounds sweet**. | |
| The milk **turned sour**. | |
| He **felt bad** about the mistake. | He **felt** the cloth **gently**. |

### Very, too, enough, so and such

| Word | Meaning | Position | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **very** | to a high degree (neutral) | before adjective/adverb | The tea is **very** hot, but I can drink it. |
| **too** | more than is acceptable (negative result) | before adjective/adverb | The tea is **too** hot to drink. (I can't drink it.) |
| **enough** | sufficiently | **after** an adjective/adverb; **before** a noun | She is old **enough** to vote. We have **enough** money. |
| **so** | to such a degree | before adjective/adverb (no noun) | The film was **so** long that I slept. |
| **such** | to such a degree | before (a/an +) adjective + noun | It was **such** a long film that I slept. |

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I love you too much. (meaning "very much") | I love you **very much**. ("too much" means more than is good) |
| He is enough old to drive. | He is old **enough** to drive. |
| It was so a good match. | It was **such** a good match. / The match was **so** good. |
| The box is very heavy to lift. | The box is **too** heavy to lift. |

**Note:** "too … to" has a negative meaning: "He is **too** weak **to** walk" = he cannot walk. JAMB interpretation questions use this.

**"Very" cannot modify most past participles used as verbs.** Use "very much" or "greatly":

- Correct: He was **very much** loved. / He was **greatly** loved.
- Acceptable: I am **very** tired / **very** interested / **very** worried (these -ed words have become true adjectives).

### Already, yet, still

| Word | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **already** | something has happened sooner than expected; mostly positive sentences | The bus has **already** left. |
| **yet** | something expected has not happened; negatives and questions; end of clause | The results have**n't** come out **yet**. Have you eaten **yet**? |
| **still** | something continues; before main verb, after "be" | He is **still** sleeping. She **still** lives in Ilorin. |

### Ago, before, since and for

| Word | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ago** | counts back from **now**; used with the **simple past** | I met him three years **ago**. (not "since three years", not "three years before" unless you mean before another past time) |
| **before** | counts back from a past time; with past perfect | He said he had met her three years **before**. |
| **since** | from a **point** in time until now; usually with the present perfect | I have lived here **since** 2019. |
| **for** | a **period** of time | I have lived here **for** two weeks. |

| Wrong (common Nigerian English) | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I have been here since two weeks. | I have been here **for** two weeks. |
| He travelled since Monday. (meaning he is still away) | He **has been away since** Monday. / He travelled **on** Monday. |
| I saw him since. | I saw him **long ago** / some time **ago**. |

(B5a covers the tense side of these.)

### Position of adverbs

English adverbs can go in three main positions: **front**, **middle** and **end**.

| Position | Typical adverbs | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front (start of clause) | sentence adverbs, linking adverbs, some time adverbs | **Unfortunately**, the bus broke down. **Yesterday** we went to Ibadan. |
| Middle (before the main verb; after "be" and after the first auxiliary) | frequency adverbs (always, often, never, usually, sometimes, seldom), and also, just, already, still, only, even, almost | She **always** arrives early. He is **always** late. They have **never** seen snow. |
| End (after the verb and object) | manner, place, time | She sang the song **beautifully**. We met **at the market** **yesterday**. |

**Middle position rules (frequency adverbs):**

- Before a single main verb: He **often** visits us.
- After "be" as a main verb: She is **often** late.
- After the first auxiliary: I have **never** been to Calabar. You can **always** call me.

**Do not put an adverb between a verb and its object:**

- Wrong: She speaks **very well** English. → Correct: She speaks English **very well**.
- Wrong: I like **very much** rice. → Correct: I like rice **very much**.

**End position order:** when several adverbials come at the end, the usual order is **manner → place → time** (how, where, when):

- She sang **sweetly** (manner) **at the church** (place) **last Sunday** (time).

With verbs of movement (go, come, arrive), place usually comes first: We went **to Abuja** **by road** **last week**.

### "Only" and "even": position changes meaning

"Only" should be placed next to the word it limits. Moving it changes the meaning. JAMB sentence interpretation questions test this.

| Sentence | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Only** Musa passed the test. | Nobody except Musa passed. |
| Musa **only** passed the test. | Musa passed, but did nothing more (e.g. he didn't get a distinction). (In speech, this can also mean "Musa passed only the test", depending on stress.) |
| Musa passed **only** the test. | The test was the only thing Musa passed. |
| Musa passed the test **only** yesterday. | Musa passed it as recently as yesterday. |

| Sentence | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Even** the teacher laughed. | The teacher, who you would not expect to laugh, laughed. |
| The teacher **even** laughed. | The teacher did several things, and also (surprisingly) laughed. |

### Negative adverbs: hardly, scarcely, barely, seldom, rarely, never

**1. They are already negative, so do not add "not".**

| Wrong (double negative) | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I can't hardly see. | I can **hardly** see. |
| She doesn't scarcely eat. | She **scarcely** eats. |
| He never said nothing. | He **never** said **anything**. |

**2. Question tags after them are positive.** (See B5c.) "She hardly ever comes, **does** she?"

**3. Inversion after negative adverbs at the start of a sentence.** When a negative or restrictive adverb begins the sentence, the auxiliary comes **before** the subject, as in a question. This is formal, emphatic English.

| Normal order | Inverted order (emphatic) |
| --- | --- |
| I have never seen such a crowd. | **Never have I** seen such a crowd. |
| He seldom visits us. | **Seldom does he** visit us. |
| She rarely smiles. | **Rarely does she** smile. |
| We had hardly sat down when the light went off. | **Hardly had we** sat down **when** the light went off. |
| He had no sooner arrived than he fell ill. | **No sooner had he** arrived **than** he fell ill. |
| You must not open this door under any circumstances. | **Under no circumstances must you** open this door. |
| He not only sings but also dances. | **Not only does he** sing, **but he also** dances. |
| I realised my mistake only then. | **Only then did I** realise my mistake. |
| They little knew what was coming. | **Little did they** know what was coming. |

**4. The fixed pairs (the biggest JAMB trap):**

| Starter | Partner | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hardly** … | **when** (or "before") | Hardly had the match started **when** it began to rain. |
| **Scarcely** … | **when** (or "before") | Scarcely had she left **when** the phone rang. |
| **Barely** … | **when** | Barely had we arrived **when** the meeting ended. |
| **No sooner** … | **than** | No sooner had the bell rung **than** the students rushed out. |

- "No sooner" is a comparative ("sooner"), so its partner is **than**. Wrong: "No sooner … when", "No sooner … then".
- "Hardly" and "scarcely" are not comparatives, so they never take "than". Wrong: "Hardly … than".
- Normally the **past perfect** follows the starter (had arrived, had started) and the **simple past** follows the partner (rang, rushed).

### Comparison of adverbs (brief)

Adverbs can be compared like adjectives. (Full rules are in B5c.)

- One-syllable flat adverbs add -er/-est: fast, faster, fastest; hard, harder, hardest; late, later, latest; soon, sooner, soonest.
- -ly adverbs use more/most: carefully, more carefully, most carefully.
- Irregular: well, better, best; badly, worse, worst; little, less, least; much, more, most; far, farther/further, farthest/furthest.

## Worked examples: answering adjective and adverb questions in JAMB

**Example 1.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*Hardly had the governor finished his speech ______ the crowd began to cheer.*

- A. than
- B. when
- C. then
- D. that

**How to think:** "Hardly" pairs with "when". "Than" pairs only with "no sooner". **Answer: B.**

**Example 2.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*Bola has been working very ______ for her examinations.*

- A. hardly
- B. hard
- C. hardest
- D. harder

**How to think:** we need "with great effort", which is the flat adverb "hard". "Hardly" means "almost not". "Hardest" and "harder" don't fit after "very". **Answer: B.**

**Example 3.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*The ______ girl won the spelling competition.*

- A. ten-years-old
- B. ten-year-old
- C. ten years old
- D. ten-year's-old

**How to think:** before a noun, the compound adjective is hyphenated and "year" stays singular. **Answer: B.**

**Example 4 (interpretation).** *The boy is too weak to walk.* This means that the boy

- A. walks slowly because he is weak
- B. cannot walk because he is very weak
- C. is weak but can walk
- D. walked until he became weak

**How to think:** "too … to" means the result is negative: he cannot walk. **Answer: B.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Adjective | A word that describes or gives information about a noun or pronoun |
| Attributive adjective | An adjective before the noun: a **tall** man |
| Predicative adjective | An adjective after a linking verb: The man is **tall** |
| Postpositive adjective | An adjective directly after the noun: something **new** |
| Proper adjective | An adjective from a proper noun, always capitalised: **Nigerian** |
| Noun modifier | A noun used like an adjective before another noun: a **school** bus |
| Compound adjective | Two or more words used as one adjective, usually hyphenated: **well-known** |
| Determiner | A word such as a, the, this, my, some, every that introduces a noun (treated as an adjective in some books) |
| Adverb | A word that modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb or a whole sentence |
| Flat adverb | An adverb with the same form as its adjective: fast, hard, late |
| Sentence adverb | An adverb that comments on the whole sentence: **Fortunately**, … |
| Conjunctive adverb | A linking adverb such as however, therefore, moreover |
| Intensifier (degree adverb) | An adverb that shows degree: very, too, quite, extremely |
| Inversion | Putting the auxiliary before the subject, e.g. after a negative adverb: Never **have I** … |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **No sooner … than; Hardly / Scarcely / Barely … when.** Never "no sooner … when" or "hardly … than".
- **Inversion after a negative adverb at the start:** "Never **have I** seen …", "Seldom **does he** …", "Not only **does she** …".
- **No double negatives:** "I can hardly hear" (not "can't hardly").
- **Hard vs hardly, late vs lately, high vs highly, near vs nearly:** they have different meanings.
- **Good is an adjective; well is an adverb** (or an adjective meaning "healthy").
- **Adjective after a linking verb:** "The food smells **good**", "I feel **bad**".
- **Adjective order:** opinion first, then size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose.
- **Singular noun modifiers with hyphens:** a **five-year-old** boy, a **ten-naira** note, a **two-week** course.
- **The + adjective for a group takes a plural verb and no -s:** "The poor **are** …".
- **Too vs very:** "too" means "more than is acceptable"; "too … to" means "cannot".
- **Enough goes after an adjective:** "old enough", not "enough old".
- **Prepositions:** different **from**, superior **to**, married **to**, interested **in**, good **at**, capable **of**, angry **with** a person.
- **Elder vs older:** "elder brother" but "older than".
- **For a period, since a point:** "for two weeks", "since 2019" (never "since two weeks").
- **Don't put an adverb between a verb and its object:** "speaks English well", not "speaks well English".

## Quick summary

1. Adjectives describe nouns and pronouns; they can be attributive (before the noun), predicative (after a linking verb) or postpositive (after the noun).
2. Several adjectives before a noun usually follow the order opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose.
3. Noun modifiers and compound adjectives before a noun are singular and usually hyphenated: a five-year-old boy, a ten-naira note.
4. "The + adjective" (the rich, the poor) names a group and takes a plural verb.
5. Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs and whole sentences; most are formed with -ly, but flat adverbs (fast, hard, late) are not.
6. Some pairs differ in meaning (hard/hardly, late/lately, high/highly), and good is an adjective while well is the adverb.
7. Negative adverbs need no extra "not", and when they start a sentence they cause inversion: No sooner … than; Hardly / Scarcely … when.
8. The position of adverbs like "only" and "even" changes the meaning of a sentence.

## Practice questions

**1. Choose the option that best completes the gap: The ______ boy recited the poem without a mistake.**

- A. five-years-old
- B. five-year-old
- C. five years-old
- D. five-year's-old

**Answer: B.** A compound adjective before a noun is hyphenated, and the noun "year" stays singular.

**2. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Hardly had we entered the hall ______ the lights went out.**

- A. than
- B. then
- C. when
- D. that

**Answer: C.** "Hardly" is paired with "when". Only "no sooner" takes "than".

**3. Choose the option that best completes the gap: No sooner had the final whistle gone ______ the fans ran onto the pitch.**

- A. when
- B. then
- C. than
- D. as

**Answer: C.** "No sooner" contains the comparative "sooner", so its partner is "than".

**4. Choose the option that best completes the gap: For her birthday, Amina was given a ______ bag.**

- A. leather black beautiful
- B. beautiful black leather
- C. black beautiful leather
- D. beautiful leather black

**Answer: B.** The order is opinion (beautiful), colour (black), material (leather).

**5. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Emeka has worked very ______ this term, so he deserves the prize.**

- A. hardly
- B. hard
- C. harder
- D. hardest

**Answer: B.** "Hard" (flat adverb) means "with great effort". "Hardly" means "almost not", which contradicts "deserves the prize".

**6. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Mrs Bello was ill last week, but she is quite ______ now.**

- A. good
- B. well
- C. nicely
- D. healthily

**Answer: B.** "Well" is the adjective meaning "in good health". "Nicely" and "healthily" are adverbs and cannot follow "is" as a complement here.

**7. Choose the option that best completes the gap: The ______ need more support from the government.**

- A. poors
- B. poor
- C. poor's
- D. poors people

**Answer: B.** "The + adjective" (the poor) refers to poor people as a group. It takes no -s and a plural verb ("need").

**8. Which of the following best explains the meaning of "Only Musa passed the test"?**

- A. Musa did nothing except pass the test.
- B. Nobody except Musa passed the test.
- C. Musa passed only this test and failed the others.
- D. Musa barely passed the test.

**Answer: B.** "Only" placed before "Musa" limits the subject: Musa was the only person who passed.

**9. Choose the option that best completes the gap: I can ______ hear you because the generator is too noisy.**

- A. not hardly
- B. hardly
- C. hard
- D. hardly not

**Answer: B.** "Hardly" is already negative, so adding "not" makes a double negative. "Hard" means "with effort", which does not fit.

**10. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Ngozi is not old ______ to vote in the coming election.**

- A. too
- B. very
- C. enough
- D. so

**Answer: C.** "Enough" comes after the adjective: "old enough to vote". "Too old to vote" would mean she cannot vote because she is too old, which does not fit "not".
