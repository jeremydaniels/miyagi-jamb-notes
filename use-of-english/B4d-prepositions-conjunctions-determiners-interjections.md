# JAMB Use of English Notes: Prepositions, Conjunctions, Determiners and Interjections

## What JAMB expects

This topic is part of Section B of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Lexis and Structure, content item (d) "word classes and their functions". It is the last of four files on word classes:

- B4a: nouns and pronouns
- B4b: verbs
- B4c: adjectives and adverbs
- **B4d: prepositions, conjunctions, determiners and interjections (this file)**

The objectives this file serves are:

- **Objective iv:** identify various grammatical patterns in use (which preposition, which conjunction, which article).
- **Objective i:** identify words and expressions in their ordinary, figurative and idiomatic contexts (many idioms depend on the right preposition).
- **Objective v:** interpret information conveyed in sentences ("few" vs "a few", "unless", "lest" all change meaning).

In the exam, these "small words" are tested mainly in **Basic Grammar** (10 questions, gap-filling) and in the **cloze passage** (10 questions in Section A), where many gaps are prepositions, conjunctions and articles. They also appear in sentence interpretation (5 questions).

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Choose the correct preposition of time, place and movement, and the correct preposition after common verbs, nouns and adjectives.
2. Use coordinating, subordinating and correlative conjunctions correctly, with correct concord and parallel structure.
3. Use the articles (a, an, the, and no article) correctly, and use other determiners (demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers) correctly.
4. Recognise interjections and punctuate them correctly.

## A note on how many word classes there are

Grammar books do not all agree on the number of word classes (parts of speech).

- **Traditional school grammars** list **eight**: noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction and interjection. Articles and words like "this", "my" and "some" are treated as adjectives.
- **Many modern grammars** (for example, Quirk and colleagues' *Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language*, and Swan's *Practical English Usage*) add **determiners** as a separate class, and some also separate **articles**, **numerals** or **auxiliaries**.

This file follows the user-friendly middle path found in many Nigerian textbooks: it explains determiners as their own group, but points out the traditional adjective names too. In JAMB, you will be asked to use these words correctly far more often than to name their class. When a question does ask for the class, look at the options: if "determiner" is not offered, the traditional answer is "adjective".

## Part 1: Prepositions

### What is a preposition?

A preposition is a word that shows the **relationship** between a noun or pronoun and another word in the sentence. The relationship is often about **place**, **time**, **direction**, **cause** or **means**.

- The book is **on** the table. (place)
- We will meet **at** noon. (time)
- She walked **into** the classroom. (direction)
- He was absent **because of** illness. (cause)
- They travelled **by** bus. (means)

The noun or pronoun after the preposition is called its **object** (or complement). The preposition and its object together form a **prepositional phrase**: on the table, at noon, into the classroom.

**Pronouns after prepositions are in the object form:** me, him, her, us, them, whom.

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| between you and I | between **you and me** |
| for my brother and I | for **my brother and me** |
| among we students | among **us** students |
| To who did you give it? | To **whom** did you give it? |

**Test:** take away the other person. "for my brother and I" → "for I" (wrong) → "for **me**" (right).

### Kinds of prepositions by form

| Kind | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple** | One word | at, in, on, by, for, from, of, to, with, about, after, before, under, over, through |
| **Compound** | One word made from two parts | into, onto, upon, within, without, throughout, inside, outside, beneath, alongside |
| **Complex (phrasal)** | A group of words acting as one preposition | because of, according to, in spite of, instead of, owing to, due to, in front of, by means of, on behalf of, with regard to, in addition to, apart from, as for, in case of, by way of |
| **Participial** | Formed from a participle | concerning, regarding, considering, including, following, during, pending, notwithstanding |

### Prepositions of time: at, on, in

| Preposition | Used with | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **at** | exact times; meal times; festivals as periods; "night"; "the weekend" (British) | **at** 6 o'clock, **at** noon, **at** midnight, **at** lunchtime, **at** Christmas, **at** night, **at** the weekend |
| **on** | days and dates; specific days | **on** Monday, **on** 1st October, **on** Independence Day, **on** my birthday, **on** Christmas Day, **on** Friday morning |
| **in** | longer periods: months, years, seasons, centuries; parts of the day | **in** June, **in** 1960, **in** the rainy season, **in** the 21st century, **in** the morning, **in** the afternoon, **in** the evening |

**Watch the differences:**

- **in** the morning, but **on** Monday morning (a particular day uses "on")
- **at** night, but **in** the night (= during one particular night: "I woke up **in** the night")
- **at** Christmas (the period), but **on** Christmas Day (the day)
- **at** the weekend (British English) / **on** the weekend (American English). JAMB follows British English.

**No preposition** before: this, next, last, every, each, today, tomorrow, yesterday.

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I will see you on next Monday. | I will see you **next Monday**. |
| He came on last week. | He came **last week**. |
| We meet on every Sunday. | We meet **every Sunday**. |

### Other prepositions of time

| Preposition | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **for** | length of a period | I have lived here **for** five years. |
| **since** | from a point in time until now | I have lived here **since** 2020. |
| **during** | within a period (when something happened) | He slept **during** the lecture. (not "during two hours"; use "for two hours") |
| **by** | not later than (a deadline) | Submit your form **by** Friday. |
| **until / till** | up to a time (continuing action) | The shop is open **until** 9 p.m. |
| **within** | inside a period | You will hear from us **within** two weeks. |
| **before / after** | earlier / later than | **Before** the exam; **after** the match |
| **from … to / till / until** | start and end | The holiday runs **from** July **to** September. |
| **ago** (adverb, comes after) | back from now | I met him three years **ago**. |

**by vs until:**

- "Return the book **by** Friday." (any time before or on Friday; the action happens once)
- "Keep the book **until** Friday." (you keep it continuously up to Friday)

**in time vs on time:**

- **on time** = at the planned time, punctual: The train arrived **on time**.
- **in time** = early enough (before it is too late): We got to the hospital **in time** to see the doctor.

**Since vs for (very common error):**

| Wrong (common Nigerian English) | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I have been waiting since two hours. | I have been waiting **for** two hours. |
| She has been ill for last week. | She has been ill **since** last week. |

### Prepositions of place: at, on, in

| Preposition | Idea | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **at** | a point; a place seen as a location for an activity | **at** the bus stop, **at** the door, **at** school, **at** the market, **at** home, **at** the party, **at** 12 Broad Street |
| **on** | a surface; a line; public transport you can walk around in | **on** the table, **on** the wall, **on** the floor, **on** the River Niger, **on** the bus, **on** the train, **on** the plane, **on** a motorbike, **on** page 5 |
| **in** | inside an enclosed space or area | **in** the box, **in** the room, **in** Lagos, **in** Nigeria, **in** a car, **in** a taxi, **in** the newspaper, **in** the picture |

**Transport:** In British English, you are **on** a bus, train, plane, ship, bicycle or motorbike, but **in** a car or taxi (small vehicles you can't stand up in).

- Correct: She was **on** the bus. / He was sitting **in** the car.
- Means of transport: **by** bus, **by** car, **by** air, **by** road, **by** sea — but **on** foot (not "by foot").

**Addresses:** "**at** 12 Broad Street" (exact address), "**on** Broad Street" (British English also uses "**in** Broad Street"), "**in** Ikeja", "**in** Lagos State".

**at school / in school / in the school:**

- He is **at school**. (He is attending lessons there; British.)
- She is **in school**. (She is still a student; more American, but common.)
- The fight happened **in the school**. (inside the school building)

### Other prepositions of place and direction

| Preposition | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **into** | movement from outside to inside | She ran **into** the house. |
| **in** | position inside (no movement) | She is **in** the house. |
| **onto** | movement to a surface | The cat jumped **onto** the roof. |
| **out of** | movement from inside to outside | He walked **out of** the room. |
| **to** | direction towards a destination | We went **to** Kaduna. |
| **towards** | in the direction of (not necessarily arriving) | He walked **towards** the gate. |
| **across** | from one side to the other (of a flat area or line) | They swam **across** the river. |
| **through** | from one end to the other of something enclosed | The train went **through** the tunnel. |
| **along** | following a line | We walked **along** the beach at Badagry. |
| **over** | directly above (often touching or covering); across the top | She put a cloth **over** the food. The plane flew **over** Abuja. |
| **above** | higher than (not touching) | The fan is **above** the bed. |
| **under** | directly below | The cat is **under** the table. |
| **below** | lower than | The temperature fell **below** zero. |
| **beneath** | under (formal or literary) | **beneath** the surface |
| **between** | in the space separating two (or more) distinct things | On the road north, Kaduna is **between** Abuja and Kano. |
| **among / amongst** | in the middle of a group | She sat **among** her friends. |
| **beside** | next to | Sit **beside** me. |
| **besides** | in addition to; apart from | **Besides** English, she speaks Hausa and French. |
| **opposite** | facing | The bank is **opposite** the church. |
| **behind** / **in front of** | at the back of / at the front of | The car is **in front of** the house. |

**arrive at / arrive in.** We "arrive **at**" a small place or building (arrive **at** the airport, **at** school) and "arrive **in**" a city or country (arrive **in** Lagos, **in** Ghana). Never "arrive **to**".

**in vs into:**

- Wrong: He jumped in the river. (This can suggest he was already in the river, jumping up and down.)
- Correct: He jumped **into** the river. (movement from outside to inside)
- Note: "in" is accepted with movement in some informal English ("put it in the box"), but "into" is clearer and safer in an exam.

**between vs among.** The traditional school rule is: **between** for two, **among** for more than two.

- Share the money **between** the two brothers.
- Share the sweets **among** all the pupils.

Most modern grammars (and dictionaries such as the *Oxford English Dictionary*) say "between" can also be used for more than two when the items are seen as separate individuals with clear relationships: "a treaty **between** Nigeria, Ghana and Togo"; "the difference **between** the three answers". "Among" is used when the members are thought of as a mass or group. In a JAMB gap with a clear group of more than two ("all the pupils", "the villagers"), choose **among**. **Amongst** is an acceptable British variant of "among", slightly more formal or old-fashioned.

**beside vs besides:**

| Word | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| beside | next to | The bag is **beside** the chair. |
| besides | in addition to; apart from | **Besides** the bag, she lost her phone. |

### Prepositions of cause, means, manner and other relations

| Preposition | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **by** | the doer in the passive; means of transport; method | The book was written **by** Achebe. We went **by** train. He paid **by** cheque. |
| **with** | the instrument or tool used | He cut the rope **with** a knife. |
| **of** | belonging, material, content | the roof **of** the house; a cup **of** tea |
| **for** | purpose; on behalf of | This knife is **for** cutting bread. He did it **for** his mother. |
| **about** | topic | a book **about** Nigerian history |
| **because of / owing to / due to / on account of** | cause | The match was postponed **because of** the rain. |
| **in spite of / despite** | contrast | **Despite** the rain, the match went on. |
| **instead of** | in place of | Drink water **instead of** soft drinks. |
| **except / except for / apart from** | exclusion | Everybody came **except** Ade. |
| **like** | similar to (followed by a noun or pronoun) | She sings **like** a bird. |
| **as** | in the role of | He works **as** a driver. |

**by vs with (agent vs instrument):**

- The mango tree was cut down **by** the farmer (the person who did it) **with** a cutlass (the tool).

**despite vs in spite of:**

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Despite of the rain … | **Despite** the rain … |
| Inspite of the rain … | **In spite of** the rain … (three words) |
| In spite the rain … | **In spite of** the rain … |

**due to vs owing to.** Traditional grammars say **due to** should be used only as an **adjective** after the verb "be", linked to a noun: "The delay **was due to** the rain" (the delay = due to the rain). They say **owing to** (or because of) should introduce an adverbial phrase: "**Owing to** the rain, the match was delayed." Modern dictionaries and most usage guides now accept "due to" in both positions ("The match was delayed **due to** the rain"). If both appear in exam options and one fits the traditional rule, choose the traditional one.

**like vs as:**

- **like** + noun/pronoun (comparison): She dances **like** her mother.
- **as** + clause (formal): She dances **as** her mother did. (Informal English often uses "like" here — "like her mother did" — but formal British English prefers "as".)
- **as** + noun = in the role of: He spoke **as** a father (he is a father). / He spoke **like** a father (he is not one, but spoke in that way).

### Prepositions after verbs, nouns and adjectives

Many verbs, nouns and adjectives are followed by fixed prepositions. (Adjective + preposition combinations are listed in B4c; phrasal and prepositional verbs are in B4b.)

**Verb + preposition:**

| Verb | Preposition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| accuse | of | They accused him **of** theft. |
| apologise | to (person) for (thing) | He apologised **to** the teacher **for** his rudeness. |
| believe | in | I believe **in** hard work. |
| belong | to | This pen belongs **to** Kunle. |
| blame | for (thing); on (person) | They blamed him **for** the accident. / They blamed the accident **on** him. |
| congratulate | on | We congratulated her **on** her success. (not "for") |
| consist | of | The committee consists **of** five members. |
| depend / rely | on | Don't depend **on** others. |
| die | of (illness) / from (injury, cause) | He died **of** malaria. / He died **from** his wounds. (Usage varies; both are often accepted.) |
| insist | on | She insisted **on** paying. |
| listen | to | Listen **to** me. |
| prefer | (X) to (Y) | I prefer rice **to** beans. (not "than") |
| prevent | from | The rain prevented us **from** going out. |
| suffer | from | He suffers **from** asthma. |
| succeed | in | She succeeded **in** passing. |
| translate | into | Translate this **into** Igbo. |
| divide | into | Divide the class **into** groups. |
| wait | for | We waited **for** the bus. |

**No preposition needed** (common Nigerian errors, see also B4b):

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| discuss about the matter | **discuss** the matter |
| emphasise on the point | **emphasise** the point |
| comprise of six rooms | **comprise** six rooms / **consist of** six rooms |
| request for a transfer (verb) | **request** a transfer |
| demand for an apology (verb) | **demand** an apology |
| resemble to his father | **resemble** his father |
| enter into the room | **enter** the room |
| lack of money (verb) | **lack** money ("He lacks money"); but noun: "a **lack of** money" ✔ |
| attack on the man (verb) | **attack** the man; noun: "an **attack on** the man" ✔ |
| reach to Abuja | **reach** Abuja |

**Noun + preposition:**

| Noun | Preposition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| answer | to | the answer **to** question 3 |
| solution | to | a solution **to** the problem |
| key | to | the key **to** success |
| reason | for | the reason **for** his absence |
| demand / request / need | for | a demand **for** higher wages |
| increase / decrease / rise / fall | in (the thing); of (the amount) | an increase **in** prices; an increase **of** 10% |
| cause | of | the cause **of** the accident |
| example | of | an example **of** a noun |
| effect | on | the effect **of** drugs **on** the brain |
| advantage | of / over | the advantage **of** speed; an advantage **over** others |
| attitude | to / towards | her attitude **to** work |
| relationship | with (person); between (two things) | his relationship **with** his father |
| congratulations | on | Congratulations **on** your admission! |

### Ending a sentence with a preposition

Some older grammar books taught that a sentence should never end with a preposition. Modern grammars (for example, Swan and Fowler's later editions) say this "rule" is a myth, and ending with a preposition is normal, especially with phrasal verbs and questions:

- What are you looking **at**? (natural) vs At what are you looking? (very formal, awkward)
- This is the house I grew up **in**. (natural) vs This is the house in which I grew up. (formal, also correct)
- The meeting was called **off**. (phrasal verb — cannot be moved)

In very formal writing, "in which", "to whom" and similar forms are preferred. Both are correct; JAMB does not test this "rule" as an error.

### Do not add or repeat prepositions

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Where are you going to? (informal, acceptable in speech) | Where are you going? |
| the house in which I live in | the house **in which** I live / the house I live **in** |
| He sat in between the two of us. (informal) | He sat **between** the two of us. |
| Off the light. | **Switch off** the light. ("off" is not a verb in Standard English.) |

## Part 2: Conjunctions

### What is a conjunction?

A conjunction is a word that **joins** words, phrases or clauses.

- bread **and** butter (words)
- in the morning **or** in the evening (phrases)
- I wanted to come, **but** I was ill. (clauses)

There are three kinds: **coordinating**, **subordinating** and **correlative**.

### Coordinating conjunctions

Coordinating conjunctions join items of **equal grammatical rank** (two nouns, two phrases, two independent clauses). The seven are remembered as **FANBOYS**:

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F**or | reason (formal) | She was tired, **for** she had worked all day. |
| **A**nd | addition | Tunde **and** Bola are friends. |
| **N**or | negative addition | He does not smoke, **nor** does he drink. (note inversion after "nor") |
| **B**ut | contrast | It was raining, **but** we went out. |
| **O**r | alternative; warning | Tea **or** coffee? Hurry up, **or** you will miss the bus. |
| **Y**et | contrast (= but, nevertheless) | He is rich, **yet** he is unhappy. |
| **S**o | result | It was late, **so** we went home. |

**Punctuation:** when a coordinating conjunction joins two **independent clauses**, a comma usually goes before it (especially if the clauses are long): "The bell rang, **and** the students rushed out." With short clauses, the comma is often left out.

**"Nor" causes inversion:** "He did not come, **nor did he** call." (not "nor he called")

**Starting a sentence with "And" or "But".** Some teachers say you must never start a sentence with "and" or "but". Modern grammars and usage guides (Swan, Fowler) say this is acceptable and common in good writing, though it should not be overdone. In formal exam writing, you can avoid the question by using "Moreover" or "However".

### Subordinating conjunctions

A subordinating conjunction introduces a **dependent (subordinate) clause** and joins it to a main clause. The subordinate clause cannot stand alone. (See B3a for clauses.)

| Meaning | Conjunctions | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time | when, while, whenever, before, after, until, till, since, as, as soon as, once | We left **after** the rain stopped. |
| Reason | because, since, as, now that | **As** it was late, we took a taxi. |
| Purpose | so that, in order that, lest | He saved money **so that** he could buy a laptop. |
| Result | so … that, such … that | It was **so** hot **that** we stayed indoors. |
| Condition | if, unless, provided (that), providing, as long as, on condition that, in case | You will pass **if** you study. |
| Contrast / concession | although, though, even though, even if, whereas, while, whilst | **Although** he was ill, he wrote the exam. |
| Place | where, wherever | Sit **wherever** you like. |
| Manner / comparison | as, as if, as though, than | She acted **as if** nothing had happened. He is taller **than** I am. |

### Common errors with subordinating conjunctions

**1. Do not use two conjunctions for one link.**

| Wrong (double conjunction) | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| **Although** he was rich, **but** he was unhappy. | **Although** he was rich, he was unhappy. / He was rich, **but** he was unhappy. |
| **Because** it rained, **so** we stayed at home. | **Because** it rained, we stayed at home. / It rained, **so** we stayed at home. |
| **Since** you are here, **therefore** help me. | **Since** you are here, help me. |

**Note:** "**Although** … **yet**" is found in some older and literary English ("Although he was poor, yet he was honest") and some Nigerian textbooks accept it, but modern grammars generally treat it as unnecessary or old-fashioned. "Although … but" is wrong in all standard grammars. In the exam, choose the option with only one linking word.

**2. Unless already means "if not".** Do not add another "not".

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Unless you don't study, you will fail. | **Unless** you study, you will fail. (= If you don't study, you will fail.) |

**3. Lest means "for fear that" / "in order that … not".** It is followed by "should + verb" or the base form (subjunctive). It is already negative.

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Walk carefully lest you will fall. | Walk carefully **lest** you **fall**. / … **lest** you **should fall**. |
| Study hard lest you don't fail. | Study hard **lest** you **fail**. |

**4. "The reason … is because".** Traditional grammar books call "The reason is because…" redundant, because "reason" and "because" both express cause. They recommend "The reason is **that** …" or simply "… because …". Many modern speakers say "the reason is because", and some dictionaries record it, but in a formal exam choose "that".

- Preferred: The reason he failed is **that** he did not study.
- Also fine: He failed **because** he did not study.

**5. "Like" as a conjunction.** In formal British English, "like" is a preposition, and "as" or "as if" is the conjunction before a clause.

- Formal: Do **as** I say. It looks **as if** it will rain.
- Informal (avoid in the exam): Do like I say. It looks like it will rain.

**6. "Whether" vs "if".** Both can introduce an indirect yes/no question: "I don't know **whether/if** he will come." Use **whether** (not "if"):

- after a preposition: We talked about **whether** we should go.
- before "or not" immediately: **Whether or not** you agree …
- before a to-infinitive: I can't decide **whether** to go.
- at the start of a sentence as subject: **Whether** he comes is not important.

**7. "Since" and "as" can mean "because".** "**Since** you are ill, stay at home." Be careful when "since" could also mean time: "**Since** he left, things have changed" (time) vs "**Since** he left, we can talk freely" (reason).

**8. "So that" (purpose) vs "so" (result).**

- Purpose: He woke early **so that** he could catch the first bus.
- Result: He woke early, **so** he caught the first bus.

### Correlative conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions come in **pairs** and join items of equal rank.

| Pair | Example |
| --- | --- |
| both … and | **Both** Ada **and** Ngozi passed. |
| either … or | You can have **either** rice **or** yam. |
| neither … nor | **Neither** the driver **nor** the passengers were hurt. |
| not only … but (also) | She is **not only** clever **but also** hard-working. |
| whether … or | I'll go **whether** it rains **or** not. |
| as … as | He is **as** tall **as** his father. |
| so … as (negative comparison, formal) | He is not **so** tall **as** his father. (Also "not **as** tall **as**") |
| no sooner … than | **No sooner** had he left **than** it rained. |
| hardly / scarcely … when | **Hardly** had he left **when** it rained. |
| rather … than | I would **rather** walk **than** wait. |

**1. Use the right partner.**

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| neither … or | **neither … nor** |
| either … nor | **either … or** |
| both … as well as | **both … and** |
| not only … but | **not only … but (also)** — "also" is often included; some grammars allow "not only … but" alone, as in "not only clever but hard-working" |
| no sooner … when | **no sooner … than** |

**2. Keep the structure parallel.** The words after each part of the pair should be the same kind of grammar.

| Wrong (not parallel) | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| She **not only** plays the piano **but also** the guitar. | She plays **not only** the piano **but also** the guitar. |
| He is **either** in his room **or** he has gone out. | **Either** he is in his room **or** he has gone out. |
| I like **both** swimming **and** to read. | I like **both** swimming **and** reading. |

**3. Concord with "either … or" and "neither … nor": the proximity rule.** When two subjects are joined by "either … or", "neither … nor" or "not only … but also", the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject.

- **Neither** the principal **nor** the teachers **were** present. (teachers = plural, nearer)
- **Neither** the teachers **nor** the principal **was** present. (principal = singular, nearer)
- **Either** you **or** I **am** going. (I is nearer)

**4. "Both … and" takes a plural verb:** **Both** the boy **and** his sister **are** here.

See B5b for full concord rules.

## Part 3: Determiners

### What is a determiner?

A determiner is a word that comes **before a noun** (and before any adjectives) to show **which** or **how much/many**. It "determines" or limits the noun.

- **the** red car, **a** small house, **this** book, **my** friend, **some** water, **every** student, **three** goats, **which** road?

| Type | Examples | Traditional (adjective) name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Articles** | a, an, the | articles (some older books simply class them as adjectives) |
| **Demonstratives** | this, that, these, those | demonstrative adjectives |
| **Possessives** | my, your, his, her, its, our, their, whose | possessive adjectives |
| **Quantifiers** | some, any, no, much, many, few, a few, little, a little, several, enough, all, both, half, each, every, either, neither, more, most, less, fewer | quantitative / distributive adjectives |
| **Numerals** | one, two, first, second | numeral adjectives |
| **Interrogatives** | which, what, whose | interrogative adjectives |

**Determiner vs pronoun.** Many of these words can also be pronouns when they stand alone:

- **This** book is mine. (determiner) / **This** is mine. (pronoun)
- **Some** students came. (determiner) / **Some** came. (pronoun)
- **My** bag (determiner) / The bag is **mine** (pronoun — note the different form)

### The articles: a, an, the

English has two kinds of article:

- **Indefinite article: a / an** — used with **singular countable** nouns when the thing is not specific or is mentioned for the first time.
- **Definite article: the** — used when both speaker and listener know which thing is meant.

### A or an? Follow the sound, not the letter

Use **a** before a consonant **sound**; use **an** before a vowel **sound**.

| Word | First sound | Article | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| apple | /æ/ vowel | **an** apple | |
| hour | /aʊ/ vowel (h is silent) | **an** hour | silent h |
| honest | /ɒ/ vowel | **an** honest man | silent h |
| heir | /eə/ vowel | **an** heir | silent h |
| honour | /ɒ/ vowel | **an** honour | silent h |
| house | /h/ consonant | **a** house | h is pronounced |
| university | /j/ consonant ("you") | **a** university | starts with a "y" sound |
| uniform | /j/ consonant | **a** uniform | |
| European | /j/ consonant | **a** European | |
| one | /w/ consonant ("won") | **a** one-naira coin | |
| umbrella | /ʌ/ vowel | **an** umbrella | |
| MBA | /e/ vowel ("em") | **an** MBA | letter M is said "em" |
| NYSC | /e/ vowel ("en") | **an** NYSC member | letter N is said "en" |
| SSCE | /e/ vowel ("es") | **an** SSCE certificate | letter S is said "es" |
| NATO | /n/ consonant (said as a word, "nay-toe") | **a** NATO member | an acronym said as a word |
| UTME | /j/ consonant ("you") | **a** UTME candidate | letter U is said "you" |
| X-ray | /e/ vowel ("eks") | **an** X-ray | |

**Note on "historic", "hotel" and "habitual":** older British usage wrote "**an** historic event" and "**an** hotel", from a time when the "h" was weakly pronounced. Modern British English pronounces the "h" and uses "**a** historic event", "**a** hotel". Both may be seen in print, but **a** is the modern standard.

### Uses of a / an

- Mentioning something for the first time: I saw **a** snake in the garden.
- One of a group: She is **a** doctor. (Nigerian English sometimes drops this: "She is doctor" is wrong.)
- Jobs and roles: He wants to be **an** engineer.
- "Per" in rates: ₦500 **a** kilogram; 60 kilometres **an** hour; twice **a** week.
- In exclamations with singular countable nouns: What **a** beautiful day!
- Some quantity expressions: **a** few, **a** little, **a** lot of, **a** great deal of, **a** couple of, **a** dozen, **a** hundred.

### Uses of the

- Something already mentioned: I saw **a** snake. **The** snake was green.
- Something unique: **the** sun, **the** moon, **the** earth, **the** sky, **the** world, **the** President of Nigeria.
- Something both speakers know: Close **the** door. (the door of this room)
- Superlatives and ordinals: **the** best student, **the** first day.
- Rivers, seas, oceans, deserts and mountain ranges: **the** Niger, **the** Benue, **the** Atlantic Ocean, **the** Sahara, **the** Alps.
- Plural or "union/republic/kingdom" country names: **the** Netherlands, **the** Philippines, **the** United States, **the** United Kingdom, **the** Republic of Benin.
- Groups of people with adjectives: **the** rich, **the** poor, **the** blind (see B4c).
- Musical instruments (playing): She plays **the** piano / **the** guitar.
- Some institutions and organisations: **the** United Nations, **the** Senate, **the** Police.
- Newspapers: **The** Guardian, **The** Punch.
- Family names in plural: **the** Okonkwos (the Okonkwo family).
- "the + singular noun" for a whole class (formal): **The** cheetah is the fastest land animal.

### No article (zero article)

Do **not** use "the" or "a" with:

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Most countries, states, cities, towns, streets | Nigeria, Ghana, Lagos, Kano State, Ikorodu Road |
| Single lakes and mountains | Lake Chad, Mount Everest, Mount Kilimanjaro (but "**the** Obudu Plateau", "**the** Jos Plateau") |
| Continents | Africa, Europe, Asia |
| Languages and school subjects | She speaks Hausa. We study Biology. (but "**the** Hausa language") |
| Meals (in general) | We had breakfast at 7. Lunch is ready. (but "**the** lunch we had at the wedding was delicious") |
| Sports and games | They play football. He likes chess. |
| Plural and uncountable nouns used in a general sense | **Dogs** are loyal. **Water** is essential. **Honesty** pays. (not "The honesty pays") |
| Days, months, festivals | on Monday, in June, at Christmas, at Easter |
| Some institutions when used for their main purpose | go to school, go to church, go to bed, go to prison, go to university, be in hospital (British) |
| Transport after "by" | by car, by bus, by air |

**Institutions — main purpose vs the building:**

- She goes to **church** every Sunday. (to worship)
- She went to **the church** to fix the roof. (to the building, for another purpose)
- He is in **hospital**. (British: as a patient) / He is in **the hospital** (American usage; or British when visiting the building).

**British vs American:** British English says "in hospital", "at university", "go to university"; American English says "in the hospital", "in college". JAMB follows British English.

### Common article errors

| Wrong | Correct | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| She is lawyer. | She is **a** lawyer. | Jobs need a/an |
| He gave me an advice. | He gave me **some advice** / **a piece of** advice. | "Advice" is uncountable |
| The honesty is the best policy. | **Honesty** is the best policy. | General uncountable noun |
| I play the football. | I play **football**. | Sports take no article |
| She plays piano. | She plays **the** piano. | British usage with instruments (American often drops it) |
| The Lagos is a busy city. | **Lagos** is a busy city. | Cities take no article |
| Niger is the longest river in West Africa. | **The** Niger is the longest river in West Africa. | Rivers take "the" (Niger the country takes no article) |
| He is the best of all student. | He is the best of all **the students**. | Superlative + plural |
| What a nonsense! | What nonsense! | "Nonsense" is uncountable |

### Demonstratives: this, that, these, those

| | Near | Far |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Singular** | this | that |
| **Plural** | these | those |

- **This** bag is heavy. **These** bags are heavy.
- **That** house is old. **Those** houses are old.

Wrong: "This kind of books" → Correct: "**This kind of book**" or "**These kinds of books**". (Informal English often says "these kind of books", but it is not accepted in formal grammar.)

### Quantifiers with countable and uncountable nouns

| With **countable plural** nouns | With **uncountable** nouns | With both |
| --- | --- | --- |
| many, (a) few, fewer, several, a number of, both | much, (a) little, less, a great deal of, an amount of | some, any, no, all, more, most, enough, plenty of, a lot of, lots of |
| **many** students, **a few** chairs, **fewer** cars | **much** money, **a little** salt, **less** noise | **some** books / **some** water |

**few vs a few; little vs a little.** The difference is very important for interpretation.

| Word | Meaning | Feeling | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **a few** (countable) | some, a small number | positive: "enough" | **A few** students passed, so the school is happy. |
| **few** (countable) | not many, almost none | negative: "not enough" | **Few** students passed; the result was poor. |
| **a little** (uncountable) | some, a small amount | positive | There is **a little** rice left; we can eat. |
| **little** (uncountable) | not much, almost none | negative | There is **little** hope of finding survivors. |

Also: **quite a few** = a fairly large number ("Quite a few people came" = many people came).

**fewer vs less.** Traditional rule: **fewer** with countable plural nouns, **less** with uncountable nouns.

- **Fewer** people came this year. (not "less people")
- There is **less** traffic today.

Many speakers use "less" with plurals in informal English ("less people"), and some dictionaries note this, but in formal and exam English, follow the traditional rule. (Note the accepted exception: "less than" with amounts of time, money and distance treated as a single quantity: "**less than** ₦500", "**less than** ten kilometres".)

**number vs amount:**

- a large **number** of students (countable)
- a large **amount** of money (uncountable)

**much vs many:**

- How **many** chairs? How **much** furniture?
- Wrong: "many furnitures", "much people".

**some vs any:**

- **some** in positive sentences and offers/requests: I have **some** money. Would you like **some** tea?
- **any** in negatives and most questions: I don't have **any** money. Do you have **any** questions?
- **any** in positive sentences means "no matter which": Take **any** book you like.

**each vs every:**

- Both take a **singular** noun and a **singular** verb: **Each** student **has** a desk. **Every** candidate **was** searched.
- **each** = individually, one by one (for two or more); **every** = all together, as a group (for three or more).
- **each** can stand alone or be used with "of"; **every** cannot: **Each of** the boys / **Each** (pronoun). Wrong: "every of the boys" → "**every one of** the boys".

**either vs neither:**

- Both refer to **two** things and take a **singular** noun: **Either** road leads to the market. **Neither** answer is correct.
- **either** = one or the other; **neither** = not one and not the other.

**all vs both:**

- **both** = two things together: **Both** my parents are teachers.
- **all** = three or more, or a whole amount: **All** my friends came. **All** the water is gone.

**no vs not:**

- **no** + noun: There is **no** water. I have **no** idea.
- **not** + verb / adjective / a/any: There is**n't any** water. He is **not** a doctor.

**half:** "**half** the class" or "**half of** the class" (both correct); "**half an** hour" (British) / "**a half** hour" (American).

## Part 4: Interjections

### What is an interjection?

An interjection is a word or short phrase that expresses a **sudden feeling** or emotion: surprise, joy, pain, sorrow, disgust, warning or greeting. It is not grammatically connected to the rest of the sentence.

| Interjection | Feeling | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oh!** | surprise, realisation, pain | **Oh!** I forgot my keys. |
| **Ah!** | surprise, understanding, pleasure | **Ah,** now I understand. |
| **Alas!** | sorrow, regret (formal, literary) | **Alas!** The hero is dead. |
| **Hurray! / Hurrah! / Hooray!** | joy, triumph | **Hurray!** We won the match! |
| **Bravo!** | approval, praise | **Bravo!** That was a great performance. |
| **Ouch! / Ow!** | sudden pain | **Ouch!** I've cut my finger. |
| **Oops!** | small mistake | **Oops!** I spilt the tea. |
| **Wow!** | amazement, admiration | **Wow!** What a beautiful dress! |
| **Hey!** | to get attention | **Hey!** Come back here! |
| **Hello! / Hi!** | greeting | **Hello!** How are you? |
| **Ugh! / Yuck!** | disgust | **Ugh!** This milk has gone bad. |
| **Hush! / Shh!** | asking for silence | **Shh!** The baby is sleeping. |
| **Eh?** | asking for repetition | **Eh?** What did you say? |
| **Well,** | hesitation; starting a reply | **Well,** I'm not sure. |
| **Phew!** | relief | **Phew!** That was close. |
| **Good heavens! / Goodness!** | shock, surprise | **Good heavens!** Look at the time! |

### Punctuating interjections

- A strong interjection is followed by an **exclamation mark**: "**Hurray!** We passed!"
- A mild interjection is followed by a **comma**: "**Oh,** I see." "**Well,** let's begin."
- Some interjections can begin a longer exclamation: "**Oh,** what a lovely surprise!"

**O vs Oh.** "O" (capital, no punctuation directly after it) is an old or poetic form used when **addressing** someone or something directly: "**O** God, help us." "**O** Lord!" "Oh" is the ordinary interjection for feelings: "**Oh!** That hurts."

### Interjections and formal English

Nigerian English speakers use many interjections from Nigerian languages and Pidgin in everyday speech, such as "Haba!", "Chai!", "Kai!", "Ehen!" and "Ah-ah!". These are lively and meaningful in conversation, but they are **not** Standard British English and should not be used in formal writing or chosen in a JAMB option. Use the standard English equivalents (for example, "Oh dear!", "Goodness!", "Really?").

Interjections are rarely tested on their own in JAMB, but they can appear in comprehension passages (to show a character's mood), in mood and attitude questions (Section A objective iv), and in occasional word class questions.

## Worked examples: answering questions on these word classes

**Example 1.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*The meeting will take place ______ Monday morning.*

- A. in
- B. at
- C. on
- D. by

**How to think:** "the morning" alone takes "in", but a particular day's morning takes "on". **Answer: C.**

**Note:** the common Nigerian phrase "The meeting will **hold** on Monday" uses "hold" intransitively. Standard British English says "The meeting will **be held**" or "will **take place**". JAMB may test this.

**Example 2.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*______ the heavy rain, the match continued.*

- A. Despite of
- B. In spite
- C. Despite
- D. Inspite of

**How to think:** "despite" takes no "of"; "in spite of" needs all three words, written separately. **Answer: C.**

**Example 3.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*Neither the captain nor the players ______ happy with the referee.*

- A. was
- B. were
- C. is
- D. has been

**How to think:** with "neither … nor", the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "the players" (plural). "Is" and "has been" are singular, and the sentence is in the past. **Answer: B.**

**Example 4 (interpretation).** *Few candidates answered the last question.* This means

- A. some candidates answered it, which was satisfactory
- B. hardly any candidates answered it
- C. no candidate answered it
- D. all the candidates answered it

**How to think:** "few" (without "a") is negative: not many, almost none. **Answer: B.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Preposition | A word showing the relationship of a noun or pronoun to another word, e.g. in, on, at |
| Prepositional phrase | A preposition plus its object: on the table |
| Complex preposition | A group of words acting as one preposition: in spite of, because of |
| Conjunction | A word that joins words, phrases or clauses |
| Coordinating conjunction | Joins items of equal rank: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (FANBOYS) |
| Subordinating conjunction | Introduces a dependent clause: because, although, if, when |
| Correlative conjunctions | Conjunctions used in pairs: either … or, neither … nor, not only … but also |
| Determiner | A word before a noun that shows which or how much: a, the, this, my, some |
| Indefinite article | a / an |
| Definite article | the |
| Zero article | Using no article, e.g. with general plural or uncountable nouns |
| Quantifier | A determiner showing quantity: many, much, few, little, some, any |
| Interjection | A word expressing sudden feeling: Oh! Alas! Hurray! |
| Proximity rule | With either/or and neither/nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Time:** **at** 6 o'clock / night / the weekend; **on** Monday / 1st October / Monday morning; **in** June / 2025 / the morning.
- **No preposition** before next, last, this, every, today, tomorrow.
- **for** a period, **since** a point in time. Never "since two weeks".
- **despite** (no "of"); **in spite of** (three words).
- **between you and me**, never "between you and I".
- **on** the bus / train / plane, **in** a car / taxi, **on** foot, **by** car.
- **prefer X to Y** (not "than"); **congratulate on** (not "for").
- **discuss, emphasise, comprise, resemble, enter (a room), request, demand (verbs)** take no preposition.
- **One conjunction only:** not "Although … but" or "Because … so".
- **Unless** and **lest** are already negative; "lest" takes "should + verb" or the base form.
- **Correlatives:** neither … **nor**, either … **or**, both … **and**, not only … **but also**, no sooner … **than**. Keep the parts parallel.
- **A / an by sound:** an hour, an honest man, a university, a European, an MBA, a one-way street.
- **few** (negative) vs **a few** (positive); **little** (negative) vs **a little** (positive).
- **fewer** with countables, **less** with uncountables; **many** with countables, **much** with uncountables.
- **each / every / either / neither** take a singular noun and a singular verb.
- **The** with rivers and oceans (the Niger), plural or union country names (the United States); **no article** with most countries, cities, single lakes and mountains.

## Quick summary

1. Prepositions show relationships of time, place, direction, cause and means; at/on/in follow the "point / surface or day / enclosed space or longer period" pattern.
2. Many verbs, nouns and adjectives take fixed prepositions, and some Nigerian English verbs add prepositions that Standard English does not use (discuss about, comprise of).
3. Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) join equal items; subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent clauses; correlative conjunctions come in fixed pairs and need parallel structure.
4. Use only one conjunction per link, and remember that "unless" and "lest" are already negative.
5. Determiners (articles, demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers, numerals) come before a noun; traditional grammars call them adjectives.
6. "A" or "an" depends on the first sound, not the first letter; "the", "a" or no article depends on whether the noun is specific, unique, general or a proper name.
7. "Few" and "little" are negative, "a few" and "a little" are positive; "fewer/many" go with countables and "less/much" with uncountables.
8. Interjections express sudden feelings and are followed by an exclamation mark or a comma.

## Practice questions

**1. Choose the option that best completes the gap: The prize-giving ceremony will take place ______ Friday morning.**

- A. in
- B. at
- C. on
- D. by

**Answer: C.** A particular day, including "Friday morning", takes "on". "In the morning" is used only when no day is named.

**2. Choose the option that best completes the gap: The headmaster shared the textbooks ______ all the pupils in the class.**

- A. between
- B. among
- C. amid
- D. within

**Answer: B.** "Among" is used for a group of more than two seen together, such as "all the pupils".

**3. Choose the option that best completes the gap: ______ the heavy downpour, the traders opened their shops.**

- A. Despite of
- B. In spite
- C. Despite
- D. Inspite of

**Answer: C.** "Despite" takes no "of". "In spite of" must be written as three words.

**4. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Neither the principal nor the teachers ______ aware of the change.**

- A. was
- B. were
- C. is
- D. has been

**Answer: B.** With "neither … nor", the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "the teachers" (plural).

**5. Which of the following is correct?**

- A. an university
- B. a hour
- C. an honest man
- D. an one-way street

**Answer: C.** "Honest" begins with a vowel sound (silent h), so it takes "an". "University" and "one" begin with consonant sounds (/j/ and /w/), and "hour" begins with a vowel sound.

**6. Choose the option that best completes the gap: You will not pass ______ you study hard.**

- A. unless
- B. if
- C. except
- D. without

**Answer: A.** "Unless" means "if … not": you will not pass if you do not study hard. "If" gives the opposite meaning.

**7. Choose the option that best completes the gap: There is ______ hope of finding the missing fishermen, so the search has been called off.**

- A. a little
- B. little
- C. few
- D. a few

**Answer: B.** "Little" (without "a") is negative, meaning "almost none", which explains why the search was called off. "Few" and "a few" are for countable nouns.

**8. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Most Nigerians prefer rice ______ any other food.**

- A. than
- B. to
- C. over
- D. from

**Answer: B.** The pattern is "prefer X to Y". "Prefer … than" is a common error.

**9. Choose the option that best completes the gap: "______! I have cut my finger," Emeka shouted.**

- A. Hurray
- B. Ouch
- C. Bravo
- D. Wow

**Answer: B.** "Ouch" expresses sudden pain. "Hurray" shows joy, "Bravo" shows approval and "Wow" shows amazement.

**10. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Hurry up, ______ you will miss the bus to Ibadan.**

- A. or
- B. lest
- C. unless
- D. so

**Answer: A.** "Or" after a command gives a warning ("otherwise"). "Lest" would need "lest you miss" (no "will"), "unless" gives the wrong meaning, and "so" shows a result, not a warning.
