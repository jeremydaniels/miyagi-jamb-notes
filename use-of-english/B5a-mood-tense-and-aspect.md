## Tense and aspect: what is the difference?

### Tense

**Tense** is a change in the **form of the verb itself** to show **time**.

English verbs change their form for only **two** times:

| Tense | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Present** | base form or base + -s | I **walk**. She **walks**. |
| **Past** | -ed or an irregular past form | I **walked**. She **wrote**. |

There is no single-word future form of an English verb (unlike, for example, French or Latin). The future is shown by other means: **will/shall + verb**, **be going to + verb**, the present progressive, and the simple present.

### Aspect

**Aspect** shows **how** an action is viewed in time: whether it is complete, ongoing, or connected to a later time. English has two aspects, which can combine:

| Aspect | Form | What it shows | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Progressive (continuous)** | be + -ing | The action is in progress, ongoing, temporary or incomplete | She **is reading**. |
| **Perfect** | have + past participle | The action is completed before a reference time, or connects the past to a later time | She **has read** the book. |
| **Perfect progressive** | have + been + -ing | An ongoing action continuing up to a reference time | She **has been reading** for two hours. |

When neither aspect is used, the form is called **simple**: She **reads**. She **read**.

### How many tenses does English have? (Textbook variation)

You will see different answers in different books, and both are useful:

- **Many linguistic grammars** (for example, Quirk and colleagues, Huddleston and Pullum, and Swan) say English has **two tenses** (present and past), which combine with **two aspects** (progressive and perfect) and with modal verbs such as will. In this view "will go" is not a "future tense" but a modal + verb.
- **Many school textbooks** (including several used in Nigeria) teach **twelve tenses**: present, past and future, each in simple, continuous, perfect and perfect continuous forms. Some books go further and add "future in the past" forms, making sixteen.

These are two ways of describing the **same forms**. The table below shows all twelve combinations with both names. For JAMB, the labels are less important than choosing the right form. If an exam question uses the term "future tense" or "present perfect tense", it means the forms in this table.

| Time | Simple | Progressive (continuous) | Perfect | Perfect progressive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Present** | I **cook** | I **am cooking** | I **have cooked** | I **have been cooking** |
| **Past** | I **cooked** | I **was cooking** | I **had cooked** | I **had been cooking** |
| **Future** (will + …) | I **will cook** | I **will be cooking** | I **will have cooked** | I **will have been cooking** |

## The present forms

### Simple present

**Form:** base form; add **-s/-es** for he, she, it and singular nouns.

- I/you/we/they **work**. He/she/it **works**.
- Negative: She **does not (doesn't) work**. Question: **Does** she **work**?

**Uses:**

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Habits and repeated actions | I **go** to church every Sunday. She **drinks** pap every morning. |
| General truths and facts | Water **boils** at 100 °C at sea level. The sun **rises** in the east. |
| Permanent situations | My uncle **lives** in Abeokuta. |
| Timetables and fixed schedules (future meaning) | The bus **leaves** at 6 a.m. tomorrow. The exam **starts** on Monday. |
| After time and condition words (when, if, as soon as, before, after, until, unless) when talking about the future | **When** the visitors **arrive**, we will serve them. (not "will arrive") |
| Instructions and demonstrations | First you **wash** the rice, then you **add** the water. |
| Commentaries and headlines | Osimhen **shoots** … and **scores**! "President **visits** flood victims" |
| Stative verbs (states, not actions) | I **know** the answer. This bag **belongs** to me. |

**The future-in-time-clause rule** is a favourite JAMB trap. After **when, if, as soon as, before, after, until, unless, once, by the time** (when the meaning is future), use the **present**, not "will":

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I will call you when I will get home. | I will call you when I **get** home. |
| If it will rain, the match will be cancelled. | If it **rains**, the match will be cancelled. |
| Wait here until I will come back. | Wait here until I **come** back. |
| As soon as he will finish, we will leave. | As soon as he **finishes** (or **has finished**), we will leave. |

**Note:** "will" after "if" is correct when it means **willingness**, not future: "**If you will** wait a moment, I'll call the manager" (= if you are willing to wait). This is a special polite use.

### Present progressive (present continuous)

**Form:** am/is/are + -ing.

- She **is cooking**. They **are not (aren't) listening**. **Are** you **coming**?

**Uses:**

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Actions happening now | Look! The children **are dancing**. |
| Temporary situations around now | I **am staying** with my aunt this term. |
| Changing or developing situations | Prices **are rising** every week. |
| Fixed future arrangements | We **are travelling** to Enugu on Friday. |
| Annoying repeated habits (with always, constantly, forever) | He **is always borrowing** my pen! |

**Stative verbs are not normally used in the progressive** (see B4b):

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I am knowing him. | I **know** him. |
| She is having two children. | She **has** two children. |
| This book is belonging to me. | This book **belongs** to me. |
| I am understanding the lesson now. | I **understand** the lesson now. |
| We are seeing the mountain from here. | We **can see** the mountain from here. |

### Simple present vs present progressive

| Simple present | Present progressive |
| --- | --- |
| Permanent or habitual: He **teaches** English. (his job) | Temporary or now: He **is teaching** English this term. (normally he teaches French) |
| General truth: Rice **grows** in wet areas. | Happening now: Our rice **is growing** well this year. |
| Timetable future: The plane **lands** at 3 p.m. | Personal arrangement: I **am meeting** her at the airport. |

### Present perfect

**Form:** has/have + past participle.

- I **have finished**. She **has gone**. **Have** you **eaten**? He **hasn't arrived**.

**Main idea:** the present perfect connects the **past** to the **present**. The time is not finished, or the result matters now, or the exact time is not stated.

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| A past action with a result now | I **have lost** my ID card. (I don't have it now.) |
| Experience in life up to now (exact time not stated) | **Have** you ever **been** to Calabar? She **has visited** Ghana twice. |
| A situation that began in the past and continues now (with for/since) | They **have lived** in Kano **since** 2015. I **have known** her **for** ten years. |
| Very recent actions (with just) | The bus **has just left**. |
| With already and yet | I **have already paid**. **Has** the result **come out yet**? |
| In a period not yet finished (today, this week, this year) | I **have drunk** three cups of water **today**. |

**Do not use the present perfect with a finished past time** (yesterday, last week, in 2020, ago, when I was young). Use the simple past.

| Wrong (common error) | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I have seen him yesterday. | I **saw** him yesterday. |
| He has died last year. | He **died** last year. |
| They have arrived two days ago. | They **arrived** two days ago. |
| When have you come? | When **did** you **come**? |
| I have finished it on Monday. | I **finished** it on Monday. |

**British vs American:** in American English, the simple past is often used with just, already and yet ("Did you eat yet?", "I just finished"). In British English, the present perfect is normal: "**Have** you **eaten** yet?", "I'**ve just finished**." JAMB follows British English, so choose the present perfect with just/already/yet.

**been vs gone:**

- He **has gone** to Abuja. (He is in Abuja now or on the way; he has not come back.)
- He **has been** to Abuja. (He went and has come back; it is an experience.)

### Present perfect progressive (present perfect continuous)

**Form:** has/have + been + -ing.

- I **have been waiting** for two hours. She **has been studying** since morning.

**Uses:**

- An activity that started in the past and is **still continuing** (emphasis on duration): We **have been waiting** for the bus **for** forty minutes.
- An activity that has **just stopped**, with visible results: You're sweating. **Have** you **been running**?

**Present perfect vs present perfect progressive:**

| Present perfect | Present perfect progressive |
| --- | --- |
| Focus on the **result** or **how many**: I **have written** three letters. | Focus on the **activity** and **how long**: I **have been writing** letters all morning. |
| Completed: She **has painted** the room. (It's finished.) | Maybe not complete: She **has been painting** the room. (She may not be finished.) |

**Common Nigerian error: present instead of present perfect with since/for**

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I am here since morning. | I **have been** here since morning. |
| I am waiting for you for one hour. | I **have been waiting** for you for one hour. |
| She is living here since 2010. | She **has lived** (or **has been living**) here since 2010. |
| How long are you in this school? | How long **have** you **been** in this school? |

### Since and for with the perfect

| Word | Used with | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **since** | a **point** in time (when it started) | since Monday, since 2019, since independence, since I was ten |
| **for** | a **period** (how long) | for two weeks, for ten years, for a long time |

Wrong: "since two weeks", "since a long time". Correct: "**for** two weeks", "**for** a long time".

**Tense in "since" clauses:** the main clause normally uses the present perfect; the "since" clause uses the simple past (pointing to the starting time).

- I **have known** him **since** we **were** in primary school.
- A lot **has changed since** you **left**.

## The past forms

### Simple past

**Form:** regular verbs add -ed; irregular verbs have special forms (see B4b).

- She **cooked**. They **went**. Negative: He **did not (didn't) go**. Question: **Did** you **go**?

**Uses:**

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| A completed action at a definite past time | Nigeria **became** independent in 1960. |
| A series of past actions | He **woke** up, **took** his bath and **left**. |
| Past habits | When I was young, I **walked** to school every day. |
| With "ago" | I **met** her three years **ago**. |
| In "unreal" situations (past subjunctive / past form for present meaning) | If I **had** money, I would buy a car. It's time we **left**. (See "Mood" below.) |

**Remember:** after "did" (questions and negatives), use the base form: "Did she **come**?" (not "Did she came?"). "He didn't **see** me." (not "didn't saw").

### Past progressive (past continuous)

**Form:** was/were + -ing.

- I **was reading**. They **were playing**.

**Uses:**

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| An action in progress at a past time | At 8 p.m. yesterday, I **was watching** the news. |
| A background action interrupted by a shorter one (simple past) | I **was walking** home **when** it **started** to rain. |
| Two actions in progress at the same time | While Mum **was cooking**, Dad **was reading**. |
| Setting a scene in a story | The sun **was shining** and the birds **were singing**. |

**When vs while:**

- **While** + progressive for the longer action: **While** I **was bathing**, the light **went** off.
- **When** + simple past for the shorter action: I **was bathing when** the light **went** off.

### Past perfect

**Form:** had + past participle.

- She **had left**. **Had** they **eaten**?

**Main idea:** the **past before the past**. It shows that one past action happened **before** another past action or time.

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| An earlier past action | When we **got** to the station, the train **had** already **left**. (The train left first.) |
| With "by the time" + past | **By the time** the police **arrived**, the thieves **had escaped**. |
| In reported speech (backshift of past/present perfect) | She said she **had finished** her work. |
| In third conditionals | If you **had studied**, you would have passed. |
| After wish / if only (past regrets) | I wish I **had listened** to my mother. |
| With "no sooner … than" and "hardly … when" | No sooner **had** we **sat** down than the light went off. |

**Compare:**

- When I **arrived**, the meeting **started**. (I arrived, then it started.)
- When I **arrived**, the meeting **had started**. (It started before I arrived; I was late.)

**Common Nigerian error: overusing the past perfect.** The past perfect is **not** a "more past" form for something that happened long ago. If there is no second, later past event, use the simple past.

| Wrong (overuse) | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| I had told you yesterday to come early. | I **told** you yesterday to come early. |
| My father had travelled to London in 1990. | My father **travelled** to London in 1990. |
| We had gone to the market this morning. | We **went** to the market this morning. |

### Past perfect progressive (past perfect continuous)

**Form:** had + been + -ing.

- They **had been waiting** for an hour when the bus finally **came**.
- His eyes were red because he **had been crying**.

**Use:** an activity that **continued up to** a point in the past, often showing duration or the cause of a past situation.

## The future forms

As explained above, English has no single future verb form. It uses several structures, each with a slightly different meaning.

| Form | Main use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **will + base form** | Predictions; decisions made at the moment of speaking; promises; offers; facts about the future | It **will** rain tomorrow. I'**ll** carry that for you. The exam **will** start at 9 a.m. |
| **shall + base form** | With I/we: offers and suggestions; formal future (traditional British) | **Shall** I open the window? We **shall** overcome. |
| **be going to + base form** | Plans already decided; predictions based on present evidence | I'**m going to** study medicine. Look at those clouds! It'**s going to** rain. |
| **Present progressive** | Fixed personal arrangements | We'**re travelling** to Jos on Saturday. |
| **Simple present** | Timetables and schedules | The train **leaves** at 6:30. |
| **be about to + base form** | The very near future | Hurry! The bus **is about to** leave. |
| **Future progressive** (will be + -ing) | An action in progress at a future time | This time tomorrow, I **will be writing** my JAMB exam. |
| **Future perfect** (will have + pp) | An action completed before a future time | By December, I **will have finished** secondary school. |
| **Future perfect progressive** (will have been + -ing) | Duration up to a future time | By June, she **will have been working** here for ten years. |

**will vs going to:**

| will | going to |
| --- | --- |
| Decision made **now**: "The phone is ringing." "I'**ll** get it." | Decision made **before**: "I'**m going to** visit Grandma this weekend." (already planned) |
| Prediction from opinion: I think Nigeria **will** win. | Prediction from evidence now: The baby is yawning; she'**s going to** sleep. |

**shall vs will:** see B4b. Modern British English mostly uses "will" for all persons; "shall" survives in offers/suggestions with I/we and in formal rules. Grammar books differ on how strictly to teach the old "I shall / you will" rule.

**Future in the past:** to talk about something that was in the future from a past viewpoint, use **would**, **was/were going to**, or **was/were to**.

- She said she **would** come. (Her words: "I will come.")
- I **was going to** call you, but I forgot.
- He **was to** become the first governor of the state. (it was destined/planned)

## Sequence of tenses

### The basic rule

When the **main clause** is in the **past**, the verb in the **subordinate clause** usually moves into a past form as well. This keeps the time consistent.

| Main clause (present) | Main clause (past) |
| --- | --- |
| He **says** that he **is** tired. | He **said** that he **was** tired. |
| I **think** she **will** come. | I **thought** she **would** come. |
| She **knows** that we **have** finished. | She **knew** that we **had** finished. |

### Reported (indirect) speech: backshift

When you report what someone said using a past reporting verb (said, told, asked), the tenses usually **shift back** one step.

| Direct speech | Reported speech |
| --- | --- |
| Simple present: "I **am** hungry." | Simple past: He said (that) he **was** hungry. |
| Present progressive: "I **am waiting**." | Past progressive: She said she **was waiting**. |
| Present perfect: "I **have finished**." | Past perfect: He said he **had finished**. |
| Simple past: "I **saw** him." | Past perfect: She said she **had seen** him. (simple past is also often kept: "she saw him") |
| will: "I **will** come." | would: He said he **would** come. |
| can: "I **can** swim." | could: She said she **could** swim. |
| may: "It **may** rain." | might: He said it **might** rain. |
| must: "You **must** go." | had to / must: She said I **had to** (or **must**) go. |
| shall (future): "I **shall** be late." | should / would: He said he **would** (or **should**) be late. |

**Other changes in reported speech:**

| Direct | Reported |
| --- | --- |
| now | then |
| today | that day |
| yesterday | the day before / the previous day |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| last week | the week before / the previous week |
| next week | the following week |
| ago | before / earlier |
| here | there |
| this / these | that / those |

Example: "I will see you **tomorrow**," she said. → She said she **would** see me **the next day**.

**Reported questions** use statement word order (no question form) and no question mark:

- "Where **do you live**?" he asked. → He asked me where **I lived**. (not "where did I live")
- "**Are you** coming?" she asked. → She asked **if / whether I was** coming.

**Reported commands** use "tell/ask/order + object + to-infinitive":

- "Sit down," the teacher said. → The teacher **told us to sit** down.
- "Don't shout," he said. → He **told me not to shout**.

### Exceptions: when backshift is not needed

1. **Universal truths and facts that are still true** may stay in the present:
   - The teacher said that the earth **goes** round the sun. (Also acceptable: "went", but the present is preferred for a permanent truth.)
2. **Situations still true now** may stay in the present:
   - She told me she **lives** in Port Harcourt. (She still does.)
3. **Reporting verb in the present** — no backshift:
   - He **says** he **is** tired.

**Note:** grammar books differ on how strictly backshift must be applied. Traditional school grammars teach strict backshift in all cases except universal truths. Modern grammars (such as Swan) say the tense can stay unchanged whenever the situation is still true. In JAMB, if the options include both, the backshifted form is usually expected **unless** the statement is a universal truth.

### Keeping tenses consistent in writing

Do not switch tenses without a reason. If a story starts in the past, keep it in the past.

- Wrong: Yesterday I **went** to the market and I **see** my old teacher, who **tells** me …
- Correct: Yesterday I **went** to the market and I **saw** my old teacher, who **told** me …

This is often tested in cloze passages: check the tense of the verbs around the gap.

## Mood

### What is mood?

**Mood** is the form of the verb that shows the **speaker's attitude**: whether they are stating a fact, giving a command, or talking about something wished for, demanded, imagined or not real.

Traditional grammars list **three** moods:

| Mood | Attitude | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Indicative** | Statements of fact and questions | The market **opens** at 7. **Is** it raining? |
| **Imperative** | Commands, requests, advice, instructions | **Close** the door. **Please sit** down. |
| **Subjunctive** | Wishes, demands, suggestions, hypothetical or unreal situations | If I **were** rich … I suggest that he **go**. Long **live** Nigeria! |

**Note on variation:** some books also list an **interrogative** mood (questions) and a **conditional** mood (would + verb). Others treat modal verbs (can, may, must …) as the main way English shows mood, and call this **modality**. JAMB textbooks mostly use the three traditional moods.

### The indicative mood

The indicative is the ordinary form used for facts, opinions and questions. Almost all sentences are indicative.

- The Niger **flows** into the Atlantic.
- **Did** you **pass** the exam?
- I **think** he **is** right.

### The imperative mood

The imperative gives commands, instructions, requests, warnings, invitations and advice. It uses the **base form** of the verb, usually with **no subject** (the subject "you" is understood).

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Command | **Stand** up! |
| Instruction | **Add** two cups of water. |
| Request (polite) | **Please pass** the salt. |
| Warning | **Be** careful! |
| Invitation | **Come** and eat with us. |
| Negative imperative | **Don't** touch that wire. **Do not** litter. |
| Emphatic imperative | **Do** sit down. (polite, insistent) |
| First-person imperative with "let" | **Let's** go. **Let us** pray. |
| With "you" for emphasis or contrast | **You** stay here; I'll go. |

**Punctuation:** an imperative ends with a full stop, or an exclamation mark if it is strong.

**Question tag after imperatives** (see B5c): "Close the door, **will you**?" "Let's go, **shall we**?"

### The subjunctive mood

The subjunctive is the most tested mood in JAMB. It is used for things that are **not facts**: wishes, demands, suggestions, and unreal or hypothetical situations. In modern English the subjunctive has only two clearly visible forms:

1. The **present subjunctive** (also called the **mandative subjunctive**): the **base form** for all persons (he **go**, she **be**, they **be**).
2. The **past subjunctive**: **were** for all persons (I **were**, he **were**).

In other cases, the "unreal" meaning is shown by using a **past tense form** for present or future meaning (for example, "It's time we **left**").

### 1. The present (mandative) subjunctive: demands, suggestions and necessity

After verbs, nouns and adjectives of **demanding, suggesting, insisting, recommending, requiring or necessity**, followed by **that**, the verb in the that-clause is in the **base form**, even for he/she/it. There is **no -s**.

**Trigger words:**

- Verbs: suggest, recommend, insist, demand, request, require, propose, order, urge, ask, move (in meetings), command, advise
- Adjectives: essential, important, necessary, vital, imperative, crucial, urgent, compulsory
- Nouns: suggestion, recommendation, demand, requirement, proposal, insistence

| Sentence | Note |
| --- | --- |
| The doctor suggested that she **stay** in bed. | not "stays", not "stayed" |
| I insist that he **apologise**. | not "apologises" |
| The court ordered that the suspect **be** released. | "be" for all persons |
| It is essential that every candidate **be** present by 8 a.m. | not "is" |
| The principal demanded that the money **be** returned. | passive: be + past participle |
| She proposed that the meeting **be** postponed. | |
| It is important that he **not be** late. | negative: **not** + base form (no "does") |

**British vs American usage (important variation):**

- **American English** uses the mandative subjunctive (base form) very regularly: "I suggest that he **go**."
- **British English** commonly uses **should + base form** instead: "I suggest that he **should go**." This is fully correct.
- **Informal British English** also uses the ordinary indicative: "I suggest that he **goes**." Some British grammars accept this; traditional and exam grammars do not.

For JAMB: the **base form** ("go", "be", "stay") and **should + base form** ("should go") are both correct. If both appear among the options, the question is badly set, so JAMB normally includes only one of them. Avoid the indicative (-s) and past forms in these clauses.

### 2. The past subjunctive "were": unreal present situations

"**Were**" is used for all persons (I, he, she, it) to talk about situations that are **imaginary, unlikely or contrary to fact** in the present.

| Structure | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **If** + were | If I **were** you, I would apologise. | I am not you (unreal). |
| | If he **were** here, he would help us. | He is not here. |
| **I wish** + were | I wish I **were** taller. | I am not tall. |
| **If only** + were | If only she **were** here! | She is not here. |
| **as if / as though** + were | He talks as if he **were** the principal. | He is not the principal. |
| **Suppose / supposing** + were | Suppose you **were** the president, what would you do? | Imaginary |
| Inverted conditional | **Were** I the governor, I would fix the roads. | = If I were the governor |

**was vs were:** in informal British English, "If I **was** you" and "I wish I **was**" are very common, and many modern grammars accept "was" in casual speech. In formal English, and in the exam, **were** is the correct choice. The fixed phrase "**If I were you**" is always "were".

**Careful:** not every "if … was" is subjunctive. When "if" introduces a **real** past possibility, "was" is correct:

- If he **was** at the party, I didn't see him. (He may have been there: a real possibility, indicative.)
- If he **were** at the party now, he would be dancing. (He is not there: unreal, subjunctive.)

### 3. Past form for present or future unreality

After some expressions, a **past tense form** has a present or future meaning. It shows that the situation is not real (yet) or is wished for.

| Expression | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **It is (high/about) time** + subject + past | It is time we **left**. It's high time the government **repaired** the roads. | We should leave now (we haven't yet). The roads should have been repaired already. |
| **I wish / If only** + past (present wish) | I wish I **had** a car. If only I **knew** the answer. | I don't have a car. I don't know the answer. |
| **I'd rather / I would rather** + subject + past | I'd rather you **stayed** at home. | I want you to stay. |
| **as if / as though** | She behaves as if she **owned** the place. | She doesn't own it. |
| **Suppose / supposing** | Suppose we **missed** the bus, what would we do? | Imaginary |

**"It is time" patterns:**

- It is time **to go**. (general: it is time for us to go)
- It is time **for us to go**.
- It is time **we went**. (past form; stronger: we should already be going)
- Wrong: "It is time we **go**." / "It is high time we **will go**."

**"I'd rather" patterns:**

- I'd rather **stay** at home. (same subject: base form)
- I'd rather **you stayed** at home. (different subject: past form)
- Wrong: "I'd rather you **stay** at home." (This is common in informal speech, especially American English, but the past form is standard in British grammar books.)

### 4. Wishes and regrets: wish + past perfect

To express a regret about the **past**, use **wish / if only + past perfect**.

- I wish I **had studied** harder. (I didn't study hard.)
- If only he **had listened** to his parents! (He didn't listen.)

To complain about a present situation and want someone to change, use **wish + would**:

- I wish you **would stop** making that noise. (I want you to stop.)
- Do not use "wish + would" for your own actions: "I wish I **would** be taller" is wrong → "I wish I **were** taller."

| Time of wish | Form | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present | wish + past (were) | I wish I **were** rich. | I am not rich. |
| Past | wish + past perfect | I wish I **had gone**. | I did not go. |
| Future / change | wish + would | I wish it **would** stop raining. | I want the rain to stop. |
| Ability | wish + could | I wish I **could** swim. | I can't swim. |

### 5. Fixed (formulaic) subjunctive expressions

Some old subjunctive forms survive in fixed expressions. Learn them as whole phrases.

- **Long live** the Federal Republic of Nigeria!
- **God bless** you. / **God save** the King.
- **Be that as it may**, … (= even if that is true)
- **Come what may**, … (= whatever happens)
- **So be it**.
- **Heaven forbid**!
- **Suffice it to say** that … (= it is enough to say that)
- **If need be**, … (= if it is necessary)
- **As it were** (= so to speak)

## Conditional sentences

A conditional sentence has two parts: the **if-clause** (the condition) and the **main clause** (the result). The tenses depend on how real or likely the condition is.

### The four main types

| Type | Meaning | If-clause | Main clause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Zero** | General truth; always true | simple present | simple present | If you **heat** ice, it **melts**. |
| **First** | Real or likely future | simple present | will + base | If it **rains**, we **will stay** at home. |
| **Second** | Unreal or unlikely present/future | simple past (were) | would + base | If I **had** money, I **would buy** a car. If I **were** you, I **would** apologise. |
| **Third** | Unreal past (did not happen) | past perfect | would have + past participle | If you **had studied**, you **would have passed**. |

**Interpreting conditionals (very important for sentence interpretation):**

| Sentence | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| If it rains, we will stay at home. | Rain is possible; the result is likely. |
| If I had money, I would buy a car. | I don't have money (now); I won't buy a car. |
| If I were the president, I would build more schools. | I am not the president. |
| If you had studied, you would have passed. | You did not study; you did not pass. |
| If he hadn't helped me, I would have drowned. | He helped me; I did not drown. |

### Mixed conditionals

Sometimes the condition and result are in different times.

- **Past condition → present result:** If I **had taken** the medicine, I **would be** well now. (I didn't take it; I'm not well now.)
- **Present condition → past result:** If she **were** more careful, she **wouldn't have lost** her phone. (She is not careful in general; she lost her phone.)

### Inverted conditionals (formal)

In formal English, "if" can be dropped and the auxiliary moved to the front.

| With "if" | Inverted (no "if") |
| --- | --- |
| If I had known, I would have come. | **Had I known**, I would have come. |
| If I were you, I would accept. | **Were I** you, I would accept. |
| If you should need help, call me. | **Should you need** help, call me. |

These are common in JAMB options. "**Had I known**" means "If I had known" — so the speaker did **not** know.

### Other condition words

| Word | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **unless** | if … not | **Unless** you hurry, you will miss the bus. (= If you don't hurry …) |
| **provided (that) / providing / as long as** | only if | You can go out **provided** you finish your homework. |
| **in case** | as a precaution against | Take an umbrella **in case** it rains. (not the same as "if") |
| **otherwise** | if not | Hurry; **otherwise**, you'll be late. |
| **suppose / supposing** | imagine that | **Supposing** you won the lottery, what would you do? |

### Common conditional errors

| Wrong | Correct | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| If I will see him, I will tell him. | If I **see** him, I will tell him. | No "will" in the if-clause (first conditional) |
| If I would have known, I would have come. | If I **had known**, I would have come. | No "would" in the if-clause (third conditional) |
| If I was you, I will go. | If I **were** you, I **would** go. | Second conditional: were + would |
| If he had come, he would see the governor. | If he had come, he **would have seen** the governor. | Third conditional needs "would have + pp" (unless it is a mixed conditional with a present result) |
| Unless you don't work hard, you will fail. | **Unless** you work hard, you will fail. | "Unless" already means "if not" |
| Had I have known … | **Had I known** … | Only one "had"/"have" |

## Worked examples: answering tense and mood questions in JAMB

**Example 1.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*By the time the firemen arrived, the building ______ down.*

- A. burnt
- B. has burnt
- C. had burnt
- D. burns

**How to think, step by step:**

1. Find the time words: "By the time … arrived" — a past reference point.
2. Ask: which action happened first? The building burnt down **before** the firemen arrived.
3. "Past before the past" = past perfect. **Answer: C.**

**Example 2.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*It is high time you ______ your attitude to work.*

- A. change
- B. changed
- C. will change
- D. have changed

**How to think:** "It is high time + subject" is followed by a past form with present meaning. **Answer: B.**

**Example 3.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*The committee recommended that the project ______ immediately.*

- A. is stopped
- B. was stopped
- C. be stopped
- D. will be stopped

**How to think:** "recommend that" is a mandative trigger. The subjunctive uses the base form, so in the passive: be + past participle. **Answer: C.** (In British English, "should be stopped" would also be correct, but it is not offered.)

**Example 4 (interpretation).** *Had the driver been careful, the accident would not have happened.* This means that

- A. the driver was careful, and there was no accident
- B. the driver was not careful, and there was an accident
- C. the driver was careful, but there was an accident
- D. the driver will be careful in future

**How to think:** "Had the driver been careful" = "If the driver had been careful" (third conditional, unreal past). So in reality the driver was **not** careful and the accident **did** happen. **Answer: B.**

**Example 5.** Choose the option that best completes the gap.
*The teacher told the class that light ______ faster than sound.*

- A. travelled
- B. travels
- C. had travelled
- D. was travelling

**How to think:** this is a scientific fact that is always true, so the present tense can stay after a past reporting verb. **Answer: B.** (Some strict traditional books would accept "travelled" too; since "travels" is the only form that is right under every grammar, it is the best answer.)

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Tense | The form of a verb that shows time; English has two by verb form (present and past) |
| Aspect | How an action is viewed in time: simple, progressive or perfect |
| Progressive (continuous) aspect | be + -ing; shows an action in progress |
| Perfect aspect | have + past participle; shows completion before a reference time |
| Perfect progressive | have + been + -ing; shows an ongoing action up to a reference time |
| Backshift | Moving tenses one step back in reported speech |
| Sequence of tenses | Keeping the tense of a subordinate clause in line with the main clause |
| Mood | The verb form that shows the speaker's attitude: indicative, imperative or subjunctive |
| Indicative mood | States facts and asks questions |
| Imperative mood | Gives commands, requests and instructions |
| Subjunctive mood | Expresses wishes, demands, suggestions and unreal situations |
| Mandative subjunctive | The base form after words like suggest, insist, essential that: "that he **go**" |
| Conditional sentence | A sentence with a condition (if-clause) and a result |
| Inverted conditional | A conditional without "if", with the auxiliary first: "Had I known …" |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **No "will" after when / if / as soon as / until / before / after** for future meaning: "When he **comes**, I will tell him."
- **Present perfect for unfinished time; simple past for finished time.** Never "I have seen him yesterday".
- **for + period, since + point:** "for two weeks", "since Monday". Never "since two weeks".
- **"I have been here since morning"**, not "I am here since morning".
- **Past perfect only for the earlier of two past actions.** Don't use "had" just because something was long ago.
- **By the time + past → past perfect** in the main clause; **by the time + present → future perfect**: "By the time you arrive, I **will have left**."
- **Stative verbs don't take -ing:** "I know", "I have a car", "It belongs to me".
- **If I were you** (not "was"); **I wish I were**; **as if he were**.
- **It is (high) time + past:** "It's time we **left**."
- **Suggest / insist / demand / recommend that + base form (or should + base):** "that he **go**", "that it **be** done".
- **Third conditional:** If + had + pp → would have + pp. Never "If I would have known".
- **Inversion:** "Had I known" = "If I had known" → I did not know.
- **Interpretation:** second and third conditionals, wishes and "it's time" all describe things that are NOT true.
- **Reported speech:** shift back the tense unless it is a universal truth or the reporting verb is present.
- **Been vs gone:** "has been to Abuja" (and come back); "has gone to Abuja" (still there).

## Quick summary

1. Tense is shown by the verb form itself (present and past); aspect shows how the action is viewed (simple, progressive, perfect, perfect progressive). Many school books call the twelve combinations "twelve tenses".
2. The present perfect links past and present and cannot be used with a finished past time such as "yesterday" or "ago"; use "for" with periods and "since" with starting points.
3. The past perfect shows the earlier of two past actions; do not overuse it for simple past events.
4. English shows the future with will/shall, be going to, the present progressive and the simple present; after time and condition words, use the present for future meaning.
5. In reported speech after a past reporting verb, tenses usually shift back, except for universal truths.
6. The three moods are indicative (facts and questions), imperative (commands) and subjunctive (wishes, demands and unreal situations: "If I were …", "It's time we left", "I suggest that he go").
7. Conditionals: zero (general truth), first (likely), second (unreal present), third (unreal past); second and third conditionals tell you the opposite of what is said is true.

## Practice questions

**1. Choose the option that best completes the gap: If I ______ you, I would apologise to the principal at once.**

- A. was
- B. am
- C. were
- D. had been

**Answer: C.** For an unreal present situation, formal English uses the past subjunctive "were" for all persons, especially in "If I were you".

**2. Choose the option that best completes the gap: It is high time the government ______ the roads in our town.**

- A. repairs
- B. repaired
- C. will repair
- D. has repaired

**Answer: B.** "It is (high) time + subject" is followed by a past form with present meaning: the roads should already be repaired.

**3. Choose the option that best completes the gap: The doctor recommended that the patient ______ in bed for a week.**

- A. stayed
- B. stay
- C. will stay
- D. has stayed

**Answer: B.** After "recommend that", the mandative subjunctive uses the base form for all persons. ("Should stay" would also be correct in British English.)

**4. Choose the option that best completes the gap: I ______ your brother at the stadium yesterday.**

- A. have seen
- B. saw
- C. had seen
- D. have been seeing

**Answer: B.** "Yesterday" is a finished past time, so the simple past is needed. The present perfect cannot be used with "yesterday".

**5. Choose the option that best completes the gap: By the time we got to the motor park, the last bus to Ilorin ______.**

- A. left
- B. has left
- C. had left
- D. leaves

**Answer: C.** The bus left before we got there, so the earlier past action takes the past perfect.

**6. Choose the option that best completes the gap: If you had told me about the problem, I ______ you.**

- A. would help
- B. will help
- C. would have helped
- D. had helped

**Answer: C.** This is a third conditional (unreal past): if + past perfect → would have + past participle.

**7. Choose the option that best completes the gap: My aunt ______ in Kaduna since 2015.**

- A. lives
- B. is living
- C. has lived
- D. lived

**Answer: C.** "Since 2015" describes a situation that began in the past and continues now, so the present perfect is needed.

**8. Choose the option that best completes the gap: When the visitors ______, we will serve them jollof rice.**

- A. will arrive
- B. arrive
- C. arrived
- D. would arrive

**Answer: B.** After "when" with future meaning, English uses the simple present, not "will".

**9. Choose the option that best completes the gap: Look at those dark clouds! It ______ rain.**

- A. is going to
- B. will have
- C. was going to
- D. has

**Answer: A.** "Be going to" is used for a prediction based on present evidence (the dark clouds).

**10. "Had I known that he was ill, I would have visited him." This means that the speaker**

- A. knew he was ill and visited him
- B. did not know he was ill and did not visit him
- C. knew he was ill but did not visit him
- D. visited him because he was ill

**Answer: B.** "Had I known" is an inverted third conditional (= If I had known), which describes an unreal past: the speaker did not know, so did not visit.
