# JAMB Use of English Notes: Number and Concord

## What JAMB expects

This topic is part of Section B of the JAMB Use of English syllabus: Lexis and Structure. It covers two items from content item (e): **number** and **agreement/concord**.

It serves these objectives:

- **Objective iv:** identify various grammatical patterns in use.
- **Objective v:** interpret information conveyed in sentences.

In the examination, concord questions come under "Basic Grammar" (10 questions in Section B). Concord is one of the most tested areas of basic grammar. Most questions are "fill in the gap" questions like this:

> Neither the principal nor the teachers ______ aware of the problem.
> A. was B. is C. were D. has been

By the end of these notes, you should be able to:

1. Explain what **number** means in English grammar and form the plurals of nouns correctly, including irregular and foreign plurals.
2. Tell countable nouns from uncountable nouns and use each correctly.
3. Explain what **concord** (agreement) means.
4. Apply the main rules of subject–verb concord, including the tricky ones: proximity, notional concord, collective nouns, *either…or*, *neither…nor*, *each*, *every*, *one of*, *the number / a number*, and *as well as / along with*.
5. Make pronouns agree with the nouns they stand for (pronoun–antecedent concord).
6. Spot the concord traps JAMB sets, including common Nigerian English errors.

## What is number?

**Number** is the grammatical way of showing whether we are talking about **one** thing or **more than one** thing.

English has two numbers:

| Number | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Singular | One | a boy, a goat, this book, she **is** |
| Plural | More than one | boys, goats, these books, they **are** |

Number affects several word classes, not only nouns:

| Word class | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun | student | students |
| Pronoun | he, she, it, this, that | they, these, those |
| Verb (present tense) | she **writes**, it **is**, he **has** | they **write**, they **are**, they **have** |
| Verb *be* (past tense) | he **was** | they **were** |
| Determiner | this, that, a/an, each, every | these, those, many, several |

**Note:** in the present tense, the **-s** ending works in opposite directions on nouns and verbs. A noun with **-s** is usually plural (*girls*), but a verb with **-s** is singular (*she sings*). This is why "The girls sings" is wrong and "The girls sing" is right.

## Forming plurals of nouns

### Regular plurals

Most nouns form their plural by adding **-s** or **-es**.

| Rule | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Most nouns: add **-s** | book → books, chair → chairs, yam → yams |
| Nouns ending in **-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -z**: add **-es** | bus → buses, class → classes, brush → brushes, church → churches, box → boxes, buzz → buzzes |
| Consonant + **y**: change y to **i** and add **-es** | lady → ladies, city → cities, baby → babies, story → stories |
| Vowel + **y**: just add **-s** | boy → boys, day → days, key → keys, monkey → monkeys, valley → valleys |
| Some nouns ending in **-f / -fe**: change to **-ves** | knife → knives, wife → wives, leaf → leaves, loaf → loaves, thief → thieves, shelf → shelves, half → halves, life → lives, wolf → wolves, calf → calves |
| Other nouns ending in **-f / -ff**: just add **-s** | roof → roofs, chief → chiefs, belief → beliefs, proof → proofs, cliff → cliffs, chef → chefs |
| Some nouns ending in **-o**: add **-es** | potato → potatoes, tomato → tomatoes, hero → heroes, echo → echoes, veto → vetoes, mosquito → mosquitoes |
| Other nouns ending in **-o**: add **-s** | radio → radios, photo → photos, piano → pianos, kilo → kilos, video → videos, studio → studios, zoo → zoos |

**Textbook variation:** a few words ending in **-f** or **-o** have two accepted plurals. Dictionaries accept both *scarfs/scarves*, *hoofs/hooves*, *dwarfs/dwarves*, *wharfs/wharves*, *zeros/zeroes*, *mangoes/mangos* and *volcanoes/volcanos*. JAMB normally avoids testing such words. If you must choose, the forms *scarves, hooves, wharves* and *mangoes* are the safest in British English.

**Watch out:** *roof* → *roofs* (not "rooves" in standard modern English) and *chief* → *chiefs* (never "chieves").

### Irregular plurals

Some very common nouns do not add -s at all. You must learn them by heart.

| Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- |
| man | men |
| woman | women |
| child | children |
| foot | feet |
| tooth | teeth |
| goose | geese |
| mouse | mice |
| louse | lice |
| ox | oxen |
| person | people (or *persons* in formal and legal English) |

**Compounds follow the same pattern:** *policeman → policemen*, *chairwoman → chairwomen*, *grandchild → grandchildren*.

### Nouns with the same form in singular and plural

Some nouns do not change at all. The verb tells you whether they are singular or plural.

| Noun | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sheep | One sheep **is** grazing. | Ten sheep **are** grazing. |
| deer | A deer **was** seen. | Many deer **were** seen. |
| fish | I caught one fish. | I caught five fish. |
| aircraft | The aircraft **has** landed. | Three aircraft **have** landed. |
| series | A new series **is** showing. | Two series **are** showing. |
| species | This species **is** rare. | These species **are** rare. |
| means | This means of transport **is** cheap. | All means **have** been tried. |
| crossroads | A crossroads **is** dangerous. | — |

**Note on *fish*:** the plural *fishes* exists. It is used mainly when talking about different **kinds** of fish (*the fishes of the Niger Delta*). For "many individual fish", use *fish*.

| bacterium | bacteria | Latin |

| memorandum | memoranda (or memorandums) | Latin |
| stratum | strata | Latin |

**Very common JAMB errors:**

- **Wrong:** *This criteria is unfair.* **Right:** *This criterion is unfair.* / *These criteria are unfair.* (But *One of the criteria is unfair* is correct: it means one criterion from a group.)
- **Wrong:** *This phenomena is strange.* **Right:** *This phenomenon is strange.*
- **Wrong:** *The bacteria is harmful.* **Right (singular):** *The bacterium is harmful.* **Right (plural):** *The bacteria are harmful.*
- **Wrong:** *the crisises* **Right:** *the crises*

### *Data* and *media*: a textbook disagreement

*Data* and *media* are plural in Latin, but modern English often treats them as singular.

| Word | Traditional (formal, scientific) | Common modern usage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| data | The data **are** reliable. | The data **is** reliable. (treated as an uncountable noun, like "information") |
| media | The media **are** powerful. | The media **is** powerful. (treated as a collective noun) |

Both patterns appear in modern dictionaries (Oxford, Cambridge). Older Nigerian grammar books, and most exam guides, prefer the **plural** use: *the data are*, *the media are*. In JAMB, if you are forced to choose, the plural verb with *data* and *media* is the safer answer, but well-set questions avoid relying on this point.

### Plurals of compound nouns

In most compound nouns, the **main** word takes the plural.

| Singular | Plural | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| son-in-law | **sons**-in-law | "son" is the main word |
| mother-in-law | **mothers**-in-law | "mother" is the main word |
| passer-by | **passers**-by | the people are passers |
| commander-in-chief | **commanders**-in-chief | the people are commanders |
| attorney general | attorneys general (also attorney generals) | "attorney" is the main word; both forms are found |
| court martial | courts martial (also court martials) | both forms are found |
| man-of-war | men-of-war | "man" (ship) is the main word |
| spoonful | spoonfuls | written as one word, so the ending takes -s |
| handful | handfuls | as above |
| cupful | cupfuls | as above |

**Watch out:** *two spoonfuls of sugar* is correct. "Two spoonsful" is old-fashioned and not used in modern exams.

**Compounds with *man* or *woman* as the first part:** both parts change: *manservant → menservants*, *woman doctor → women doctors*.

### Plurals of letters, numbers and abbreviations

- Abbreviations: *MP → MPs*, *CD → CDs*, *NGO → NGOs* (no apostrophe in modern British usage).
- Letters: *Mind your p's and q's*; *There are two s's in "dessert"* (the apostrophe is kept here for clarity).
- Numbers: *in the 1990s* (no apostrophe in modern British usage).

## Countable and uncountable nouns

Number in English depends heavily on whether a noun is **countable** or **uncountable**.

- A **countable noun** names something you can count: *one chair, two chairs*. It has a singular and a plural form.
- An **uncountable noun** (also called a **mass noun** or **non-count noun**) names something you cannot count as separate units: *water, rice, advice*. It has **no plural form** and takes a **singular verb**.

| Feature | Countable nouns | Uncountable nouns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plural form | Yes: books, mangoes | No: ~~furnitures~~, ~~informations~~ |
| Used with *a/an* | Yes: a book | No: ~~an advice~~ |
| Used with numbers | Yes: three books | No: ~~three luggages~~ |
| Verb | Singular or plural | Always singular |
| *many* or *much* | many books | much information |
| *few* or *little* | few books | little information |
| *fewer* or *less* | fewer books | less information |

### Uncountable nouns that Nigerian speakers often pluralise

These nouns are countable in some languages, and many Nigerian speakers use them with **-s** or with *a/an*. In Standard British English they are **uncountable**. JAMB tests them very often.

| Uncountable noun | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| advice | She gave me many advices. | She gave me **a lot of advice**. / She gave me **a piece of advice**. |
| information | I have some informations for you. | I have some **information** for you. |
| furniture | They bought new furnitures. | They bought new **furniture**. / They bought **some pieces of furniture**. |
| luggage | Our luggages are in the boot. | Our **luggage is** in the boot. |
| baggage | All baggages must be checked. | All **baggage** must be checked. |
| equipment | The laboratory equipments are new. | The laboratory **equipment is** new. |
| news | The news are bad. | The **news is** bad. |
| evidence | The evidences are clear. | The **evidence is** clear. |
| knowledge | He has many knowledges. | He has **a lot of knowledge**. |
| machinery | The machineries have arrived. | The **machinery has** arrived. |
| stationery | Buy your stationeries early. | Buy your **stationery** early. |
| jewellery | Her jewelleries were stolen. | Her **jewellery was** stolen. |
| research | He did many researches. | He did **a lot of research**. (In academic English, *researches* is occasionally found, but avoid it in exams.) |
| behaviour | Their behaviours are bad. | Their **behaviour is** bad. (*Behaviours* is used in psychology and biology for different types of behaviour, but not in ordinary English.) |
| homework | We have many homeworks. | We have **a lot of homework**. |
| traffic | There were many traffics. | There was **a lot of traffic**. / There was **heavy traffic**. |
| money | She has many monies. | She has **a lot of money**. (*Monies* exists as a legal/financial term, meaning sums of money, but avoid it in ordinary English.) |
| hair (on the head) | She plaited her hairs. | She plaited her **hair**. (*a hair / hairs* = individual strands: *There is a hair in my soup.*) |
| slang | — | **slang** has no plural |
| offspring | — | **offspring** is the same in singular and plural: *Her offspring are all married.* |

**To count an uncountable noun**, use a **unit word** (a partitive):

- a **piece** of advice / information / furniture / news / equipment / evidence
- an **item** of news / clothing / luggage
- a **loaf** of bread, a **bar** of soap, a **grain** of rice, a **drop** of water, a **sheet** of paper, a **cup** of tea, a **bag** of cement, a **tuber** of yam

Example: *My uncle gave me **two pieces of advice**.* (not "two advices")

### Nouns that can be countable or uncountable

Some nouns change meaning:

| Uncountable meaning | Countable meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *Paper* is made from wood. (material) | I bought a *paper* (newspaper). / She read two *papers* at the conference. |
| *Glass* breaks easily. (material) | Give me a *glass* of water. |
| *Iron* is a metal. | She bought an *iron* (for pressing clothes). |
| *Experience* is important. (knowledge from doing) | We had many strange *experiences*. (events) |
| *Work* is tiring. (labour) | the *works* of Chinua Achebe (books) |
| *Time* is money. | I have read it three *times*. |
| *Light* travels fast. | Switch on the *lights*. |

## Nouns that are always plural

Some nouns exist **only in the plural** and always take a **plural verb**.

### Things made of two parts

*trousers, scissors, pliers, spectacles, glasses (for the eyes), binoculars, shorts, jeans, pants, tongs, shears*

- **Right:** *My trousers **are** torn.* **Wrong:** *My trouser is torn.*
- **Right:** *The scissors **are** on the table.*
- To speak of one, use *a pair of*: *A pair of scissors **is** on the table.* (Here the verb agrees with *pair*, which is singular.)

**Nigerian English trap:** "trouser" (singular) is often used in Nigeria for one pair of trousers. In Standard British English, use *trousers* or *a pair of trousers*. (*Trouser* can appear as an adjective before a noun: *trouser pocket*, *trouser leg*.)

### Other plural-only nouns

*cattle, police, people (meaning "persons"), clothes, goods, premises, outskirts, surroundings, thanks, congratulations, belongings, earnings, riches, savings, proceeds, arms (weapons), remains, stairs, contents, valuables*

- *The cattle **are** grazing near the stream.* (never "cattles", never "the cattle is")
- *The police **have** arrested the suspect.* (never "the police has")
- *The people of Kano **are** hospitable.*
- *Her clothes **are** expensive.*
- *The goods **have** arrived.*
- *Congratulations **are** in order!*

**Note on *people*:** *people* is the plural of *person*. But *a people* (with *peoples* as its plural) means a nation or ethnic group: *The Igbo are a people with a rich culture.* / *the peoples of West Africa*.

## Nouns that end in -s but are singular

These look plural but take a **singular verb**.

| Group | Examples | Example sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *news* | news | The news **is** good. |
| Subjects and fields of study | mathematics, physics, economics, statistics (the subject), linguistics, politics, ethics, phonetics | Mathematics **is** my favourite subject. |
| Diseases | measles, mumps, rickets, diabetes | Measles **is** a dangerous childhood disease. |
| Games | draughts, billiards, darts, dominoes | Draughts **is** popular in many Nigerian motor parks. |
| Some place names | the United States, the Philippines (as a country), Wales | The United States **is** a large country. |
| Titles of books and films | *Things Fall Apart*, *Arrow of God* | *Things Fall Apart* **is** a famous novel. |

**Careful with subjects of study:** when *politics, statistics, economics* or *ethics* do **not** mean the subject of study, they can take a plural verb.

- *Statistics **is** a branch of mathematics.* (the subject: singular)
- *The statistics **show** that unemployment is rising.* (the figures: plural)
- *Politics **is** an interesting subject.* (the subject)
- *His politics **are** very conservative.* (his political views)

## What is concord?

**Concord** (also called **agreement**) is the rule that related words in a sentence must **match** in number and person. The most important type is **subject–verb concord**:

- A **singular subject** takes a **singular verb**.
- A **plural subject** takes a **plural verb**.

| Subject | Correct verb | Wrong verb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The boy | **plays** | ~~play~~ |
| The boys | **play** | ~~plays~~ |
| My sister | **is** | ~~are~~ |
| My sisters | **are** | ~~is~~ |
| She | **has** | ~~have~~ |
| They | **have** | ~~has~~ |

The other important type is **pronoun–antecedent concord**: a pronoun must agree with the noun it stands for.

- *The girl lost **her** bag.* (singular noun → singular pronoun)
- *The girls lost **their** bags.* (plural noun → plural pronoun)

### Step-by-step method for any concord question

1. **Find the verb** in the gap (or the verb being tested).
2. **Find the real subject** of that verb. Ask: *Who or what is doing the action?*
3. **Ignore the "noise"**: phrases that come between the subject and the verb (for example, *of the students*, *with his friends*, *as well as his wife*).
4. **Decide the number** of the real subject: singular or plural? Use the special rules below for tricky subjects.
5. **Choose the verb** that matches. Check tense too: the correct option must also fit the tense of the rest of the sentence.

#### Worked example

> The box of books ______ on the table.
> A. are B. were C. is D. have been

1. The verb is in the gap.
2. What is on the table? *The box* (the books are inside it).
3. Ignore *of books*.
4. *Box* is singular.
5. Singular verb: *is*. **Answer: C.**

## The rules of subject–verb concord

### Rule 1: Words between the subject and the verb do not change the subject

The verb agrees with the **head noun** of the subject, not with a noun inside a phrase that follows it.

- *The **quality** of the mangoes **is** poor.* (not "are")
- *The **price** of foodstuffs **has** risen.* (not "have")
- *One of the **boys** **is** absent.* → careful: see Rule 9.
- *The **leader** of the protesters **was** arrested.*
- *A **list** of successful candidates **has** been published.*
- *The **students** in the hostel **are** noisy.*

**JAMB trap:** the noun nearest to the gap is plural, so candidates choose a plural verb. Always find the head noun.

> The cost of the new school buildings ______ very high.
> A. were B. are C. is D. have been
>
> **Answer: C.** The subject is *cost*, which is singular.

### Rule 2: Subjects joined by *and* are usually plural

- *Ada and Chinedu **are** cousins.*
- *My father and my mother **have** travelled to Enugu.*
- *Rice and beans **are** cheap this week.* → careful: see the exception below.

**Exception 1: one idea or one dish.** When two nouns joined by *and* form **one unit**, the verb is singular.

- *Rice and beans **is** my favourite meal.* (one dish)
- *Bread and butter **is** a common breakfast.* (one meal)
- *Eba and egusi **is** what we are having for lunch.*
- *Fish and chips **is** popular in Britain.*
- *Slow and steady **wins** the race.* (a proverb: one idea)

**Exception 2: one person with two titles.** If one article (*the*) covers both nouns, they refer to one person.

- *The **principal and proprietor** of the school **has** arrived.* (one person who is both principal and proprietor)
- *The **principal and the proprietor** of the school **have** arrived.* (two people, because *the* is repeated)
- *My **friend and benefactor** **is** here.* (one person)

**Exception 3: *each* or *every* before nouns joined by *and*.**

- ***Every** boy and girl **is** expected to attend.*
- ***Each** teacher and student **has** a role to play.*

### Rule 3: *as well as, along with, together with, in addition to, with, including, besides, accompanied by, like*

These phrases do **not** join subjects the way *and* does. The verb agrees with the **first** subject, the one before the phrase.

- *The governor, **as well as** his aides, **is** coming.* (not "are")
- *The teacher, **together with** her students, **was** at the event.*
- *Musa, **along with** his brothers, **has** gone to the farm.*
- *The minister, **accompanied by** two commissioners, **visits** the site today.*
- *My father, **in addition to** my uncles, **supports** the idea.*
- *The students, **as well as** the teacher, **were** happy.* (first subject plural → plural verb)

**JAMB trap:** this is one of the most commonly tested rules.

> The captain, together with the players, ______ received by the president.
> A. were B. was C. have been D. are being
>
> **Answer: B.** The subject is *the captain* (singular). *Together with the players* is extra information.

### Rule 4: *Either…or* and *neither…nor*: the proximity rule

When two subjects are joined by *either…or*, *neither…nor*, *or*, *nor* or *not only…but also*, the verb agrees with the subject **nearer to it**. This is called the **rule of proximity**.

- *Either Bola or Tunde **is** responsible.* (both singular → singular)
- *Neither the boys nor the girls **were** punished.* (both plural → plural)
- *Neither the principal nor the **teachers were** aware.* (nearer subject plural → plural)
- *Neither the teachers nor the **principal was** aware.* (nearer subject singular → singular)
- *Either my brothers or **I am** going.* (nearer subject *I* → *am*)
- *Either you or **he has** to apologise.* (nearer subject *he* → *has*)
- *Not only the students but also **the teacher was** surprised.*
- *Not only the teacher but also **the students were** surprised.*

**Style tip:** when one subject is singular and the other plural, put the plural one **second**, so the plural verb sounds more natural: *Neither the principal nor the teachers were…*

**Note on *neither* and *either* on their own** (without *nor/or*): they are singular.

- ***Neither** of the boys **is** guilty.* (formal and exam-safe)
- ***Either** of the answers **is** acceptable.*

**Textbook variation:** in informal speech, many native speakers say "Neither of the boys *are* guilty." Grammar books such as Swan's *Practical English Usage* note that a plural verb is common in informal English. In exams, always choose the **singular** verb with *neither of* and *either of*.

### Rule 5: *Each, every, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, nobody, no one, nothing*

These words are **singular** and take a **singular verb**.

- ***Each** of the students **has** a textbook.* (not "have")
- ***Every** student **is** expected to wear a uniform.*
- ***Everybody** **was** happy with the result.*
- ***Everyone** **knows** the answer.*
- ***Nobody** **has** come yet.*
- ***Someone** **is** knocking at the door.*
- ***Nothing** **was** stolen.*
- ***Anyone** who **wants** to come **is** welcome.*

**Watch out:** *each* **after** a plural subject does not change the subject. The verb stays plural.

- *The boys **each have** a bicycle.* (The subject is *the boys*.)
- ***Each** of the boys **has** a bicycle.* (The subject is *each*.)

### Rule 6: *None*

*None* means "not one" or "not any". Grammar books disagree about its verb:

- *None of the students **is** absent.* (traditional, formal: *none* = not one)
- *None of the students **are** absent.* (very common in modern British English)

Both are accepted in modern dictionaries and grammars (for example, Swan's *Practical English Usage* and the *Cambridge Grammar of English*). Older Nigerian textbooks often insist on the singular. With an **uncountable** noun, *none* always takes a singular verb: *None of the money **was** found.*

**Exam advice:** if both *is* and *are* appear in the options after *none of* + plural noun, choose the singular, as most Nigerian exam guides prefer it. Good JAMB questions usually avoid testing this point.

### Rule 7: Collective nouns

A **collective noun** names a group: *team, family, committee, government, class, jury, audience, crowd, staff, board, army, congregation, panel, union, council, choir, club, public, majority, management*.

In **British English**, a collective noun can take a **singular** or a **plural** verb, depending on the meaning:

- **Singular verb** when the group acts **as one unit**:
  - *The committee **has** submitted its report.*
  - *The family **is** a basic unit of society.*
  - *The jury **has** reached its verdict.*
- **Plural verb** when we think of the **members as individuals** acting separately:
  - *The committee **are** divided over the issue.* (the members disagree with one another)
  - *The family **are** quarrelling among themselves.*
  - *The jury **were** unable to agree.*
  - *The staff **are** going home one by one.*

**Keep it consistent:** don't mix singular and plural in the same sentence.

- **Wrong:** *The team **has** lost **their** match.*
- **Right:** *The team **has** lost **its** match.* (one unit)
- **Right:** *The team **have** lost **their** match.* (British, members in mind)

| British English | American English |
| --- | --- |
| Allows a singular or plural verb: *The government **has/have** decided.* | Normally uses a singular verb: *The government **has** decided.* |
| *Arsenal **are** winning.* (sports teams are often plural) | *Arsenal **is** winning.* |

**Exam advice:** JAMB tests Standard British English. When a sentence clearly shows unity (*its*, *as a body*, *unanimously*), choose the singular. When it shows division or individual action (*among themselves*, *their opinions differ*, *going home separately*), choose the plural. When there is no clue, the **singular** is the safer choice, because it is correct in both British and American English.

**Collective nouns that are always plural:** *police, cattle, people, clergy, vermin, poultry (birds)*.

- *The police **are** investigating.* (never "is")
- *The clergy **were** present.*

### Rule 8: *The number* and *a number*

- ***The number of*** + plural noun → **singular** verb (because the subject is *number*, the total figure).
  - *The number of students **has** increased.*
  - *The number of accidents on the Lagos–Ibadan Expressway **is** worrying.*
- ***A number of*** + plural noun → **plural** verb (because *a number of* means *several* or *many*).
  - *A number of students **have** applied.*
  - *A number of accidents **were** reported.*

| Expression | Meaning | Verb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the number of | the total figure | singular: *The number of applicants **is** large.* |
| a number of | several, many | plural: *A number of applicants **are** waiting.* |

**The same pattern works with *majority*, *variety*, *lot*:**

- *A lot of people **were** there.* (plural: *people*)
- *A lot of rice **was** wasted.* (singular: *rice* is uncountable)
- *A variety of fruits **are** on sale.* (common, treated as "many")
- *The variety of fruits **is** amazing.* (the variety itself)
- *The majority of students **are** in favour.* (plural noun after *of*)
- *The majority **was** overwhelming.* (the size of the majority, as a single figure)

### Rule 9: *One of* + plural noun

***One of*** + plural noun takes a **singular** verb, because the subject is *one*.

- *One of the boys **is** missing.* (not "are")
- *One of my friends **lives** in Port Harcourt.*
- *One of the reasons for the failure **was** poor preparation.*

**The tricky one: *one of those who…***

In ***one of the … who/that …***, the relative pronoun *who/that* refers to the **plural noun**, so the verb in the relative clause is **plural** in traditional grammar.

- *He is one of the students **who have** passed.* (= Of the students who have passed, he is one.)
- *She is one of those women **who work** hard.*

**But** if *the only* comes before *one*, the verb is singular:

- *He is **the only one** of the students **who has** passed.* (Only one passed.)

**Textbook variation:** many native speakers use the singular ("one of those who **has**…") and some modern grammars accept both. Nigerian exam guides follow the traditional rule: plural in "one of … who", singular in "the only one of … who".

### Rule 10: Expressions of time, money, distance, weight and quantity

When an amount is thought of as **one total**, use a singular verb.

- *Five thousand naira **is** too much for this shirt.*
- *Ten kilometres **is** a long way to walk.*
- *Two years **is** a long time to wait.*
- *Fifty kilograms **is** the maximum weight allowed.*
- *Three months **is** too short a time to finish the project.* (one total period)

When the units are thought of **separately**, use a plural verb:

- *Ten naira notes **were** found on the floor.*
- *The last three years **have** been difficult.* (each year separately)

**Fractions and percentages** agree with the noun after *of*:

- *Half of the cake **has** been eaten.* (cake: singular)
- *Half of the students **have** left.* (students: plural)
- *Forty per cent of the land **is** fertile.*
- *Forty per cent of the farmers **are** women.*
- *Two-thirds of the class **is/are** present.* (collective noun: see Rule 7)

**Arithmetic:** both are found: *Two and two **is/are** four.* *Five times five **is** twenty-five.* The singular is more common in sums, and exams usually avoid this point.

### Rule 11: *There is / There are* and *Here is / Here are*

In sentences beginning with *there* or *here*, the real subject comes **after** the verb. Look ahead to find it.

- *There **is** a goat in the compound.*
- *There **are** many goats in the compound.*
- *There **was** a lot of noise.* (*noise* is uncountable)
- *There **were** a lot of people.*
- *Here **are** your books.*
- *Here **is** the money.*

**Nigerian English trap:** "There **is** many reasons…" is wrong. **Right:** *There **are** many reasons…*

When two nouns follow, formal English agrees with both: *There **are** a table and two chairs in the room.* In everyday speech, many people agree with the first noun (*There's a table and two chairs*). In exams, use the formal pattern.

### Rule 12: Inverted sentences

When the subject comes after the verb, find it first.

- *On the hill **stands** an old church.* (subject: *an old church*)
- *Among the guests **were** two senators.* (subject: *two senators*)
- *Under the mango tree **sit** the elders.* (subject: *the elders*)

### Rule 13: Relative clauses: *who, which, that*

The verb after *who/which/that* agrees with the noun that the relative pronoun refers to (its antecedent).

- *The **woman who sells** akara is my aunt.*
- *The **women who sell** akara are my aunts.*
- *This is the **book that has** the answers.*
- *These are the **books that have** the answers.*
- *It is **I who am** to blame.* (formal: agrees with *I*)

### Rule 14: Titles, names and clauses as subjects

- A title is singular: ***Things Fall Apart** **was** written by Chinua Achebe.*
- A company or organisation name: *Dangote Industries **is** a large company.* (British English sometimes uses a plural verb for companies and teams: *Shell **are** recruiting.* The singular is safer in exams.)
- A **clause** or **gerund** used as a subject is singular:
  - ***What he needs** **is** money.*
  - ***Swimming in the lagoon** **is** dangerous.*
  - ***Reading novels** **improves** your vocabulary.*
  - ***That he lied** **is** clear.*

**Note:** *What we need **are** more teachers* is also accepted when the complement is plural. The singular is the safer choice in exams.

### Rule 15: *Many a* + singular noun

***Many a*** is followed by a **singular noun** and a **singular verb**, even though the meaning is "many".

- *Many a student **has** failed this paper.* (not "have", and not "many a students")
- *Many a man **has** made that mistake.*

Compare: *Many students **have** failed this paper.*

### Rule 16: *More than one*

***More than one*** + singular noun → **singular** verb.

- *More than one student **was** punished.*

But ***more than*** + plural number → plural verb.

- *More than two students **were** punished.*

### Rule 17: *Pair*, *kind*, *type*, *sort*

- *A pair of shoes **is** on the mat.* (subject: *pair*)
- *Two pairs of shoes **are** on the mat.*
- *This **kind** of mango **is** sweet.* (singular)
- *These **kinds** of mango **are** sweet.* (plural)
- **Wrong:** *These kind of people…* **Right:** *This kind of person…* or *These kinds of people…* (Note: "these kind of" is common in informal speech, but it is not accepted in formal exams.)

### Rule 18: Concord of person

The verb must also agree with the subject in **person**.

| Person | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | I **am**, I **have**, I **go** | we **are**, we **have**, we **go** |
| Second | you **are**, you **have**, you **go** | you **are**, you **have**, you **go** |
| Third | he/she/it **is**, **has**, **goes** | they **are**, **have**, **go** |

**Note:** *you* always takes a plural-form verb, even when it refers to one person: *You **are** my friend* (not "you is"); *You **were** late* (not "you was").

### Summary table: tricky subjects

| Subject | Verb | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A and B | plural | Ada and Emeka **are** here. |
| A and B (one idea) | singular | Rice and stew **is** my favourite. |
| A as well as B | agrees with A | The man as well as his sons **is** here. |
| Either A or B / Neither A nor B | agrees with B (nearer) | Neither the man nor his sons **are** here. |
| Each / every / everyone | singular | Each of them **has** a book. |
| None of + plural noun | singular (formal) or plural | None of them **is/are** here. |
| Collective noun as unit | singular | The committee **has** decided. |
| Collective noun as individuals | plural (British) | The committee **are** divided. |
| The number of | singular | The number of cars **is** high. |
| A number of | plural | A number of cars **are** parked. |
| One of + plural | singular | One of the cars **is** red. |
| One of those who | plural (in the *who* clause) | He is one of those who **work** hard. |
| Many a + singular | singular | Many a student **has** tried. |
| Amount of money, time, distance | singular | Ten thousand naira **is** enough. |
| Uncountable noun | singular | The furniture **is** new. |

## Pronoun–antecedent concord

The **antecedent** is the noun (or pronoun) that a pronoun refers back to. A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in **number**, **person** and **gender**.

### Number

- *The **boy** washed **his** uniform.*
- *The **boys** washed **their** uniforms.*
- *Every **student** must bring **his or her** own textbook.*

### Person

- *If **one** works hard, **one** will succeed.* (formal British; also accepted: *If one works hard, **he or she** will succeed.*)
- **Wrong:** *If **one** works hard, **you** will succeed.* (mixing *one* and *you*)

### Gender

- *The **cock** flapped **its** wings.* (animals usually take *it*)
- *The **queen** waved to **her** subjects.*
- Ships and countries are sometimes called *she* in older or poetic English (*Nigeria and her people*), but *it* is more common in modern English (*Nigeria and its people*).

### Collective nouns

Keep the verb and pronoun consistent.

- *The **jury has** given **its** verdict.* (unit)
- *The **jury have** gone back to **their** homes.* (individuals)

### Indefinite pronouns (*everyone, somebody, each, nobody*)

Traditional grammar uses **he or she** (or just *he*, in older books). Modern English widely uses **singular *they***:

- *Everyone should bring **his or her** own pen.* (traditional, formal)
- *Everyone should bring **their** own pen.* (modern, accepted by Oxford, Cambridge and most modern grammars)
- *Somebody left **their** bag in the classroom.*

**Textbook variation:** older Nigerian textbooks mark *everyone… their* as wrong. Modern British usage accepts it, especially when the sex of the person is unknown. In an exam, if both *his or her* and *their* appear, choose ***his or her***, the form no grammar book rejects. Note that *everyone* still takes a **singular verb**: *Everyone **has** (not "have") brought their pen.*

### Avoid unclear antecedents

- **Unclear:** *Chika told Ngozi that **she** had passed.* (Who passed?)
- **Clear:** *Chika told Ngozi, "You have passed."* or *Chika said that Ngozi had passed.*

## Other types of concord

### Concord of tense (sequence of tenses)

Verbs in a sentence should agree in time. If the main verb is in the past, verbs in reported clauses usually move to the past.

- *He **said** that he **was** tired.* (not "he said that he is tired", unless it is still true now)
- *She **told** me she **would** come.* (not "will come")
- *If I **had** known, I **would have** helped.*

**Exception:** general truths may stay in the present: *The teacher **said** that the earth **goes** round the sun.*

(Tense is covered fully in B5a-mood-tense-and-aspect.md.)

### Concord of determiners and nouns

- *this/that* + singular: *this book, that goat*
- *these/those* + plural: *these books, those goats*
- *much* + uncountable: *much rice*; *many* + countable plural: *many bags*
- *less* + uncountable: *less money*; *fewer* + countable plural: *fewer students*

**Common errors:**

- **Wrong:** *Less students attended.* **Right:** *Fewer students attended.*
- **Wrong:** *This equipments are new.* **Right:** *This equipment is new.*
- **Wrong:** *Those kind of shoe.* **Right:** *That kind of shoe* / *Those kinds of shoe(s).*

**Textbook variation:** "less" with plural nouns ("less people") is common in informal English, but every exam guide marks it wrong. Use *fewer* with countable nouns. (Exception: *less than* is normal with amounts: *less than ten kilometres*, *less than 500 naira*.)

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Number | The grammatical category that shows one (singular) or more than one (plural) |
| Singular | Referring to one person or thing |
| Plural | Referring to more than one person or thing |
| Countable noun | A noun that can be counted and has a plural form, e.g. *chair, chairs* |
| Uncountable (mass) noun | A noun that cannot be counted and has no plural, e.g. *advice, furniture* |
| Irregular plural | A plural not formed with -s or -es, e.g. *child → children* |
| Foreign plural | A plural kept from Latin or Greek, e.g. *criterion → criteria* |
| Partitive (unit word) | A word used to count an uncountable noun, e.g. *a piece of advice* |
| Concord (agreement) | The matching of related words in number, person or tense |
| Subject–verb concord | A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb |
| Antecedent | The noun that a pronoun refers back to |
| Collective noun | A noun naming a group, e.g. *team, committee, family* |
| Proximity rule | With *either…or* / *neither…nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject |
| Notional concord | Agreement based on meaning rather than grammatical form, e.g. *The committee are divided* |
| Head noun | The main noun in a subject phrase, which controls the verb |

## Exam tips and common mistakes

- **Find the real subject first.** Cross out phrases like *of the students*, *as well as his wife*, *together with his friends*. Then decide.
- **Watch the "noise noun".** In *The cost of the buildings ___*, the plural *buildings* is next to the gap, but the subject is *cost*.
- ***And* = plural; *as well as / with / along with* = agree with the first subject.**
- ***Either…or* / *neither…nor*:** agree with the subject **nearer** to the verb.
- ***Each, every, everybody, everyone, nobody, one of, many a*** all take a **singular** verb.
- ***The number of* = singular; *a number of* = plural.**
- **Never pluralise** *advice, information, furniture, luggage, equipment, news, evidence, knowledge, stationery*.
- ***Police, cattle, people, clothes, trousers, scissors*** are plural.
- **Foreign singulars:** criterion, phenomenon, datum, medium, bacterium, stimulus, nucleus, crisis, analysis, thesis. Their plurals: criteria, phenomena, data, media, bacteria, stimuli, nuclei, crises, analyses, theses.
- **Check tense as well as number.** Sometimes two options are both singular, but only one fits the tense of the sentence (e.g. *yesterday* needs a past tense).
- **Be consistent with collective nouns:** *its* goes with a singular verb, *their* with a plural verb.
- **When grammar books disagree** (*none*, *data*, *everyone… their*), choose the traditional form: *none is*, *the data are*, *his or her*.

## Quick summary

1. **Number** shows singular (one) or plural (more than one). It affects nouns, pronouns, determiners and verbs.
2. Most nouns add **-s/-es** for the plural, but learn the irregular plurals (*men, children, feet, teeth, mice*) and foreign plurals (*criteria, phenomena, bacteria, crises, stimuli*).
3. **Uncountable nouns** such as *advice, information, furniture, luggage, equipment, news* have no plural and take a singular verb. Use *a piece of* to count them.
4. **Concord** means a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. Always find the real (head) subject first.
5. Subjects joined by ***and*** are plural, but *as well as, together with, along with* do not change the subject. With ***either…or / neither…nor***, the verb agrees with the nearer subject.
6. ***Each, every, everyone, one of, many a, the number of*** take singular verbs. ***A number of*** takes a plural verb.
7. In British English, **collective nouns** take a singular verb when the group acts as one, and a plural verb when the members act separately. Keep pronouns consistent.
8. Pronouns must agree with their antecedents. Where books differ (*none is/are*, *data is/are*, *everyone… their*), the traditional form is safest in exams.

## Practice questions

**1. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: The quality of the oranges sold in the market ______ improved.**

- A. have
- B. has
- C. were
- D. are

**Answer: B.** The subject is *quality* (singular). *Of the oranges sold in the market* is a phrase that does not change the subject.

**2. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: Neither the headmaster nor the teachers ______ aware of the plan.**

- A. was
- B. is
- C. were
- D. has been

**Answer: C.** With *neither…nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, *the teachers*, which is plural.

**3. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: The governor, as well as his commissioners, ______ expected at the ceremony.**

- A. are
- B. were
- C. have been
- D. is

**Answer: D.** *As well as his commissioners* does not join subjects. The verb agrees with *the governor*, which is singular.

**4. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: My uncle gave me some useful ______ before I left for the university.**

- A. advices
- B. advice
- C. pieces of advices
- D. an advice

**Answer: B.** *Advice* is uncountable. It has no plural and cannot take *an*. (*Pieces of advice* would be correct, but not *pieces of advices*.)

**5. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: A number of candidates ______ not written the examination.**

- A. has
- B. have
- C. was
- D. is

**Answer: B.** *A number of* means "several", so it takes a plural verb.

**6. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: The police ______ arrested the suspects.**

- A. has
- B. was
- C. have
- D. is

**Answer: C.** *Police* is a plural-only noun and always takes a plural verb.

**7. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: Each of the girls ______ a sewing machine.**

- A. own
- B. owns
- C. are owning
- D. have owned

**Answer: B.** *Each* is singular, so it takes the singular verb *owns*.

**8. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: The researcher said that one ______ was not met in the study.**

- A. criteria
- B. criterias
- C. criterion
- D. criterions

**Answer: C.** *Criterion* is the singular; *criteria* is the plural. *One* needs the singular form, and *criterias* and *criterions* do not exist in standard English.

**9. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: Mathematics ______ my favourite subject when I was in secondary school.**

- A. are
- B. were
- C. is
- D. was

**Answer: D.** *Mathematics*, as a subject of study, is singular. The time clause *when I was in secondary school* needs the past tense, so *was* is correct. (Option C is singular but in the wrong tense.)

**10. Choose the option that best completes the sentence: The committee has submitted ______ report to the principal.**

- A. its
- B. their
- C. it's
- D. theirs

**Answer: A.** The verb *has* shows that the committee is treated as one unit, so the pronoun must be singular: *its*. (*It's* means "it is" or "it has".)
